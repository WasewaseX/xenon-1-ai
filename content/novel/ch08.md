# Chapter 8 — War of Ledgers

The salt storm had been three days old when the war began. Not with a bang, but with the quiet click of a ledger sealing shut, somewhere in the Choratorium's vaulted halls. Dar felt it as a tremor in the Lily's roots, a vibration that traveled through his veins and made his teeth ache. The city had always been a pressure cooker, but now the lid was rattling.

"They're blaming the Gavel," Suri said, her voice barely above the whisper of wind through the alley's dead-end. She crouched beside him, her carbon-scaled arm pressed flat against the damp brick wall. The scales caught what little light filtered down from the storm-walls above, glittering like fish scales left too long on dry land. "The Chorus leaked word that the Gavel's been harvesting early to weaken their stock."

Dar traced the outline of his ribs beneath his shirt, feeling the steady, alien pulse beneath his flesh. The Lily had settled since the memory bleed, but it left echoes—fragments of salt gardens and humming lullabies that surfaced when he least expected. "And the Gavel?"

"Retaliated. Burned a tithe-warehouse in the Marrowworks. Three harvests lost." Suri's fingers twitched, counting something only she could see. "Ledgermen are scrambling to cover their books. Wardens are cracking skulls to keep the peace."

They moved through the Saltstairs like eels through a net, staying close to the walls where the shadows pooled deepest. The Lily drank the gloom, and with each step into shadow, Dar felt his strength return—not just the strength of his body, but something more, something that made the darkness feel less like absence and more like home. His senses sharpened until he could smell the sour sweat of the family huddled in the cellar below, hear the frantic pulse of a rat in the walls, taste the salt on the air that spoke of the storm still raging above.

"Price of a safe room doubled," Suri muttered, leading him through a narrow archway into the Undergleam. The air here was different—thick with the smell of lamp oil, damp paper, and something vaguely metallic. "And the ledgers are watching."

Dar followed her gaze to a trio of men in long coats standing by a flickering streetlamp. Their collars were turned up, but their eyes tracked every movement. Syndic informants, or worse—ledgermen collecting debts that weren't owed yet. "We need to move faster."

"We are moving faster," Suri said, quickening her pace. "You're the one who keeps stopping to breathe."

Dar hadn't realized he'd been holding his breath. The Lily demanded more now, more darkness, more stillness. It was becoming a hunger that matched his own. "I'm fine."

"Fine is when you don't flinch at your own shadow," Suri retorted, rounding a corner and pulling him into a narrow alley that smelled of urine and despair. A child's face appeared in a second-story window, eyes wide with fear before vanishing. "This city is eating itself, Dar, and we're caught between the teeth."

They emerged onto a rooftop that overlooked the Undergleam like a god surveying a broken kingdom. The city stretched below them in a tangled web of light and shadow, the storm-walls rising like teeth at the horizon. In the distance, the Marrowworks pulsed with bioluminescent signs, their glow bleeding into the salt fog.

"Look at it," Dar said, his voice softer than he intended. "All those lives, all those debts, and it comes down to numbers on a page."

Suri leaned against the parapet, her carbon-scaled arm gleaming in the dim light. "Numbers are the only promises this city keeps," she said. "Everything else is just words on salt."

Dar watched her profile against the backdrop of the dying city. The scales on her arm weren't just decoration—they were a reminder, a payment she'd made in blood and skin. He'd asked once, years ago, when he'd first stitched the wound that ran from her elbow to her wrist. She'd told him it was none of his business, and he'd respected that boundary. But now, with the Lily whispering in his chest, boundaries felt thinner than paper.

"You never talk about it," he said carefully. "The arm."

Suri's fingers tightened on the parapet. "I was nineteen. Debt for my mother's surgery. They took the skin, gave me this." She tapped the scales with her free hand. "Kept me for six weeks. Ransomed me back when my brother found the coin." She finally turned to face him, her eyes dark in the gloom. "Numbers, Dar. That's all it was. Six weeks. Two hundred milliliters. A carbon graft."

"And the debt?"

"Paid," she said, but her voice was flat. "In full."

Dar knew better. No debt in Ashkarad was ever truly paid. It just changed form. "The rumors," he said, keeping his voice low. "About you being Chorus property."

Suri's laugh was sharp as broken glass. "Let them talk. It keeps the ledgermen at bay."

They stood in silence for a moment, the only sounds the distant wail of a siren and the whisper of the Lily in Dar's chest. The storm above had finally begun to break, the salt clouds thinning to reveal stars like scattered diamonds.

"We should move," Suri said, breaking the silence. "Before the Wardens start their sweep."

As they made their way back to the alley, Dar felt a shift in the air—a presence that had nothing to do with the storm. Three men emerged from the shadows, their faces hidden beneath hoods, their movements too precise to be random. Syndic Wardens, or worse.

"Courier Kaveh," one of them said, his voice devoid of warmth. "You're coming with us."

Suri didn't flinch. "On whose authority?"

"The Gavel's," another said, stepping forward. "You're accused of trafficking in restricted organs."

"That's a lie," Dar said, stepping between them and Suri. He could feel the Lily stirring, the darkness deepening around them. "She's with me."

The third man laughed. "And you are?"

"Nobody important," Dar said, his voice steady.

The lead Warden drew a pulse-reader from his coat, its brass gears clicking in the silence. "We'll be the judge of that."

The Lily pulsed in Dar's chest, a warning. He could feel the shadows thickening, the air growing still. But the Wardens had their pulse-readers trained on them, their fingers on the triggers. There was no room for the Lily's power here, not without revealing everything.

Suri placed a hand on his arm. "It's alright, Dar."

"No, it's not," he said, meeting her gaze. "They're taking you for something you didn't do."

She smiled, a rare, genuine expression that reached her eyes. "This is how it goes, Dar. Ledgers have to balance."

The Warden grabbed her arm, his grip tight on the carbon scales. "Move."

Dar watched them take her away, the pulse-reader clicking like a metronome counting down to disaster. The Lily's hunger was a physical pain now, a demand for action, for blood. But he held it back, knowing that to give in now would be to lose himself, to become something other than the man who'd promised to protect her.

· · ·

The city had descended into chaos by nightfall. The salt storm had passed, leaving behind a sky bruised with the promise of a new moon. Dar moved through the streets like a ghost, avoiding the Wardens' checkpoints, the Lily guiding him through the darkest paths. He needed to find Suri, to understand why the Gavel would take her, and what they wanted with her.

He found his answer waiting in his rented room—a small, windowless space that smelled of dust and desperation. On the floor, beside a flickering lamp, lay a small wooden box. Tied to it was a note, written in the precise, angular script of the ledgers.

The price of the courier is the vessel's heart—beating, in a box, by the new moon.

Dar's fingers trembled as he untied the note. The Lily pulsed in his chest, a cold dread spreading through his veins. They weren't after Suri. They were after the Lily. And they knew exactly what it was.

The new moon rose, casting the city in shadows deeper than any Dar had ever known. He looked down at the box in his hands, its surface smooth and unmarked. Inside, he knew, was a heartbeat waiting to be silenced. The Lily's heartbeat. And the price was Suri's life.
