# Chapter 12 — Chorus of Flesh

The salt wind carried the scent of blood and hymns as Dar pressed his back against the cold stone of the Choratorium's undercroft. Above them, the grading night was underway—the annual assessment when the Church's nursery vessels were measured, weighed, and cataloged before the "reserve stock" moved to the graft-theaters. Vahid traced the symbols carved into the wall, his fingers trembling with a mixture of fear and anticipation. "The entrance is behind this false section of the nursery," he whispered, his breath fogging in the damp air. "The children are taught hymns to make their tissue compliant—keeps the graft rejection low." Suri checked her carbon-scaled arm, the plates clicking softly as she flexed her fingers. "Counted twelve heartbeats above," she murmured. "Plus the Choirmaster. Thirteen targets. Eleven vessels to extract. Triage ethics under fire." Dar closed his eyes, focusing on the Lily's pulse in his chest—a steady, rhythmic drumbeat that had become his anchor since the Blood Vein opened. The Lily had grown more vocal these past weeks, not in words, but in cravings and dreams. It wanted to bloom, and tonight, it wanted to hear the singing. "Let's move," Dar said, his voice tight. "The sooner we're in, the sooner we're out."

The hidden door swung inward with a groan of ancient hinges, revealing a narrow corridor lined with humming jars. Inside each glass container, a preserved organ pulsed with a faint, rhythmic light—a heart, a lung, a kidney—each kept alive by the Chorus's dark science. The air was thick with the scent of formaldehyde and something else, something sweet and cloying like overripe fruit. "The reliquary," Vahid breathed, his eyes wide with recognition. "They keep the harvested organs singing, like a choir." Dar's fingers brushed against one jar, the glass cold beneath his touch. Inside, a heart beat in a slow, mournful rhythm. The Lily in his chest stirred, a sudden, sharp pang of hunger that made him stumble. "They're alive," Dar whispered, realization dawning. "Not just preserved—they're alive." Suri grabbed his arm, her grip firm. "Focus, Dar. We came for the children, not the dead." They moved deeper into the corridor, passing row after row of humming jars, each one containing a piece of someone's past, someone's life, reduced to a song in a glass prison. The walls were lined with crude musical notation etched into the stone—hymns to compliance, to sacrifice, to the beauty of being made whole by being made apart. "This is the nursery," Vahid said, stopping before a heavy iron door. "The children are taught these hymns every day. Makes their tissue more receptive to the grafts." The door creaked open, revealing a chamber lined with small cots. On each cot lay a child, no older than ten, their eyes closed as they sang in unison—a chorus of small, clear voices that echoed through the chamber. "Perfect vessels," Vahid murmured, his voice thick with emotion. "They've been raised for this." Dar's heart ached—both his own and the Lily's. The symbiote in his chest pulsed with a strange, predatory hunger, drawn to the purity of the children's song. "We need to move fast," Dar said, his voice low. "Before the Choirmaster comes." Suri nodded, her eyes scanning the room. "Counted eleven children. Ilay's among them." Vahid pointed to a small girl with dark hair sitting near the back, her hands folded in her lap. "There. That's her. The registry marked her as 'perfect, reserved for the throne.'" Dar approached the children, his steps silent on the stone floor. "Wake them," he whispered to Suri. "Quietly. We need to go now." Suri moved from cot to cot, gently shaking the children awake. One by one, they opened their eyes, confusion dawning as they saw the strangers in their midst. "Don't be afraid," Dar said, his voice soft. "We're here to help you." The children stared at him, their eyes wide with fear. "The hymns," one boy whispered, his voice trembling. "We must sing the hymns." "No," Dar said firmly. "You don't have to sing anymore. Come with us." He reached for Ilay's hand, and she flinched, pulling back. "The singing," she whispered, pointing to the ceiling. "It's coming from the walls." Dar frowned, following her gaze. The walls of the nursery seemed to hum with a low, resonant frequency, as if the very stone was alive and singing. The Lily in his chest pulsed in response, a wave of longing washing over him. "It's the First Singer," Vahid said, his voice strained. "They've buried her beneath the Marrowworks, and her song resonates through the city." Ilay nodded, her eyes wide with understanding. "I hear it too. The walls sing when the lights are out." Dar helped Ilay to her feet, her small hand trembling in his. "We need to go now," he said, glancing toward the door. "Before they notice we're missing." The children followed him, their small feet silent on the stone floor as they moved through the corridor toward the hidden entrance. Behind them, the nursery door swung shut with a final, echoing thud.

The harvest amphitheater was a nightmare of flesh and glass. Dar, Suri, and the eleven children moved through a narrow corridor that opened into a vast chamber, its walls lined with humming jars of preserved organs. In the center of the room stood a raised platform, surrounded by bone-plate enforcers in white gloves and bone masks. On the platform, a child lay strapped to a table, surrounded by gleaming surgical tools. The Choirmaster stood over them, their voice a smooth, melodic baritone that seemed to fill the room. "Today we welcome little Elias to the choir," the Choirmaster announced, raising a scalpel. "His lungs are perfect—clear and strong. They will sing for decades to come." The crowd of Chorus members in the amphitheater applauded, their faces hidden behind white masks. Dar's stomach turned. The Lily in his chest pulsed with a mixture of hunger and revulsion, as if it too could feel the violation of the room. "Now," Dar whispered to Suri and Vahid, who had joined them at the back of the amphitheater. "We move on my signal." Vahid nodded, his eyes fixed on the platform. "I need to find Shirin's lobe. It's here somewhere." Suri tightened her grip on her carbon-scaled arm, the plates clicking softly. "Counted six enforcers on the platform. Two at the exits. Eight more in the crowd. Twenty-two targets. Eleven children to extract. Triage ethics under fire." Dar took a deep breath, the air thick with the scent of antiseptic and something else—something metallic and coppery, like old blood. "Now," he said, his voice sharp. Suri moved like a shadow, her carbon-scaled arm flashing as she disarmed the enforcers at the exits with precise, economical movements. Dar charged the platform, his hands moving with the speed of a seasoned surgeon as he grabbed the surgical tools and turned them on the enforcers. The bone-plate masks shattered under the force of his blows, revealing the pale, terrified faces beneath. The Choirmaster whirled around, their scalpel raised. "Intruders!" they shrieked. "Sound the alarm!" The amphitheater erupted into chaos as Chorus members scrambled to defend their harvest. Dar moved through the crowd, his hands a blur of motion as he disarmed and disabled the attackers. The Lily in his chest pulsed with each blow, its hunger growing with every scream that echoed through the chamber. Vahid darted toward the wall of humming jars, his fingers flying over the labels. "There," he breathed, pulling a jar from the shelf. Inside, a lobe of brain tissue pulsed with a faint, rhythmic light. It was Shirin's—her voice, preserved in glass, singing a soft, mournful melody. "Shirin," Vahid whispered, his voice choked with emotion. "I'm so sorry." With a surge of strength, he smashed the jar against the floor, the glass shattering into a thousand pieces. The lobe pulsed once more, then went dark. Vahid stared at the remains, his face pale with grief. "I couldn't save her," he murmured. "But I could free the others." Dar grabbed the child from the table and handed them to Suri, who was already ushering the children toward the exit. "Move!" Dar yelled, grabbing Vahid's arm and pulling him toward the door. The Choirmaster lunged at them, their scalpel flashing in the dim light. Dar sidestepped, his hand closing around the Choirmaster's wrist. With a sharp twist, he dislocated the joint, the bone snapping with a sickening crack. The Choirmaster screamed, stumbling backward. "You've robbed a dispensary, cutter," they gasped, their voice twisted with pain and fury. "The Custodian is building a BODY. Ask yourself what the city is for." Dar stared at them, the words echoing in his mind. The Custodian was building a body? What did that mean? Before he could ask, Suri grabbed his arm. "Dar, we have to go! Now!" He nodded, following her as she led the children and Vahid out of the amphitheater and into the narrow corridor beyond. Behind them, the alarm bells began to ring, their sharp, insistent cry echoing through the Choratorium. They ran, the children's small feet pounding on the stone floor, Suri's carbon scales clicking with each step. Dar could feel the Lily's pulse racing in his chest, a mixture of triumph and unease. They had saved the children, but what had they really accomplished? The Choirmaster's words echoed in his mind: "The Custodian is building a BODY." What did that mean? And what was the city for? They burst out of the hidden entrance and into the salt-laced night air, the Choratorium looming behind them like a monstrous tomb. As they caught their breath, a figure emerged from the shadows—a woman in white gloves, her face hidden behind a bone mask. She stood at the highest window of the Choratorium, watching them go. Slowly, deliberately, she began to applaud, her gloved hands moving with a quiet, chilling rhythm. Dar stared up at her, a cold dread washing over him. They had escaped, but they were far from safe. The Custodian knew who they were now. And she was not pleased.
