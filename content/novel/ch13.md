# Chapter 13 — Nerve

The silence in the Saltstairs hideout was a physical thing, a thick wool blanket pressed over the mouth. Ilay sat in the corner, tracing a finger along the floor's cracked salt-glaze, her small face turned away from the group. Suri counted the seconds between her own breaths, her carbon-scaled arm wrapped around her knees. Vahid polished his spectacles with a stained handkerchief, though they hadn't been dirty.

Then it started.

A high, thin note from the Choratorium, miles away but inside my skull. A low thrum from a harvest-floor beneath the Undergleam. A chorus of screams from the ledgers, compressed into a single, vibrating chord. They layered over each other, a cathedral of pain laid across Ashkarad like a second map, invisible but inescapable. My hands clenched on the dusty floorboards, my nails splintering.

"Dar?" Suri's voice was a knife through the noise. "You're white as a ledgerman's sheet."

I couldn't answer. The noise wasn't sound; it was pressure, a physical force pushing against my bones. Each choir organ was a different instrument, a different agony played in a different key. The Choratorium's was a pipe organ, solemn and cruel. The ledgers were a shrieking violin section. Somewhere, a debtor's heart was failing, a slow, mournful cello that vibrated in my teeth. I gagged, bile rising in my throat.

Vahid was beside me, his hands on my temples. "Focus, boy," he said, his voice a calm island in the storm. "Don't listen to the song. Listen to the silence between the notes. Build a door."

A door. In the noise. The idea was absurd, impossible. The cathedral filled every inch of my awareness.

"Close your eyes," Vahid instructed. "Imagine a wall. Thick stone. No windows. No doors."

I tried. I pictured the salt walls of the hideout, but they dissolved into the screaming of the ledgers. I pictured Saint Ferrous Hospital, but it became the rhythmic thump of a harvest-floor piston.

"No," Vahid said, his grip tightening. "Not a place. A concept. A barrier. Think of it as a surgeon's field. Small. Contained."

I focused on the feeling of my own skin. The texture of my shirt against my collarbone. The warmth of my own breath on my lips. I held onto that tiny sensation, a single thread in the hurricane. Slowly, painstakingly, I built a wall around it, a wall of pure will. The screaming of the city didn't fade, but it... receded. It was still there, pressing against my mental barrier, but it was no longer inside.

"Good," Vahid said, his voice strained. "Now, open a crack. Just enough to hear one street."

I did. I eased a corner of the wall, and a flood of information rushed in. Not pain, not yet. Just the mundane symphony of the Saltstairs: the cough of a dying woman in the apartment above, the frantic pulse of a rat in the wall, the slow, steady beat of a child sleeping in the next room. I focused on the child, the pure, untroubled rhythm. I made that my door.

The hideout rushed back into focus. Suri was staring at me, her eyes wide. Ilay had stopped tracing the floor and was watching me with unnerving stillness. Vahid let out a breath he'd been holding.

"The Nerve Vein," he said, wiping his brow. "It opens the city to you. Every living thing, singing its own song. Most drown in it. You... you built a raft."

I was sweating, my heart hammering against my ribs. The Lily inside me pulsed in time with it, a cool, steady counterpoint to my own panic. It wasn't afraid. It was... curious.

We spent the next days in a state of controlled chaos. With my doors cracked, I could triangulate the hidden harvest-floors the Chorus used when the official tithe-barrys were too slow. Suri would case the location, her carbon arm a silent weapon, and Vahid would find a way to cut the power or jam the doors. I would stand by, my senses narrowed to a single street, listening for the tell-tale thrum of the harvesting machinery.

The first liberation was brutal. A converted warehouse in the Marrowworks, where three debtors were strung up like sides of beef. Their screams were a physical assault even through my doors. Suri moved like smoke, disabling the guards with swift, economical strikes. Vahid worked at the restraints with a bone-saw he'd carried for decades.

We didn't free them into the city. We couldn't. The Wardens would scoop them up for new debts. Instead, we guided them to a pre-arranged drop with a syndicate that owed Suri a favor. The price of their freedom was a new debt, a smaller one, to the Gavel. It was a choice between the Chorus's knife and the syndicate's ledger. They took the ledger.

We did it again. And again. Each time, the city's song shifted. The screams of the harvest-floors were replaced by the frantic pulse of gang wars, the territorial drumming of syndicate enforcers. We were tipping the underworld, and the city was singing about it.

But the cost was intimacy. Suri sat beside me one evening, cleaning her carbon scales with a rag. I flinched.

"What?" she asked, not looking up.

"Your pulse," I said. "It's like a hammer on a tin roof."

She stopped, her hand hovering over her arm. "It's always been like that."

"Not to me," I said. "Now I can't unhear it. I can't unfeel it. The man in the apartment above us has a heart murmur that sounds like a teakettle. The rat in the wall has a fever. Everything is a noise, and everything is a feeling."

Suri was quiet for a long time. "Count it," she finally said. "Count the beats. Make it a number instead of a sound."

I tried. It helped, a little. But the numbers had their own rhythm, a cold, mathematical pulse that was just as invasive as the biological one.

Ilay was different. She never spoke, but she drew. On scraps of paper, with charcoal she'd found, she made maps. Not of streets, but of what she called "the singing." She would draw a cluster of sharp, angry lines and say, "The Choratorium." A series of slow, heavy circles, "The ledgers." And between them, a space that was empty, a silence in the noise.

"The city's song has a shape," I told Vahid one night, showing him Ilay's drawing. "And the shape has a hole."

He frowned, tracing the empty space with a grimy finger. "The hole is where the First Singer should be," he said, his voice low. "The Chorus's secret. The thing they're building beneath the Marrowworks."

The next day, we went deeper. Using Ilay's crude map as a guide, we tunneled through service corridors beneath the industrial belt, following the thrum of machinery that fed the city's vital-debt system. We were looking for the central harvest-floor, the one that powered the entire operation. The one that must be connected to the First Singer.

The air grew thick with the smell of ozone and something else. Something organic. Old. We followed the sound of it, a deep, resonant thrum that vibrated in my bones, a bass note to the city's screaming treble.

We found a grate in the floor of an abandoned maintenance tunnel. Below it, darkness. But the sound was louder here, a physical presence in the air.

"Can you hear it?" Vahid asked, his face pale in the dim light of our lamp.

I nodded, closing my eyes. I opened my doors wider, letting the cathedral of pain flood in, but focused on the sound below. It wasn't an organ. It wasn't a choir. It was something else. Something vast. Slow. Older than the Church.

"It's not an organ," I whispered. "It's... alive."

We pried the grate open. Below was a shaft, dropping into absolute blackness. The thrumming was coming from down there, a sound that felt more like geological time than biology.

"I'm going down," I said, before either of them could protest.

The ladder was rusted, rungs slick with condensation. I descended into the darkness, the Lily in my chest growing warmer with each step. The thrumming grew louder, filling my entire world. It wasn't a sound I heard with my ears; it was a vibration I felt in my teeth, in my bones, in the very marrow of my spine.

I reached the bottom. The space was vast, bigger than the Choratorium. The air was cold and still, smelling of wet earth and something ancient and sweet. In the center of the chamber, pulsing with a slow, rhythmic light, was something that defied description. It was a mass of tissue, vast and slow, like a buried heart or a dreaming brain. It was the source of the thrumming, the furnace the Chorus fed.

And it was awake.

The thrumming stopped.

For a single, perfect second, there was only silence. The silence of the deep earth, the silence before a star is born. Then, the thing pulsed again. But differently. It was no longer a steady, furnace-like beat. It was a question. A slow, deliberate thump that seemed to echo in the chamber's stone.

Thump.

My own heart answered.

Thump.

It was a rhythm I knew. A lullaby. The one my mother used to sing.

Thump.

The thing pulsed again, matching my heartbeat exactly. It was amused. Ancient. And it was listening.
