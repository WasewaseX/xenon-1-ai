# Chapter 10 — Vein of Breath

The water hit Dar's face like a slap, cold and sudden, as Professor Vahid Anousheh kicked the bucket over. It sloshed across the stone floor of the abandoned cistern, pooling around Dar's bare feet. "Breathe deep, boy," Vahid slurred, wiping his own beard with a stained handkerchief. "Fill those lungs. You'll need them."

Dar took a breath, the air tasting of rust and something else—decay from the depths. He'd been standing blindfolded for what felt like hours, listening to Vahid's shuffling footsteps and the drip-drip-drip of water from the ceiling. The blindfold was rough burlap, smelling of mildew and old rope.

"Right," Vahid said, his voice echoing slightly. "Now, I want you to hold that breath. And don't let go."

Dar exhaled slowly. "How long?"

"As long as you can. Until you don't need to anymore."

The instruction was madness. A man could drown in minutes. But this was the price of the Lily's gifts. Each Vein-opening was a metamorphosis with real costs, and the Breath Vein was next on the ladder. His body still remembered the bloom—the black vascular lace that had covered his skin in the fighting pit, the terrifying speed, the numbness that had followed when he'd forced it to stop. He couldn't let that happen again. Not to Suri.

Dar closed his eyes, the blindfold redundant now. He drew in another breath, filling his chest until it ached. He held it. The seconds stretched. One. Two. Thirty. His lungs began to burn, a familiar pressure building behind his ribs. He focused on the sensation, on the burning, willing it away.

"Breathe," Vahid urged. "Or don't. The choice is yours."

Forty-five seconds. The burn intensified. His heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic counterpoint to his stillness. This was the way. The Lily had shown him in the dreams—a darkness so complete it was a presence, a pressure that pushed back against his own need. He had to find that pressure and hold it.

Sixty seconds. The burn was a fire now, consuming his chest. He could feel the Lily in his chest, a silent, patient thing. It wasn't helping. It wasn't hurting. It was just there, an alien observer. Dar focused inward, past the pain, past the frantic pulse of his own blood, searching for the symbiote's core.

Seventy-five seconds. His vision began to tunnel, the edges darkening. The water lapped at his ankles, cold and indifferent. He was going to drown. He could feel it, the primal panic rising, screaming at him to breathe. He fought it, teeth clenched, jaw tight.

And then he found it. Not a presence, but an absence. A void in his own awareness where the Lily's influence should have been. It was a place of perfect stillness, of no-needs. Dar focused on that point, letting the void expand within him, swallowing the panic, swallowing the burning.

At ninety seconds, the pain in his lungs vanished.

It wasn't a relief. It was an erasure. The need for air simply stopped existing. He was still underwater, still holding his breath, but the biological imperative was gone. The water was just water. The cistern was just a room.

"How?" Vahid's voice was strained, awed. "How are you doing that?"

Dar didn't answer. He couldn't. He was experimenting, pushing the boundaries. He focused on the void, on the absolute stillness it represented. He could feel his own blood moving beneath his skin, a dark river, but he was no longer bound to its rhythm. He was separate from it. He was separate from the body that held him.

One hundred twenty seconds. The burning in his lungs returned, but it was different now. It was a memory of pain, not the thing itself. He could feel the water on his skin, the chill seeping into his bones, but it was distant, like a sensation felt by someone else. He was the observer, and the body was just the vessel.

One hundred fifty seconds. The void was expanding. It was pushing out, past his skin, into the water around him. He could feel the cistern walls, the cold stone, the dripping water, but it was all filtered through this new sense of self. He didn't need air because he was no longer entirely in his body. He was in the space between things.

At two minutes, Dar let the breath go.

A stream of bubbles rose from his lips, silent in the water. He didn't gasp, didn't choke. He simply released the air and stood there, his lungs empty, his body functioning perfectly. The void was still there, a silent hum in his awareness. He was breathing water.

Vahid was staring, his mouth agape. "By the First Vessel," he whispered. "That's... that's not just Breath. That's something else."

Dar pulled off the blindfold, his vision clear. "What is it?"

"I don't know," Vahid said, his voice trembling. "The texts say the Breath Vein makes the air optional. They don't say anything about... this. About becoming the water."

The Saltstairs cisterns were a labyrinth of damp stone and echoing chambers, a city beneath the city. Suri's voice came through the tiny earpiece, crisp and precise despite the acoustics. "Two o'clock, fifty paces. Reliquary archive. Water's waist-deep. No heat signatures. Motion sensors are offline. They've been offline for three days. Maintenance log says 'flood damage'. Convenient."

Dar waded through the chill water, his movements silent. The void was still with him, a constant companion. He didn't need to breathe, but he moved as if he did, conserving energy, his senses extended through the water. He could feel the cold, but it didn't bother him. He could feel the drag of his clothes, but it was just data.

The reliquary archive was a circular chamber, its domed ceiling lost in darkness. Shelves carved into the stone walls held glass reliquaries, most empty, some containing small preserved organs. The water here was clearer, still and cold. Dar ran his fingers over the surface of one reliquary. It contained a heart, shriveled and brown, floating in clear fluid. A name was etched into the glass: Kaelen. BLOOMED THROUGH.

He moved to the next. Another heart. Elara. FLED.

The pattern was grim. A ledger of failed experiments. Dar moved deeper into the chamber, his bare feet silent on the stone floor. Suri's voice crackled in his ear. "You're in the main archive now. According to the blueprints, the vessel registry should be against the far wall. Behind the second row of empty reliquaries."

Dar found it. A stone slab set into the wall, covered in rows of names and dates. He ran his fingers over the etched words. Most were closed with the same two phrases: BLOOMED THROUGH, FLED. He was looking for a strain number, Vahid had said. Something that would tell them about the Lily's origins.

He moved his finger down the list, past dozens of names, all closed. Then he found it. Not a closed entry, but an active one.

VESSEL: ILAY
AGE: 9
GRADE: PERFECT
RESERVED: THRONE

Dar's blood ran cold, even though he didn't need it to. Ilay. The child from the nursery. The perfect vessel. He traced the letters with his fingertip, the stone rough beneath his touch. And then he saw it, a column he'd never seen on any other page.

SIBLING STOCK: ACTIVE

Dar's breath caught. The Lily wasn't alone. It had siblings. Active siblings. He needed to copy this page. He pulled a piece of charcoal from his pocket and a thin sheet of treated vellum he'd prepared earlier. He began to trace the letters, working quickly in the darkness, the water lapping at his waist.

"Copy's done," he whispered into the earpiece. "Getting out."

As he turned to leave, a bell began to toll. Not the gentle, rhythmic bell of the Choratorium marking the hours. This was a single, sharp, insistent note that echoed through the cisterns, bouncing off the stone walls and vibrating through the water. It was a bell that tolled out of hours.

Dar froze. Suri's voice came back, tight with alarm. "Dar? What was that?"

"I don't know," he whispered, his eyes fixed on the entrance to the reliquary chamber. "But I think we just woke something up."
