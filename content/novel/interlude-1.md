# Interlude I — The Tithe-Clerk's Day

Milla Ferun's fingers were always cold. They had to be. Cold fingers didn't tremble when the pulse-reader whined, didn't sweat when the debtor's pulse thinned under the clamp. Today they were colder than usual, the air from the Marrowworks vents carrying salt and the faint, sweet scent of preserved tissue. She stamped another form—T-7B, lung lobe, standard tithe—and slid it into the growing pile. The quota for the day was thirty-three hearts. She'd processed twenty-seven. Six more to go.

"Morning, Milla," said Elara, dropping a stack of fresh forms on her desk. The ink still smelled wet. "Saltstairs shipment coming in at noon. Better clear space."

Milla nodded, arranging her stamps in their designated slots. The Heart-Stamp, the Debt-Stamp, the Voluntary-Stamp. Each one made a different sound when it hit the paper. The Heart-Stamp was the heaviest, a solid thud that felt final. She'd started counting the sounds at the beginning of the month. Twelve thousand, seven hundred and forty-three. That was how many hearts she'd stamped in her five years as a tithe-clerk. She knew the number as well as she knew the sound of her own pulse.

The clinic was a long, white room with rows of examining tables where people lay still as sacrifices. Beyond the glass wall, the nurses moved with practiced efficiency, their gloved hands disappearing into body cavities and emerging with organs nestled in coolers. Milla never looked directly at the tables. She looked at the forms. Forms were clean. Forms had names and debt ledgers and next of kin. Forms were people before they became inventory.

"Name's Kaelen," said the man sliding into the chair opposite her desk. He was young, maybe nineteen, with the hollow cheeks of someone who'd already sold something valuable. "Here for the heart tithe."

Milla took his census chit, the small brass disc that held all his information. She slotted it into the reader. His face appeared on the small screen, then his debt ledger. Eighteen thousand, four hundred and ninety-seven marks. For his sister's lung operation. The system was designed to be inescapable. A debt paid with blood was a debt that grew.

"Standard procedure," Milla said, her voice flat. She slid a consent form across the table. "Read section seven. The one about the grace period."

Kaelen's eyes were wide. "They said... they said I wouldn't feel it. The withdrawal."

Milla's fingers paused over the Voluntary-Stamp. "The heart doesn't hurt when it's gone," she said, which was true. "What hurts is the living without it." She stamped the form. "Table seven. Nurse Jory will prep you."

As he walked away, Milla made a small note in her ledger: *Kaelen, L. - Heart Tithed - Grace Period: 72 hours*. She knew what would happen. In seventy-two hours, the grace period would expire, and his debt would be recalculated based on his new, diminished capacity to work. He'd come back. People always came back. The Chorus was patient. They had to be. A garden couldn't be rushed.

The afternoon bled into evening. The last shipment from the Saltstairs arrived—a dozen debtors chained together at the wrists, their faces gray with resignation. Milla processed them quickly, her stamps clicking like a metronome. She didn't learn their names. Names were dangerous. They made you imagine them as people who had lives outside this room.

When the last form was stamped and filed, Milla stretched her cold fingers. Her supervisor, a man named Borin whose eyes were as flat as the clinic's polished floors, approached her.

"Milla," he said, not looking at her. "A pulse-printout came in from the Saltstairs. Flagged RHYTHM UNRESOLVED. File it with the anomalies. And lose it."

Milla's stomach tightened. Anomalies were bad. They were things the Chorus didn't like, things that didn't fit the neat patterns of their ledgers. "What's wrong with it?"

"None of your concern," Borin said. "Just make it disappear. Permanently."

He handed her a thin strip of paper covered in jagged, irregular lines. Most pulse-readouts were smooth, predictable waves. This one was a mess of spikes and dips, like a heart trying to find a rhythm and failing. Milla took it, her fingers brushing Borin's. His skin was cold, too. Everything in the Marrowworks was cold.

After Borin left, Milla sat at her desk for a long time, staring at the printout. She could just file it, as instructed. Or she could do what she sometimes did when the weight of the ledgers became too much—she could make a copy. She slipped a blank sheet of paper into the clinic's old thermal copier, fed the printout through, and watched as the jagged lines appeared in gray on the new sheet. Then she took the original and fed it into the disposal chute, where it would be incinerated along with the biological waste.

She folded the copy and slid it into her pocket. It was a small thing, a tiny act of defiance that would be forgotten by morning. But it was hers.

On her way out, Milla passed the nursery wing. The doors were always closed, but she sometimes heard singing, faint and beautiful, from behind them. She never stopped to listen. It was better not to know what they were growing in there.

The walk to her apartment in the Saltstairs was a descent into fog. The salt storm had begun, a thick, white mist that swallowed the upper levels and left the streets below in a perpetual twilight. Milla pulled her coat tight, the cold seeping through the fabric. The storm was called a "grace" by those who didn't have to breathe it. For the poor, it was just another way the city tried to kill you slowly.

Her apartment was small and damp, with a single window that looked out onto a brick wall. She lit a lamp, its flame casting long shadows that danced on the ceiling. She took the pulse-printout from her pocket and spread it on the table. The rhythm was strange. If she read it not as a single pulse, but as a sequence, the pattern began to emerge. Five spikes, then a dip, then five more spikes. It was a shape. A five-petal flower, drawn in jagged lines.

Milla traced the pattern with her finger. It was the same flower she sometimes saw in the margins of her ledgers, a small thing she drew when she was tired of counting hearts. She'd never known why she drew it. Now, looking at the pulse-printout, it seemed less like a coincidence and more like a message.

That night, Milla dreamed. She was in a garden made of salt, the ground crunching beneath her feet. The air was thick with the scent of something sweet and metallic. In the distance, rows of people lay sleeping on stone tables, their chests rising and falling in perfect unison. Above them, a figure in a white robe hummed a low, tuneless song. The figure turned, and Milla saw that its face was smooth and blank, like a porcelain doll. It smiled, and the smile was too wide, stretching across its face in a way that wasn't possible.

Milla tried to scream, but no sound came out. She tried to run, but her feet were rooted to the salt ground. The figure hummed louder, and the sleeping figures began to stir. Their eyes opened, all at once, and they all looked at her.

Milla woke with a start, her heart pounding against her ribs. She sat up in bed, gasping for air. It was dark, but she could see her hand clearly. On her fingertips was a fine dust, white and chalky. Salt. She rubbed her fingers together, but the dust wouldn't come off. It was under her nails, in the creases of her skin. It was in her dreams.
