# Chapter 3 — Grace

The salt storm hit like a fist to the throat, turning Ashkarad into a whiteout tomb. Dar stumbled through the Saltstairs, his collar pulled high against the grit that stung like needles. The wind howled through the alleyways, carrying the scent of brine and decay. He counted the turns to Suri's bolt-hole—three left, two down, one through the collapsed archway. The cellar was a known shelter during grace, a place where the poor huddled like mice while the rich rode their glass funiculars above the fog. The door groaned as he pushed it open, revealing a space already crowded with bodies pressed close for warmth. A single oil lamp burned on a crate, casting long shadows that danced like nervous spirits.

"Got room for one more?" Dar called, his voice swallowed by the storm's roar.

A woman with a child clinging to her back nodded toward a corner near the furnace grate. Dar squeezed through, brushing against damp wool and the smell of unwashed bodies. Suri was already there, her carbon-scaled arm wrapped around a small bundle of blankets. She looked up, her eyes sharp even in the dim light.

"Thought you were dead," she said, her voice low.

"Death's a paperwork problem," Dar replied, settling beside her. "They haven't filed it yet."

The lamp sputtered, then died, plunging them into absolute darkness. The collective gasp was followed by a shuffling as people adjusted to the black. Dar felt it immediately—a warmth spreading through his chest, a hum that vibrated in his teeth. The Lily was in heaven. Total dark. No harsh light to weaken it. It drank the absence of photons, and Dar's senses bloomed in response. He could hear the heartbeats around him like cups filling—slow, fast, irregular, each a tiny drum in the symphony of survival. The cellar's breath became a tangible thing, moving across his skin in waves of heat and cold.

"Your heart's racing," Suri whispered beside him, her breath warm on his cheek. "Too much excitement for a dead man?"

"Just adjusting to the dark," Dar said, though he knew better. The Lily was adjusting too, and its adjustment had needs. Dark wasn't enough. It wanted blood given in the dark. Dar felt a pull, a leaning toward the warmth beside him—Suri. Her pulse was a steady thrum against his ribs, a beacon in the black. The Lily wanted what she had to give.

Dar's hand trembled as he reached into his coat, fingers closing around his bone-saw. The tool was cool and familiar, its weight a comfort in the chaos. He could take what he needed without her knowing. A quick nick in her sleep, a few drops to satisfy the beast in his chest. The thought made his stomach twist. This was what the city had taught him—everything had a price, and someone always paid. But Suri had bargained for his life, had chosen him over her employers. She wasn't a ledger entry. She was a person who counted steps and lies and debts, who had been kind when kindness was a currency she couldn't afford.

Dar made his choice. He drew the saw across his own palm instead. The pain was sharp and clean, a familiar sting. He let his hand fall open, letting the blood well in the darkness. The Lily surged toward it, a hungry warmth that spread through his veins like liquid fire. It drank, and in the black, Dar felt something horrifyingly like a first kiss with something that had no mouth. The blood was light-starved, perfect, given in the dark just as the Lily craved. As it drank, Dar's bones began to knit denser, his marrow turning over like soil being turned. A deep ache bloomed in his teeth, his jaw feeling heavy, as if his skeleton was being remade from within. Strength, enormous and quiet, settled into his frame. The Marrow Vein had opened.

The storm raged on, but Dar barely heard it. His senses had expanded, sharpened to a painful clarity. He could hear the child's breathing, the scrape of a rat against the far wall, the slow drip of water from the ceiling. He could feel the cellar's breath, the way the cold air sank and the warm air rose. He could feel the Lily inside him, not just as a presence but as a part of him, connected to his nerves, his bones, his blood. It was a transformation that came with a cost—his body was becoming something else, something that could endure what should kill it.

When dawn finally came, the storm lifted as suddenly as it had begun. The first light of the salt-moon filtered through the cellar's grate, casting long beams that illuminated dust motes dancing in the air. Dar blinked, his eyes adjusting to the light. He looked at the wall where frost had breathed during the night, and his blood ran cold. The condensation had bloomed into the shape of petals—delicate, black, reaching toward where Suri had slept. The Lily had been one bad night from choosing her.

Dar scrubbed the image away with his sleeve before she could wake, the water leaving dark streaks on the stone. He understood now. The Lily wasn't just a passenger. It was a gardener, and it was planting seeds in the dark. He had to find a way to control it before it bloomed through him—and before it chose someone else to tend.
