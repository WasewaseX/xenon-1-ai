# Chapter 15 — Truce of Salt

The cistern smelled of wet stone and old salt, the air thick enough to chew. Dar stood with his back to the carved mermaid that once spewed water into a basin now filled with rainwater and cigarette butts. Around him, the city's worst had gathered: Mother Gavel's enforcers in their stained leather aprons, the Ledgermen with their brass knuckles counting coins, even a representative from the Gavel's rival syndicate, the Hammers, who kept his distance near the archway. Suri stood beside Dar, her carbon-scaled arm crossed over her chest, counting the exits, the heartbeats, the lies. The Cartographer stood opposite them, masked in brass, her hands folded as if she were about to draw a map of the room itself instead of speaking.

"The terms are simple," Dar said, his voice echoing in the damp space. "No tithe-taking from the Saltstairs. No debtors sent to the ledgers without trial. And no harvesting."

Mother Gavel scoffed, a sound like stones grinding together. "And what gives you the right to make terms, little surgeon? You're just a man with a fancy new heart."

"I'm the reason your pit is still standing," Dar said. "I'm the reason your enforcers walk the streets instead of rotting in the ledgers. I'm the reason you're sitting here instead of being hauled before the Syndic Wardens for what happened in the Marrowworks."

The Hammers representative shifted. "The Wardens don't care about us. They care about the ledgers filling up. They care about the Chorus taking too many mouths to feed."

"Exactly," the Cartographer said, her voice metallic but calm. "The Chorus grows stronger while we fight each other. They want us divided. They want us weak."

"Or they want us desperate," Suri added. "When a man can't pay his debt, he'll sell anything. His blood, his organs, his years. They don't need to fight us if we're already handing them what they want on a silver platter."

The room fell silent. Dar could feel the Lily in his chest, a cold pulse against his ribs. It was feeding on the darkness of the cistern, on the tension in the air, on the fear that was thicker than the salt fog outside. He could feel the Bone Vein stirring beneath his skin, a deep ache in his marrow like a storm turning in his bones.

"I've brought something," the Cartographer said, reaching into her satchel. She pulled out a rolled parchment and spread it on the stone floor. It was a map of Ashkarad, but not like any Dar had seen before. The streets were lines of living tissue, the buildings organs, the Choratorium a black hole at the center. "The city is singing. And the song is changing. Something is growing beneath it."

Mother Gavel leaned forward, her eyes narrowed. "What does that mean?"

"It means the Chorus isn't just harvesting organs," Dar said. "They're building something. Something big. And if we don't stop them, they won't just take our people. They'll take the city itself."

The Hammers representative spat on the floor. "Impossible. The Chorus is just a bunch of mad surgeons in masks."

"Are they?" the Cartographer asked. "Then why do their tithe-clinics never run out of supplies? Why do the Wardens turn a blind eye when they 'voluntarily' harvest debtors? Why does the salt moon eclipse coincide with their biggest festival?"

Dar could feel the Lily thrumming in time with his own heartbeat, a cold song that promised strength but demanded more. He could feel the Bone Vein opening further, his bones hardening, becoming denser, more resistant. He could feel the weight of the city pressing down on him, the weight of every life that had been taken, every debt that had been called in.

"Fine," Mother Gavel said, standing up. "We'll agree to your truce. But only if you get us something in return."

Dar raised an eyebrow. "What do you want?"

"Your surgeon," she said, nodding at Suri. "I want her to work for me. Her arm is impressive, but I know she can do more. I know she can count things other than debts. I want her to count our enemies."

Suri stiffened. "I don't work for syndicates."

"Then you work for the ledgers," Mother Gavel said. "Your choice."

Dar looked at Suri, then back at Mother Gavel. "She works for me. And in return, your syndicate provides safe passage. No one touches her. No one touches anyone from the Saltstairs."

Mother Gavel smiled, a thin, predatory expression. "And what do you get out of this, little surgeon? Besides a dead enemy?"

Dar touched his chest, where the Lily pulsed beneath his skin. "I get to live. And I get to make sure they don't take anyone else."

The truce was sealed with a handshake, sealed with blood drawn in the dark, sealed with the weight of unspoken promises. The syndicates agreed to the terms, the couriers agreed to run the canals dark, the Cartographer agreed to spread the word. And as they left the cistern, one by one, Dar felt the Lily drinking it all in, the darkness, the tension, the fear, the hope. He felt the Bone Vein opening further, his bones becoming denser, harder, more alive. He felt the weight of the city pressing down on him, the weight of every life that had been taken, every debt that had been called in.

Dawn came with a salt-white sky that bleached the color from the world. Dar stood on the balcony of his apartment, overlooking the Saltstairs, the buildings stacked like bones against the storm-walls. He could feel the Lily in his chest, a cold pulse against his ribs. It was feeding on the dawn light, on the hope that was thinner than the salt fog outside. He could feel the Bone Vein settling, his bones hardening, becoming dense as coral, his senses sharpening until he could hear the heartbeats of the city below, could smell the blood in the streets, could taste the fear in the air.

Suri stood beside him, her carbon-scaled arm crossed over her chest, counting the heartbeats, the lies, the debts.

"They agreed," she said. "Mother Gavel, the Hammers, even the Undergleam boats. They agreed to the truce."

"Did they really?" Dar asked. "Or did they just agree to wait? To let us do the dirty work while they watch from the sidelines?"

Suri was silent for a moment. "They agreed not to harvest from the Saltstairs. That's something."

"It's something," Dar said. "But it's not enough. Not when the Chorus is building something beneath our feet. Not when they're trying to become the city itself."

He looked out at the Saltstairs, at the people going about their lives, selling blood, selling organs, selling years, just to survive. He looked at the storm-walls, at the salt storms called "grace" that white out the streets for days. He looked at the Choratorium, the cathedral of grafts, rising like a bone-white spire against the sky.

"We have to stop them," Dar said. "Before it's too late."

Suri nodded. "We have a plan. The sewers. The Cartographer mapped them. We can get in through the old aqueducts."

Dar nodded. "And Vahid?"

"Still at the Choratorium," Suri said. "He left a note. He said he had to destroy Shirin's voice. He said he had to finish what they started."

Dar sighed. "He's going to get himself killed."

"Maybe," Suri said. "Or maybe he's going to do what we can't. Maybe he's going to give us the chance we need."

Dar looked at the Lily in his chest, at the cold pulse against his ribs. He could feel the Bone Vein settling, his bones hardening, becoming dense as coral, his senses sharpening until he could hear the heartbeats of the city below, could smell the blood in the streets, could taste the fear in the air. He could feel the weight of the city pressing down on him, the weight of every life that had been taken, every debt that had been called in.

"We have to move," Dar said. "Before the Blacktide."

Suri nodded. "Before the eclipse."

They turned to leave the balcony, to prepare for the sewers, for the Choratorium, for whatever waited beneath the city. But as they stepped back inside, Dar stopped. He looked back at the Saltstairs, at the syndicate banners hanging from the buildings. And he saw it.

Every banner, every flag, every piece of cloth marking a syndicate's territory was marked with a petal of black frost. Delicate as lace, intricate as a spider's web, the frost petals covered every banner in the district. And every petal pointed the same way, compass-true, toward the Choratorium.

Dar stared at the frost petals, at the delicate black lace against the salt-white sky. He could feel the Lily in his chest, a cold pulse against his ribs. It was feeding on the frost, on the fear that was thicker than the salt fog outside. He could feel the Bone Vein settling, his bones hardening, becoming dense as coral, his senses sharpening until he could hear the heartbeats of the city below, could smell the blood in the streets, could taste the fear in the air.

"She knows," Dar said, his voice barely a whisper. "She knew all along. She left the door open. She wants us to come."

Suri looked at the frost petals, then at Dar. "Then we come."
