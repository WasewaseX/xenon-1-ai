# Chapter 28 — Sanctuary Terms

Mother Gavel annexed the Choratorium on a Tuesday, between the morning bells, without firing anything.

Suri read the notice where it had been pasted to the tithe-barge office in the Marrowworks belt, and counted the clauses because that was how she read everything now: seven. All Church property within the storm-walls was claimed by the Gavel under right of salvage, the claim to be held in trust for the city until a lawful authority existed to receive it. All former Church debts in Gavel territory were voided — *voided*, not forgiven; a voided debt could be re-entered in a new book later, and Suri imagined Mother Gavel knew the difference the way a knife knows the difference between resting and waiting. And clause five, the clause that had been written last and therefore mattered most: all syndicates, couriers, and free institutions within territory claimed by the Gavel owed the Gavel protection dues, payable in coin, in labor, or — the phrase sat on the paste like a fly in amber — *in records.*

The Truce of Salt was fraying. Suri had watched it happen with the professional calm of a woman who has audited a sinking hull before: no Choir to track anyone, no Church to arbitrate anything, no Wardens with teeth, and the Hammers and the couriers too thin to hold what the fall had opened up. A vacuum in Ashkarad was not an absence. It was an opportunity with a ledger behind it, and the Gavel had simply walked in and opened the book. The Choratorium's bone arches flew frost-petal banners by nightfall — the same compass-true petals that had pointed at the Choratorium during the march, now flown by the people who had once marched under them, which Suri thought was either irony or economics and knew better than to bet on which.

And clause five had a rider, posted within the day, small, exact, and addressed: *the free clinic of the Saltstairs will render its patient registry as protection records, per clause five, due within the week.*

The price of protection. The names.

Suri brought the rider to Dar, and Dar read it twice and said nothing for the length of three breaths, which from him was a wound assessment. Then he said, "We're not a syndicate."

"We're an institution with a registry," Suri said. "That's what the clause actually says. Institutions with registries. She wrote it for us. Everything else is salvage."

"She can salvage the ruins," Dar said. "The registry stays sealed."

"She'll send men to unseal it."

"Then we'll discuss it with her men," Dar said, in the voice that meant a price had already been set and only the parties were outstanding.

Her men came on the fourth day, at the shift-change hour, when the lanes were empty and the light was the color of old debt. Suri counted them down the tenement stair: four Gavel enforcers in mixed bone-plate and leather, brass knuckle-counters on their belts, and a ledgerman behind them with a slate — the Gavel always sent a ledgerman, because the Gavel understood that force was only the interest; the principal was always paper.

They had come for the Parra family. Old Parra, canal-works widower, both hands ruined by pump-vanes, and his two grandchildren, had defaulted on nothing and owed nothing, which was precisely the problem: the Gavel was enrolling debtors again, quietly, street by street, and a man with no debts made a bad first entry. The family had come to the clinic at dusk and asked — in the old formula, from the years when the formula meant the Chorus — to be written down as patients, and Dar had written them down, because that was what the book was for. Old Parra sat in the ward's corner with his ruined hands folded like tools retired from service, and the grandchildren — a boy of seven who counted everything under his breath, having learned it from watching the couriers, and a girl of four who had decided the clinic's cat-shaped shadow on the ceiling was a pet — had already been absorbed into the ward's economy: the boy carried water with Elias, the girl had appointed herself the pallets' protector and took the office seriously. Suri had entered them under *PARRA, HOUSEHOLD (3)*, doses and dates, no prices, and had found her own hand hesitate over the line, because once, in another ledger, in another city, a clerk had hesitated over her name, and the hesitation had been the last unpriced thing that touched her for a year.

The enforcers filled the ward doorway the way men do when they have been told the building is small. The ledgerman read from the slate: "Three entries outstanding. Parra, head of household, canal-works, enrolled under the protection schedule. Surrender the household and the registry, per clause five, and we file this as compliance."

"Compliance has a counteroffer," Dar said.

He was standing at the operating table with his sleeves rolled, midway through closing a crane-man's gashed thigh, and he did not stop working; the needle rose and dipped with the unhurried rhythm of a man whose heartbeat had never once been in arrears. "First: the family stays. They came in as patients. Patients leave when they're discharged, and discharge is a medical decision, and the only medical officer in this building is me. Second: the registry stays sealed. Third—" and here the needle paused, and Dar looked up, and gave the four of them the whole of his attention, which was a famously expensive thing to receive, "—every man in this room will someday need stitching, and I mend without asking who sent you, and I remember without writing anything down. Those are the terms. There's no version of the next hour where they improve for you."

The youngest enforcer shifted, and Suri watched Dar's left hand, flat on the table beside a bowl of dark water — and watched the water in the bowl stir, faintly, against gravity, a single thread of red rising out of it like a filament remembering what it was. The Blood Vein did not threaten. It quoted. The enforcers' leader saw it too; his jaw set the particular way of a man recalculating in front of witnesses.

"The registry," the leader said, "or the schedule."

"You can tell her," Dar said, resuming the stitch, "that the registry is the patients, and the patients are under my knife, and my knife has never once been for sale — and she has known me long enough to know I put a fair price on everything else. Send the counteroffer up the chain. I'll receive it tonight."

They withdrew. The ledgerman left last, and at the door he turned, and said, with the first honesty of his visit: "She's going to like that you bargained. She hates a gift. Gifts can't be entered." The bowl's red thread settled back into dark water, and the clinic breathed.

By evening the lane outside had its opinions, the way lanes do. The Hammers' remaining crews went conspicuously, loudly unarmed past the clinic's door — a message priced to be read: the Gavel had moved on the Choratorium and the Hammers had not, and every silent walk-past was an audit of the truce. In the Undergleam, the Open Counts' talkers were saying that a new count was coming and the only question was whose thumb would hold the ledger down, which Suri filed under statements that were true and useless. Ashkarad was choosing up, the way a body chooses up around a wound — not toward health, toward the infection with the better terms.

The counteroffer came up the chain faster than Suri expected, which told her the Gavel had wanted a reason to deal. She carried it herself to the annexed Choratorium that evening, because the negotiation was hers — Dar standing off enforcers was surgery; this was commerce, and commerce was her vein — and because Mother Gavel had once put a price on Suri's head, and Suri had a superstition about finishing transactions in person.

The graft-theater had been swept of its reliquaries and swept again. Gavel crews worked the bone arches by lamplight, and at the center, on the surgeon's dais where the First Singer's altar had been broken, Mother Gavel had set a table, a lamp, and her ledger, and sat behind them with her scarred roadmap of a face and her eyes flat as a filed page. She did not offer tea. Suri respected her enormously for it.

"You bargained through your cutter," Mother Gavel said. Her voice needed no brass tube at this range; it arrived with its own weight. "I sent four men and a slate for one family. You sent a counteroffer for a building. I raised four; you raised the stakes. I could be insulted."

"You could be paid," Suri said. "Here's the trade. The clinic is declared sanctuary — formal, posted, both sides' seals on it. Anyone under its roof is under the clinic's name, not yours, not the Index's, not the Wardens'. No names leave the building. In exchange, the clinic mends your crews — walk-ins, no questions, uncounted. Your men bleed in my ward and no entry is ever made with their names in it. You don't ask for our book; we don't keep a book on yours. Mutual blindness. That's the whole price, both directions."

"And my clause five?"

"Is about institutions with registries," Suri said. "Ours has no prices in it. You can't collect what's never been valued. Ask your ledgermen — they'll tell you a book with no numbers in it is the one book that can't be audited. I'm offering you the only ledger in Ashkarad that's safe to leave open, because there's nothing in it anyone can take."

Mother Gavel was silent for a moment. Somewhere above, a Gavel crew lowered a reliquary rack down a stairwell with the tenderness of men moving a coffin. "Mutual blindness," she said at last, and there was something in the flat voice that might, in a face less filed, have been amusement. "I built my crews on knowing everything that moves. You're asking me to underwrite a hole in my own map."

"I'm asking you to underwrite the only building in this city that stitches your men free of charge," Suri said. "That's not a hole in your map, Mother. That's a mercy your book can't hold. Even you have to enter it somewhere."

The old woman looked at her for a long moment, eyes flat as a ledger page, and then did the thing that made the hair rise on Suri's arms, because it was the first genuinely unpriced gesture she had ever seen from her: she closed her own ledger.

"Sanctuary," Mother Gavel said. "Posted terms, both seals. No names leave the building — theirs or mine." A pause, one beat, an auditor's beat. "Agreed."

Too easily, Suri thought, walking home through the fog with the signed card in her coat like a coal. Too easily by exactly one clause she hadn't asked for and couldn't find.

She found it the next morning, on the stair opposite the clinic door: a Gavel ledgerman with a slate, unhurried, comfortable, paying out twine like a man measuring a room. Across the lane, a second. He did not look away when she looked at him. He looked at the door, and made a mark, and spoke to the slate in the tone of a man reading figures.

Counting. Not names — the names were sealed, and the seal held. Foot-traffic. Who entered the clinic and when, how long they stayed, who left and in what direction. Hour by hour, a census of the doorway itself, assembled out of nothing more expensive than patience — and Suri stood on her own step and understood, with the flat clarity she reserved for the worst entries, what she had sold.

She had kept the names out of the book. She had not kept the building out of the count.

She went in and opened her ledger to a fresh column, and wrote the heading in the small hand she saved for things that had no price and would be billed anyway:

GAVEL — SANCTUARY (AGREED)

And below it, after a moment:

OBSERVED — DAILY, COUNTED — DAILY
