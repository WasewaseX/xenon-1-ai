# Chapter 88 — What the Harvest Was For

The parley flag was white with salt by the time it reached the tide-gates, and the man under it was older than the flag's cloth by ninety years.

Behind him the seabed kept its patient arithmetic. The tide had not come back — the sea stood ten miles out like a debtor avoiding a door — and the fleet's camp lay grid-perfect on the wet mud where fish should have been: tents of clerks, ranks of white-gloved auditors, the herald-chorus at drill in files you could have ruled with a straight-edge, and at the center of it all the wain, vast, barnacled, hung with frost that pointed inland, a reliquary on wheels that had crossed the ledger of the world to stand at this gate. The bloom-fog breathed around it all. Somewhere in that grid a cough counted itself in a cadence Suri had known since childhood, and Suri counted the parley's minutes and did not turn her head, because turning her head would have been an entry, and she was not ready to file it.

Wen came alone this time — no auditors, no case, no courtesy except the flag itself — and he came to the gate the city had unbarred for him, and he stood in the grey light with his hands folded like a clerk at a counter, and he said, without preface:

"You testified me, cutter. The whole city heard the first seed's deposition. I've heard it too, by now — everything bloomed hears everything bloomed, that was always the design — and the Warden's office has spent two days filing it." He looked up at the walls, at the censer-smoke rising from the streets in its rationed threads, at the frost-petals on the gate's iron pointing inland like a weathervane made of debt. "The books are wrong about one line, and I've come under the flag to correct the record before the wain moves. Ask me anything. I'm authorized to answer, which should frighten you more than any terms I've ever brought."

Dar came down to the gate with Suri at his shoulder counting the parley's minutes, Vell at her elbow with the chalk, and the Choirmaster behind them with his fire-axe left behind and his Church still on him like a coat. Vell chalked the hour of the parley's opening on the gatepost, and as she finished, the frost-petals on the gate's iron turned — all of them, together, very slightly, the way a room of listeners turns toward a witness — toward the man under the flag.

"Say it plainly," Dar said. "What does the fleet want with Ashkarad?"

"Not what you've priced," Wen said. "Not punishment. Not an example. The schedule was never cruelty, and I'll say that to Vell of Brack's face, though I know what it costs to hear." He inclined his head to the mute clerk — and Vell, who had crossed the flats on chalk and fury, did not move, and did not forgive, and wrote one line on her slate and turned it around: *THE RECORD IS LISTENING.* "The fleet wants the Door opened. That is the whole of the warrant. Every tent on that seabed is a clerk of the opening."

"The Door," the Choirmaster said. His voice had gone very careful, the voice of a man handling a page he has only ever seen under glass. "Capital-D."

"Capital-D," Wen agreed. "The one beneath the salt. The one your Church was built on top of without ever once being told what it rested on. Your Ordinal's first page, choirmaster — the doctrine the founders cut away from the rest and carried like a bone. Say it. I'd rather hear a Churchman say it than say it myself."

The Choirmaster said it. He said it the way he had once read children's names from a burning registry, level and clear, a man determined to be accurate about the thing that had eaten his life: "The Ordinal of the Undersong, first page, oldest clause. *The deep door keeps itself. No hand opens it from without. It answers only to a living Throne set within — the door opens from inside, or it does not open.*" He let the axe-hand he wasn't carrying fall open. "Forty years I served the machine that sang the lid to sleep, and I never once asked what the lid was on. We were the padlock's night-watch."

"The harvest feeds the Root," Dar said. He could feel the geometry closing as he said it, account by account, the way the Mind Vein had taught him to feel a diagnosis arrive. "The prunings. The round. Brack, Lowmark, the rest. Rendered and poured down the deep roads on a schedule — not tribute. *Sedation.* Keep it dreaming while the key is grown."

"While the key is grown," Wen said. "Yes. A living Throne, cutter, and there are only two ways in a century's arithmetic to grow one. Bloom a seed to Throne in the field — eight cities tried it, and eight cities are stamps in a registry now — or grow the key *in place,* slow, fed, sung to, indoors, where the books can watch it. The Gardener has been growing a key in this city for two years without touching it. She grew it the moment the ninth seed chose you. Everything since — the heralds, the surveys, the offered terms, the wain, the census of who in Ashkarad can sing — is the husbandry of one plant."

"The terms on every door," Suri said, flat. "Stock children and the ninth seed as tribute, and a place at the pruning schedule's tail. That wasn't a ransom."

"It was a *planting bed,*" Wen said. "You were offered the tail of the schedule because a harvested city is a quiet city, and a quiet city is where you grow a Throne without the neighborhood waking. She has done it before. She has had a century of practice at growing keys that don't turn."

"And your Custodian," the Choirmaster said. The words came out of him like a splinter leaving the hand. "Vey. Anra Vey. The woman who built the Choratorium and ran the harvest and meant to be the city's heart at the Blacktide — "

"— was her subcontractor," Wen said. "Filed. You understand the word; your Church ran on it. Vey was promised the chair — the wardenship of the opening, the seat your friend here keeps being offered in one language or another — in exchange for growing the key on site. She thought the key was herself. She built forty years of throat-machinery to force her own bloom at your eclipse, and the door's inhabitant sang her out of her gloves before the verse finished. The books wrote it down as a defaulted line and moved on." He looked at the Choirmaster with something that was not quite apology and not quite pity, and was perhaps the oldest thing at the gate. "You kept her night-watch for her. I kept *hers.* Between us we have served the same locked door under two different seals."

He said it without drama, and that was the worst of it — a clerk correcting a column. And then the clerk's voice did change, once, the way old cloth tears, because he had come to the line he had carried up the whole length of his kept-alive century, and the Warden of the Reclaim set it down on the counter between them like a coin he had been holding under his tongue since he was a boy in the fog with a dying lantern for a heart.

"She doesn't want a Throne. She wants her daughter's door unlocked. I was the first key she tried. I jammed."

No one at the gate moved. Out on the flats the fog moved for them, slow, carrying the smell of tallow and far cities.

"She made me before the sowing," Wen said. "Or she *took* me — the order of operations stopped mattering to me sometime in the second decade. I was a boy and she put the ninth seed in me, the last one, the one that had been waiting longest to be carried. And it fled me. Not because I was cruel — I would have carried it anywhere, I would have carried it *still* — because I was *first,* and firsts are for learning on, and the seed knew the arithmetic better than I did. It left me in the dark on the flats and it went to find a better chest, and it took a hundred years to find yours." He touched his collarbone, where — Dar had seen it at the first parley, under the half-bloomed lace — the scar sat like a keyhole with nothing left in the house to lock. "A century, cutter. She kept me alive on her books that whole time. Do you understand what the *books* are? Not punishment. Not reward. A ledger is a promise that you will still exist when your account is next looked at. She kept me existing. She has never once asked me whether I wanted to be a line."

"Her daughter's door," the Choirmaster said quietly. "The daughter she locked above the deep. The first vessel. You're telling the court the warrant is — " he stopped, and started again, and the second time it came out as the thing it was. "She's been harvesting cities to keep a dead thing asleep so that nothing wakes up and disturbs her before she can carry her daughter home."

"I'm telling the court the warrant is *grief,*" Wen said. "Filed in a hundred-year hand. I was there when the deep stopped answering her — I was her key before I was her clerk; I was counting when the count stopped counting back. You heard the first seed's testimony. The Root is not hers anymore. It has not answered to her hand in decades, and she has been feeding it *blind* ever since, on books, on schedule, on the only song she has left — because the alternative is to stop feeding it, and a gardener does not stop feeding, whatever the garden has become. She thinks she is a warden. I have watched her for a hundred years, and I will file it under what it is. She is a woman at a locked door with her daughter's body in her arms, and the door only opens from inside, and the only thing that has ever been able to stand *inside* and open it is a living Throne." He looked at Dar, and his ancient face did the thing it had done at the first parley, when he had asked his one quiet question and cracked Dar worse than any terms. "You refused her twice. Blacktide and bloom. I'm not here to change that. I'm here because the geometry finally closes and somebody had to read the total aloud."

"The total," Suri said.

"The fleet does not want Ashkarad punished," Wen said. "The fleet wants the Door open, and the key grown, and the daughter buried in the garden she was stolen from — and every path to it runs through the ninth seed and the man it chose, and the man has spent two years refusing to be a door. So. Now you know what the harvest was for. Now you know what the wain is *for.* File it where you like."

He gathered his flag. The parley's minutes were up — Suri called the count, flat, and Vell chalked it — and for a moment the Warden of the Reclaim stood in the gate with the fog beading on his century like salt on glass, and asked, for the record, the one thing left on his sheet.

"The fleet's true books," he said. "The ones I keep, not the ones she lets be posted. Every seed is entered by its song — it's how the round is audited, it's how a city's ripeness is certified. Brack's seed: entered, harvested. Lowmark's: entered, harvested. The Vigil's: entered, quarantined, singing to itself in the dark for two hundred years." His hand lay flat on the gate's iron, over the frost-petals, over the scar-line of the door beneath the world. "The ninth's entry has stood open for a century. One line. *Song: unverified.*" He looked at Dar's chest, at the place where two heartbeats kept a ledger the books had never held, and the Warden of the Reclaim, first of her failed keys, asked the only thing he had ever asked for himself, for the true books, before he ended:

"Does it really sing?"
