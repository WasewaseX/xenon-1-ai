# Chapter 56 — The Burned Garden

Vahid read the law off his own cellar wall before he would sign the descent order, because the law was his, chalked up there at the price of a season's arguments: WE DO NOT OPEN DOORS WE DID NOT BUILD.

"Knocking," Dar said, "is not opening."

"Knocking," Vahid said, with the precision he kept for the moments the gin was furthest from him, "is a request to open. The door in your head is a tenancy, boy, and I have spent a year teaching you that the corridor is yours and the door is not. If she opens it — " and his one hand opened and closed on nothing, the old gesture of a man counting instruments he no longer had — "then it was never yours to begin with, and we had all better know that now, under controlled conditions, with the rope counted and the counting woman on the line, than alone at three in the morning with the ash in your teeth. Conditions. Or I burn the order."

The conditions ran to nine lines and Suri copied them into the ledger, and the ninth was Isra: she goes down. Vahid asked for her himself, having watched her for two days through the bottom of his spectacles and the top of his despair, and what he said was: "The eldest does not know us. The eldest knows her own. The root in that woman's chest has been drinking from the deep since before the seawalls. If anyone on this earth is a door the Sister will answer, it is her Vessel, and I will not pretend otherwise to spare the girl the stairs." Isra, asked directly in the coal-dark, wrote one word, YES, underlined nothing, and that was the whole of the committee's deliberation.

They went down the next night, because the descent had learned to be nocturnal — the dark fed her — and because Dar wanted the city asleep above them when he did what he was going to do. Roya held the shaft head, as she held it always, with the chalk at her feet and the mask turned down into the dark like a lamp. Suri counted the steps aloud from the first, her flat working voice dropping down the shaft ahead of them like a plumb line: one, two, three, the numbers going down into the Marrowworks' underbelly, past the donors' old climbing chalk, past the plate with its nailed warning — DO NOT COUNT THE HEARTBEATS — which Suri stepped over the way you step over a threshold in a house where someone has died, and on down past the dead First Vessel's cathedral, the cold one, the vast flesh-architecture with its too many closed eyes, where the air had the stillness of a room nobody had aired since the fighting.

At step five hundred the walls began to ring. By six hundred they rang with every footfall, salt singing like struck glass, and Isra walked it barefoot, and the salt rang under her feet differently — answered her, a half-step down, the way it had answered the deep music in the cellar — and by seven hundred the second heartbeat was no longer something Dar heard. It was something he stood in. The risen tide of it filled the vault, filled his coat, filled the space his blood claimed for itself, patient and immense and one beat behind his own, and the older voice under it was so low he felt it in his jaw, in his teeth, in the fillings of the world.

The corridor ended at the door of translucent salt-glass, clear as deep water, standing ajar by a hand's width, breathing its warm loam-scented air out of the dark behind it. The five-petaled keyhole sat level with his chest. Everything was where the petal had left it.

Isra went to the glass and put her palm flat on it, and the petal at her collarbone lifted its edges and pressed itself to the salt the way a face presses to a window, and out of the deep the great beat faltered — not stopped; stumbled; a count losing its place for the length of one held breath — and then resumed, closer somehow, as if something enormous had turned over in the dark and put its ear against the other side of the door.

Dar took out the petal-token — Old Tally's, the third seed's, black and cool a month ago — and it was blood-warm in his hand, and he laid it against the glass beside Isra's fingers.

Three heartbeats. His, and the ones following his. The deep went still as a held ledger-line, and the frost bloomed.

It came up under his palm and Isra's and the token, grey five-petaled flowers fanning across the salt-glass in matched pairs, the pattern it had made the night the two songs had first found each other through this exact wall — and then the pattern deepened, and the frost opened outward in rings, ring inside ring, a recognition written in the only language the vault had, and Isra made a sound that was not a word and not a cry but the breath of someone watching a debt they thought unpayable quietly get paid.

"Now," Dar said, and sat down against the glass with his coat over his knees like a man on a night shift, and closed his eyes, and built the corridor.

The Mind Vein opened the way it always opened, not as a gift but as a wound: the dark behind his eyes organized itself into architecture, walls, halls, the long gallery of himself, and he walked it at the pace of a man crossing his own clinic. He passed the swept rooms first, because they stood along the way and because discipline was passing them: the room where his mother's face had hung — swept, bare hooks, nail-holes like small closed mouths; the room where his father's laugh had lived, swept, the shelving dustless and bare; the room of the first kiss; the room that had been the color of his childhood home, swept to the plaster. Nothing spent tonight. Nothing taken out. He kept his hands at his sides in the corridors of himself, a surgeon's field of will, and walked to the deepest hall, where the floor changed under his attention and the lamplight came from a source he had never placed.

The door stood at the hall's end. The one he had never built. Furnished, somehow, behind its own wood — a frame he did not know, a hang of old cloth, lamplight moving faintly beneath it, warm on the far side the way a room is warm when someone has just left it, or never left it. It hummed. It had hummed for a year, faint, a tune he almost knew, and he had walked past it nine times and declined it nine times, because the law on the cellar wall was the law.

But the law, he had told Vahid, said nothing about knocking.

He stood in front of the door in the deepest hall of his own mind, and he heard, through it, for the first time not faintly but whole, the tune it hummed: the lullaby, entire, the dead language he understood without hearing, and under the lullaby a sound he had never heard in his own architecture before, a sound with cinders in it, a vast soft endless crackling, like a fire heard through a wall. A fire that had been heard through a wall for a very long time.

He knocked. Two raps, surgeon's-knuckle, the way you knock on a consulting room.

The humming stopped.

The lamplight under the door went out. And the handle, which was not his, which had never been his, turned from the inside, and the door opened away from him into a heat he felt in his physical teeth, and Dar stepped — or was taken; the grammar of it would not file — through.

The garden was burning. It had been burning. It would be burning. The sky over it was a sky of falling ash, ash falling with the patience of snow in a country where it had been snowing for a hundred years, and the garden lay out under that sky in long tilled rows to every horizon, and every row was charred, and in the char, impossibly, green things were trying — he could see them trying, row after row, seeds straining up out of the ash and blackening at the first leaf — and none of it was memory the way his rooms were memory — still, filed, available to be walked — it was a world looping, the First Vessel's world in flames, the same fire over and over, the same ash, the same straining seeds, a whole sky's weather running on a wheel that never reached the top of its turn.

And in the burning's center, tending the rows, was a figure in white gloves.

She knelt in the ash and worked the soil with bare-tipped fingers that had once been gloved — the gloves white as bone, folded back at the wrist — and she planted, seed by seed, into rows that could not take root, and the rows blackened behind her, and she went back to the row's head, and planted again, over and over, forever, and at her wrists, above the folded gloves, was a ring of old burn-scars he had seen once before, signing a page in a drowned city, left-handed, bone-white, patient as a stamp.

The presence came then, the thing the garden was the inside of. It did not arrive; it had always been arriving. It was older than his architecture could hold and it leaned down through its own burning the way the Lily leaned him, and it gathered itself to a point in front of him, and it spoke.

Not in the walls. Not through the stone. In the room where he stood, in the dead language he understood without hearing, in a voice like the last organ of a dead choir remembering its part — the first words she had said to anyone in a century:

"Tell me the garden did not burn. Lie to me."

Dar, who had spent his mother's face and would not spend a lie on top of it, stood in her burning with his hands empty at his sides, and did the only honest thing his whole ledger-self knew how to do: he gave her the count. "I wasn't there," he said. "I don't know what burned. I know what's above us. A city that counts its dead by hand — four hundred and twelve names chalked on a cistern wall the week the Choir went silent, and the counting never stopped. A clinic where a woman entered a stranger in a book under no debt at all. I can't tell you the garden didn't burn. But I can tell you someone came down seven hundred and eleven steps to knock."

The ash thickened. It was falling on him now, he realized — on the corridors, the ash from her sky was settling through his halls, filing itself along his shelves, and where it settled, his rooms were changing: the swept room with the nail-holes was filling softly, gorgeously, with cinders, and the cinders were taking the shape of things, wrong shapes, ash-shapes, his mother's face being re-filed out of ash into something the fire preferred —

He heard, from very far up, through the vault, through the salt, through the wall of the world, a slate crack against salt-glass, and a palm go flat, and a petal flare; Isra, at her post, the anchor end of the rope, and the Lily in his chest drew herself down to a bulb, small, clenched, a child making itself nothing in a room where the fighting had started, and Dar took his own count in his teeth — two rhythms, one behind the other, hold the line — and stepped backward out of the burning garden, and pulled the door shut on its own warm dark, and sat against the salt-glass in the vault with the cold at his back and his heart going like a press-room.

The frost on the door was already subliming away, the rings fading, the message received. Isra knelt beside him, writing on the slate with no fluency at all, slowly, with effort, her hand shaking, the root spending her by the letter, and he watched the words assemble, and they were not a question:

SHE SHOWED YOU THE MOTHER.

Dar looked at his hands, at the fine grey dust already drying in the creases of his knuckles like a month of ashfall, and understood that the contact had not entirely closed behind him — that the burning had followed him up the corridor of himself as a smell follows a coat — and that somewhere behind his sternum, in a room he had never built, a figure in white gloves was still kneeling, tending rows that would not take root, over and over, forever.
