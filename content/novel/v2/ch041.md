# Chapter 41 — Beyond the Seawalls

By morning the whole city had the filing, because couriers are couriers, and a sentence spoken to the open air in a syndicate's annexed theater is a sentence that ships.

Vessel located. Send the wain.

It moved through Ashkarad the way a default notice moves through a tenement: shutter to shutter, faster than the bells, changing weight at every landing. The Index posted a notice on the census-hall ruin by noon — RE: REGISTRY IRREGULARITIES, SALTSTAIRS PARISH — which everyone correctly read as an offer to sell the parish's irregulars to whatever was coming, in exchange for being left off the audit. Mother Gavel's ledgermen began, quietly, to count the roads. The Open Counts sent the clinic a soft-capped delegation that said, in their new soft language, that the Wardens' protection extended to the seawalls, and Dar thanked them and did not say the obvious thing, which was that the seawalls were precisely what the protection was standing on.

Hesh's hired room over the chandler's shop stood empty by dawn — stripped to the boards, the surveyor's brass gone, the frame that had held a glass heart gone with it. On the sill, squared to the edge with a surveyor's neatness, lay one coin. The lamp-fee. He had paid Mother Gavel's insulting little charge on his way out of the city, and no one, afterward, could say in which direction he had gone, except that the fog to the south was thinner that morning, as if something had walked through it carrying a light.

The council at the clinic was short, because they had all done the arithmetic during the night and the arithmetic did not have two answers.

"The wain is a siege engine or a tithe-barn on wheels, and I don't know which," Vahid said, chalk in hand, one finger of the chalk ladder. "The old papers call it a conveyance and leave it at that — the way the Church's books called the harvest a tithe and the nursery a choir school. Nobody living in this city has seen one and stayed sane enough to file the description. If it's the first, it sits outside the walls and starves the truce out — and the truce, boy, the Truce of Salt, binds the syndicates to each other. It does not bind one step of ground beyond the seawalls. The moment that thing crosses the tide-line, every promise this city has made itself ends at the wall, and everything inside the wall is what gets negotiated away. You know what gets negotiated away first. She's nine, and she's stamped PERFECT, and the Index already has a notice on Milla's printout."

"If it's the second," Roya said, from her corner, in her tin voice, "it loads the harvest and takes it. Either way it comes for the vessel. And the vessel, at present, is the loudest thing in the salt." She tapped her own temple, where the song-lobe had been, where the silence lived. "We stopped lying to it last night. It has sung your true rhythm every hour since. You cannot be sheltered here. A city cannot hide a coin that rings."

"The answers are on the route," the Choirmaster said. He stood by the door of the cellar where the woman from Brack slept, half-starved still, upright out of old habit, like a man at a choir rail. "I kept the harvest-registry for forty years and I never once asked what the round was for. Somebody along it knows. Somebody along it has watched a city be closed like an account and lived."

"Then it's settled," Suri said, and closed her own count the way she closed everything, with the total spoken aloud. "You can't stay, we can't hide you, and you can't fight a wain with a clinic. So we don't fight it. We read it. The round runs forward" — she looked at Roya — "and errors are found reading backward."

"Walk the RECLAIM round in reverse," Dar said. "Start at the end of the ledger. Ashkarad, and the two behind it. Find what the collection is for before it arrives to collect."

Nobody argued. That was the strange mercy of the morning: that after two months of the city paying for his heartbeat in small uncounted ways, every person in the room had already, privately, without being asked, priced his leaving as the cheapest entry on the books. He took less than an hour to pack, because a surgeon's kit packs itself if you have done it enough times, and because the clinic was not his to carry — it was theirs, now, the way the truce was theirs, and he had to leave both in hands he trusted or the journey would be a debt he walked out on.

The hardest column of the leaving he kept to himself, and it was the one under the city. Somewhere below the Marrowworks, past the dead First Vessel's cold cathedral of flesh, at the end of the deep salt vaults, a door of translucent salt-glass stood ajar — unlocked from the other side, a five-petaled flower for a keyhole, and behind it the patient immense second heartbeat that had spent two months learning his own. She wanted to be found and was afraid of being found. He had stood at her door with his palm flat on the salt-glass and promised her nothing, because promises were the one currency he had learned not to issue, and now he was going to walk out of the city and leave her door open behind him like an unpaid account. The Lily turned once toward the deep, the whole length of his walk down, like a woman looking back up a stair.

They went down to the seawall gate at first light, through streets that had come out to watch, because Ashkarad had never in living memory opened the seawall for a departing walker, only for tides.

From the clinic's upper windows, the eleven looked down — the freed vessels, the last cohort of the Church's long arithmetic, Elias among them with his perfect lungs and his ruined idea of what a hospital was. None of them waved. They stood at the sills in a silence that had its own acoustics, and along the frames, between their small hands, the black frost-blooms of the city's quiet garden grew, and Dar understood, with two streets still between him and the harbor, that the garden had already ruled on his departure. Every bloom on the clinic face was pointing south. Not at him. With him.

Ilay was waiting at the foot of the gate stair with Roya, and she did not say goodbye, because Ilay did not use the word; she pressed her sound-map of the deep into his hand instead — the paper soft as cloth from two months of her pockets, the great spiral drawn in her careful child's strokes, the innermost ring dated soon, the whole descent of the salt-glass door rendered in the language of a girl who heard the world from underneath. Dar took it the way you take a held breath from someone. "So you remember which way down is," she said, "in case you ever need to be under something." And then, because she was nine and precise: "Come back and tell me what the music says when it's outdoors."

"It's a promise," Dar said. "Unpriced. Don't let anyone here collect on it."

"The Professor says unpriced things are the only things that stay," Ilay said, and stepped back into Roya's shadow, work done.

Suri took his pack off his shoulder before he reached the stair, weighed it in her ruined-graft hand — the dead carbon scales clicking like a burned ledger closing — and shouldered it onto her own. "Porter," she said, when he looked at her. "You work for me now, Mehran. Surgeons carry nothing but their hands; it keeps the hands honest. I carry the kit, the water, the salt-rations, the charts, and you, if it comes to that." She counted him, once, head to boots, the old flat audit. "I'm carrying you back. You're just going to be heavy."

"You're not priced for this," Dar said, because someone had to say it, because the ledger deserved the attempt.

"Nobody's priced for anything," Suri said. "That's the finding of my audit. Now walk."

Vahid came last down the stair, one hand on the rail, a canvas case over his shoulder, and he did not make a lecture of it, which was how Dar knew it was costing him. "Shirin's cylinders," he said, giving the case into Dar's arms — fourteen wax cylinders, each one wrapped in the Professor's own shirt-linen like relics, which they were. "Her archive. Everything we ever proved and half of what we only hoped. Listen to her before you listen to anyone, including me. She meant what she recorded." His voice went dry and exact. "I only ever talked." Then, at the foot of the stair, with the seawall looming: "And boy — the Ninth is a door, not a rung. Whatever hands you a key out there, look at the price stamped on the bow before you turn it."

The Choirmaster did not come down at all. He stayed at the top, by the clinic door, with the harvest-registry in its oilcloth across his folded arms — the vessel list entire, the names not yet crossed out — and he bowed over it, a small precise bow, the deepest thing Dar had ever seen him do. "Somebody burned the book I kept," the Choirmaster said. "The least I can do is stand where it isn't burning." Behind him in the doorway, Milla Ferun stood with the lamp still in her hand and her salt-white fingernails folded into her sleeves, and she gave Dar the nod that one clerk gives another across a closing entry: filed, witnessed, carried.

Vell was already at the gate, of course. She had been at the gate since before light; a gate-ward swears she was there when the watch changed. She stood with her slate against her chest and her face turned south, and when Dar reached the foot of the stair she turned the slate for him to read, in her tally-built letters, the whole of her application for the journey:

I COUNT FOR THE LIVING NOW. WATER MARKS. TWO CITIES. I AM YOUR FIGURE ONE.

Then she took a place in the column behind Suri without asking, the way a number takes its place in a sum.

The wardens sang out the gate-cries — the old cries, salt-cries, that the harbor children used to mock — and the great gate groaned back on its chains, and the wind of the open world came through, tasting of iron and salt and of nothing else at all, no hum, no chorus, no debt. From the canal wall behind them, thin across the morning, Lena Vance's returned voice rose after them for one bar of something that was not the lullaby, and Dar was glad of it; the city should send its own music to the door.

Then the seawalls opened, and Dar saw the fog-sea for the first time.

He had lived his whole life inside the accounting of it — tide-tables, gate-bells, the fog-sea as a margin at the bottom of every map — and it was nothing his margins had priced. It went out and out, grey-white, breathing, a country of weather lying on top of a country of water, and it made the city behind him feel like what it was: one entry. And the tide was going out. Not ebbing — going out, the exposed flats shining and clicking and steaming at the gate's foot, the water withdrawing from the land for a distance no tide-table had ever entered, as if the whole sea had been asked for its balance and was paying — far, far out, toward a horizon that sat low and patient on the world.

As if something out there, at the edge of the page, were drinking.

Behind him, under the city, the second heartbeat kept its new time — whatever the new time was — and against his own ribs the Lily leaned once, hard, back toward the deep she was leaving, and then, deliberately, forward, toward the sea.

Dar stepped past the gate-wardens, out from under the last stone of Ashkarad, and turned his face down the reversed round, and began to walk.
