# Chapter 78 — Her List of the Dead

Isra came up the shaft at the hour between bells, when the lamplowers were still arguing with the cold, and she carried the bundle against her chest the way divers carry their last breath — close, in both arms, as if letting go of it were a thing that happened to other people.

Seven hundred and eleven steps. She had gone down nine nights earlier with an empty satchel and a promise nobody had thought to ask her to make, and she had come back up with the satchel full and her lips the color of a ledger's margins. Suri met her at the shaft head, did the arithmetic aloud, because that was how she loved people.

"Nine nights in the cold. Writing." She took the bundle's weight one-handed and looked at Isra's blue knuckles, the frost-burn at the wrists where the root sat under the skin like a second pulse. "Sit down before I file you as an expense."

Isra sat. The bundle went onto the table between them, and it did not sit like cloth. It sat like a book: rectangular, cornered, sewn at the spine with sinew, the pages tallow-cured hide rubbed with ash-ink, and even before anyone touched it the room knew what it cost. Cold came off it in a slow fold, the way cold comes off a body that has stopped. Every stroke on every page had been paid for out of the writer's own warmth, nine nights of it, in the dark, seven hundred and eleven steps below the last lamplight in Ashkarad.

Isra let herself be steered to the stove bench and sat with her palms open toward the coals, and while the warmth crept back into her by visible degrees she watched the bundle the way a diver watches the surface — not with longing, exactly. With accounting. She had made an arrangement with the deep once, on the flats, out beyond the seawalls, and the arrangement had never once asked her for anything she hadn't chosen to give. Nine nights of dictation in the vault dark, the sister's cadence coming up through the stone like a pulse through a mattress, and Isra's hand keeping the record because Isra's hand was the only hand down there that could be paid in warmth and stay open. The root at her collarbone had drunk the dark and grown greedy, the way it always did below; the lamplight had taxed it, the way light always did; and between the two of them — the lamp and the dark — she had priced every page and paid it and gone on writing, because the voice under the world had said one thing the first night, and it had been enough.

WRITE IT TRUE. NO ONE ELSE WILL HAVE IT.

Suri opened the clinic book and ruled a line. "Gift, incoming," she said. "No debt column. Not yet."

They read it that evening in the cellar, under darkened lamps, with the doors drawn and the censers breathing their clean smoke. Dar at the table. Suri at his shoulder with the tally. Milla Ferun with her registrar's nib and the salt still under her nails. Vell at the wall with her chalk, taking the second copy stroke for stroke. Roya in the corner with Ilay, because the list was read twice that night — once off the page, and once out of the stone.

The pages were written in the dead language, the ledger-script scored a mile long on the flats, the script Dar understood without hearing. He had hoped, dimly, that understanding would feel like nothing. It did not. It felt like reading his own handwriting in a stranger's book.

The hand never varied. A century of discipline in nine nights of copying: every letter the same weight, every column ruled true, the dates entered not in days or moons but in counts — vast seven- and eight-figure numbers, each one a position in the deep heartbeat's ledger, each one a moment in the Root's long account. Milla converted nothing. "You don't translate a witness," she said. "You transcribe one."

Vahid leaned in over the first page with his one hand pressing the hide flat, and his spectacles down on his nose, and for a long moment the Professor of Impossible Anatomy read like a man reading his own discredited papers returned from the dead.

"Method," he said at last, half to himself. "Understand what you are looking at. She was filing while the world burned. Whatever else is down there, whatever it ate, whatever it lost — it kept books. Dates. Columns. A double entry, if my eyes are any use, and they remain my only remaining virtue." He tapped a column of figures with a cracked fingernail. "These are not annotations. These are balances. She has run the Root's account across a century the way a factors' house runs a freight ledger, and she has never once missed a posting. That is not the madness of a buried thing, boy. That is the discipline of a witness who knows the day will come when her word is all anyone has."

The names were cities.

Not the nine. Not first, anyway. The first pages belonged to a world before the Church, before Ashkarad, before salt meant grace — cities whose names went through Dar's understanding like water over slate, dozens of them, each with its date, each date a number in the millions, and Dar read them aloud in a flat surgeon's cadence because the alternative was to read them like an elegy and no one in the room could have stood that. Cities harvested into glass. Cities that burned. Cities whose names the script wrote the way you write a debt — once, cleanly, and never again.

Vell's chalk slowed at the ninth page, and stopped. She looked at Isra. Isra looked at the wall, and wrote one word on her slate, and held it up.

TESTIMONY.

"She has prepared it for a trial," Dar said. The understanding arrived the way diagnoses arrive — all at once, with the history already attached. A century below, keeping the lid, counting the Root's heartbeats, watching cities die through the stone: and through all of it, the witness had done what witnesses do. She had kept exhibits. She had kept her counts exact. Because someday someone would sit in judgment — the Gardener, the fleet, the far cities waking, the city she had defected to — and she had wanted, on that day, to be able to open a book and say: here is everything, dated, entered, true.

"She expects to be tried," Milla said, not looking up. Her nib moved through a silence with forty cities in it. "Clerks always do. We prepare for the audit we deserve."

Ilay's hands were flat on the cellar floor. She had been still a long time. Now she lifted her head, and her voice came out flat and literal, the way it came when the stone was saying things through her, and everyone stopped writing.

"She says: someone will. She would rather it were us."

The chalk did not move for a while after that.

The nine came at the end, and the nine were entered differently — not as the dead but as the living, each seed's city named in the Church's own tongue, each with its date of planting, its date of blooming, and a third column Dar made himself read twice. Brack. Lowmark. The Vigil. Gullet. Sarn. Weep. Ossuar. Hallowfen. Eight columns read BLOOMED in the script he now knew better than his own. Brack's third column had been written in the fleet's grammar, learned secondhand down the deep roads, and it read the way the tally-book had read it from the first: PRUNED, PAID.

One city dead in this round, seven more ripe on the schedule, and Ashkarad's own column left blank — not VESSEL: FLED—RECLAIM, Dar noticed. The Sister's ledger had no such stamp. Her column for the ninth said only: LENT.

"Ask her," Suri said quietly, because Dar had gone still over the word. "Ask her what lent means, and when she wants it back."

Dar didn't. He knew lenders; he had been one's patient, one's employee, and one's thief, and every lender he had ever met kept receipts precisely so the question would never have to be asked twice. A lent thing is a thing expected back whole. He closed the page on the word with more care than he had closed the pages of the dead.

But Ilay was already shaking her head, hands pressed harder to the floor, and what came up through her was not an answer about the ninth at all. It came slowly, in the Sister's own cadence, and Ilay delivered it word for word like a courier who knows the freight is fragile.

"If I go mad down there, sing the second verse, and I will remember I was loved before I was used."

Nobody spoke. The censers breathed. Vahid took his spectacles off, polished them on his cuff with his one hand, and put them back on, which was what he did instead of weeping; Roya's mask gave back the lamplight, blank as a filed receipt. It was Suri who moved first, because Suri always moved first when something needed to be entered before the room could talk itself out of it. She opened the census to its first ruled page, under Ilay's nine-petaled flower, and wrote in her flat clerk's hand a standing order, dated, sealed with both the sanctuary's seals:

THE CITY OWES THE DEEP ONE VERSE, ON DEMAND. SECOND VERSE OF THE GARDEN'S SONG. SINGER: ILAY. NO PRICE ATTACHED.

"The first unpriced thing we've ever owed," she said. "Keep counting anyway."

Ilay listened to something a long moment, then nodded once, solemn as a signature. "She heard it," she said. "She says the order is received." A pause. "She says no one has ever put her in a book that wasn't a cage."

Milla finished the page she was on before she spoke, because a registrar finishes her page. "Then she has never been read to," she said. "Only entered." Nobody answered her, and the nib went back to work, and the silence after it was the kind that files itself away to be spent later, when it will cost more.

It was Milla, transcribing the final page, who found the last entry — because it was the right shape to catch a clerk's eye. Not a city. The grammar was wrong for a city; it had no harvest columns, no bloom date. It was a single name in the dead script, short, and beside it a date from deep in the first million of the Root's count, a century old and more.

"The carrier," Isra wrote, before anyone could ask. "The first one. The one who fell."

The boy with the lantern, Dar thought, and did not know where the image came from — a fog, a light going down, an old grief that was not his and was. The Lily did not stir. That was the strangest thing. He had learned her moods the way he had learned his own pulse, and at the name on that page she went altogether still, in the way of a thing that has heard its own history read into the record.

Beside the name, in the same disciplined hand, the same ash-ink, the ink still smelling faintly of the vaults — added after a century, and the warmth-cost of three words paid as recently as nine nights ago, three words that had no business surviving a hundred years of ledgers:

He did not die.

Suri looked at the entry, then at Isra, then at the entry again, the way she looked at a column that would not balance. "When," she said. Not a question; a requisition.

Isra turned the slate, wiped it with her sleeve, wrote slowly, letter by letter, paying the warmth as she went.

SHE ADDED IT EIGHT NIGHTS AGO. THEN SHE LET ME STOP.

The lamps burned. The censers breathed. Seven hundred and eleven steps down, something that had spent a century keeping the only true books in the world sat behind a cracked glass door with her testimony finished at last, her exhibits in order, her count exact — and the last line of the ledger she had kept against the day of her trial said that the boy who fell did not die, and none of them knew yet what that was going to cost.
