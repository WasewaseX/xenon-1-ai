# Chapter 27 — Where the Singing Went

Roya had spent eleven years listening to a city no one else could hear.

The song-lobe they had wired behind her ear had sung to her in pressure — streets as currents, cellars as chambers, the whole of Ashkarad legible the way a reef is legible to a diver, and she had drawn it all, maps with lines that rose off the chalk like veins under skin, maps with weight. Then the First Singer had sung its one release-song, and the connection was severed — not muted, severed, cut at the root like a graft rejected — and what was left was a woman with a very good memory, a permanent brass mask, and the specific silence of a cartographer gone deaf in the one language her maps were written in.

The city was a closed book to her now. She was learning to read it through a child.

It was a humiliation with the skin of a privilege, and Roya audited it as such, nightly, because an unexamined feeling was a leak. She had been the Cartographer. The word had been a price list: the woman who could find anything that made a sound. Vey had hunted her signal for years across the Undergleam's drains and ducts — Roya had owned corridors the Custodian's census never reached — and in the end it was not Vey but the release-song that took the trade away, one chord, whole, like a page torn out. What remained was the mask, which she wore now against a world that had tried to harvest her song and might try again with less ceremony; and the memory; and her hands. On the bad nights she priced what was left exactly: a blind navigator, a sharp eye, a scarred hand, and the one instrument in Ashkarad that could still hear what she had lost, and the instrument was nine years old and came free.

Free was the one figure Roya had never learned to trust.

Ilay worked at the low table in the clinic's back room with the terrible patience of her kind of quiet, and Roya sat where she could watch the girl's hands, because the hands were the instrument now. Copper hair held back with a strip of clinic linen. Eyes the color of river stones, fixed on nothing, which meant they were fixed on everything at the depth where nothing lived. For three weeks after the Blacktide the girl had drawn what she had always drawn: great slow spirals, room by room, that she called *where the singing used to be* — a search, a grief, a record of a chord that had gone out of the walls.

On the first morning of the fourth week, the drawings changed.

Roya noticed it before she understood it, which was how she had noticed everything worth noticing since the mask. The spirals had direction now. They overlapped — each ring falling inside the last with a consistency that her eye, which had spent a decade pricing distances, refused to call accidental. And at the head of each ring, in charcoal so light it was almost breath, Ilay had begun to set small marks. Feet. The spiral had feet.

"It moved," Roya said.

Ilay looked up. When she spoke it was always in the flat, literal register of a child stating weather. "It's not where the singing was anymore." She turned the sheet around so the mask could read it. "It's where the singing went."

"Is it still singing?"

"It stopped singing." Ilay considered, and the consideration itself was worse than any answer she could have chosen. "Singing is when you stay in one place and the sound goes out." She put one small finger on the spiral's outermost ring and walked it, slowly, inward, ring by ring, and the finger never wavered, and Roya felt the hair on her arms rise under her sleeves. "This is walking. It goes in circles. Smaller circles. Like it's looking for the middle."

"Like it's homing," Roya said, and the word came out in the mask's flat metal, and she wished it back.

Ilay nodded once, satisfied, and went back to drawing.

The method took four nights to build and was, Roya admitted to herself on the third, merely her old trade with the wires reversed.

The grammar had to be negotiated, because the child did not think in directions; she thought in distances and loudnesses, the way the old song-lobe had. On the second night Ilay put the slate in Roya's hands and drew, slowly, a teacher's diagram: two circles, one large, one small. "The big sound is slow," she said, tapping the large circle. "It takes long between. Like a heart that is very far down or very large, and it's both." Then the small circle: "The little sounds are fast. Those are up. People." She took the slate back and wrote, beneath the circles, in her careful hand: *steps = how far. size = how loud. that's all it is.* "That's all it is," she repeated, and Roya, who had once charged the syndicates a month's tithe for that same sentence with more syllables on it, laughed inside the mask where no one could bill her for it.

Each night, Ilay listened. Roya watched the girl's lips move, counting something that had no number yet, and watched her hands, and asked the only questions the mask could ask without lying: *Where? How loud? How far between?* And Ilay answered, and between them, ring by ring, on the cellar wall that Vahid had given up to the project because the salt kept blooming under his chalk anyway, the new map grew.

It was a spiral, but by the sixth night it was a survey. Fourteen rings, each dated in Ilay's careful child's hand — she dated everything now, Roya had noticed; a child who had been filed her whole life filed her drawings back — each ring tighter than the last, each ring with its bearing off the compass rose, and the bearings converged. Not on the clinic. Not on the Choratorium, whose ruins the Gavel's banners were already claiming across the rooftops. The rings tightened toward the Marrowworks belt, and under the Marrowworks belt, along the one vertical line every old map in Roya's possession agreed on like a scar all of them shared: the Throne shaft. The great shaft. The deep salt vaults at the bottom of it, where the dead First Vessel had sung itself silent.

Suri audited the dates against the one external record they had: the rooftop. Dar had felt the second heartbeat from the clinic roof in the first storm, three weeks back; he had felt it again on each of the four storm-nights since, and each time, he reported, a little less far away. Ilay's first ring was dated the morning after that first storm. The rings and the roof agreed to the night. Roya had spent eleven years selling certainty, and she had never once had two independent instruments confirm a plot the same evening, and she found that the certainty sat in her chest like swallowed river water — heavy, cold, impossible to spit out.

Roya laid her oldest chart over the wall one night — the shaft-map, the one with the ledger-script margin, DO NOT COUNT THE HEARTBEATS, in a hand she had sold for favors a decade ago. The spiral's center sat on the shaft's lowest ring like a coin dropped on a target.

"Converging," Suri said behind her, arms crossed over her ruined scales, counting the rings out of habit. "Fourteen. It's had fourteen rings and it's still spiraling in. That's not wandering. Wandering doesn't keep books."

"It's arriving," Roya said.

Dar stood in the doorway with his coat still on from the clinic's last hour, and he looked at the map the way he looked at films before cutting — the whole surface, then the one line that mattered. "The second heartbeat," he said. "The one from the roof. This is what Ilay's been hearing."

"Ilay's been hearing more than that," Roya said, and looked at the girl, because it was the girl's report to give, and the mask did not carry other people's confidences.

Ilay was drawing again, the evening ring, the fifteenth, and she answered without looking up, literal as an invoice. "There's the big one." The finger walked the spiral, inward. "The big one walks. And underneath the walking there's a small one." She tapped the center of the spiral, twice, the way you tap a wall to find where it's hollow. "The small one keeps time with Dar."

The room did what rooms do when a child prices a secret in front of the room: nothing, loudly. Suri's count, Roya noticed, had stopped entirely, and Suri did not stop counting for anything that had a price.

"The small one," Dar said at last, carefully, in the voice he used to tell patients that the surgery would cost what it cost.

"In here." Ilay touched her own sternum. Then she reached up and touched Dar's coat, over the chest, once, with the matter-of-fact authority of a girl who had been a vessel-in-inventory before she had been a person and had never once confused the two filings. "You have two. The big one has one. It's lonely." A pause, the length of a held breath. "That's why it walks in circles. It's not hunting." She looked up at them then, river-stone eyes in lamplight, and delivered the sentence that Roya would keep behind the mask for a long time after: "It's coming to the only other one."

They finished the fifteenth ring near midnight. Ilay fell asleep in the chair with the chalk still in her fist, the way she did, and Roya carried her to the cot in the back, and came back to the wall because the wall had one more entry to be read.

The innermost ring. The fifteenth, tonight's, the tightest — a circle small enough now that Roya could cover its whole course with her scarred palm, the blade-scar whitening across it like a meridian. And at the ring's edge, in the careful hand that dated everything, Ilay had written the answer to a question nobody had asked yet.

Not a bearing. Not a count. A word, five letters, set down with the total seriousness of a child filing a fact into the only ledger she trusted:

soon.

Roya stood in front of the word for a long time, mask reflecting the lamp back at the wall.

Behind her, the clinic went on being the clinic — Suri's low voice setting the courier routes for tomorrow, a patient's cough through the floorboards, the ordinary noise of the only unpriced institution in the city. Above all of it, too deep for ears, the second heartbeat made its turn, and Roya realized she had stopped needing the girl's confirmation to feel it. Fifteen rings. Four weeks. Whatever walked below the salt had drawn a map of its own intentions on the wall of a child's refuge, and had dated it, and had been, by every instrument they had, on time.

She had sold every map she ever drew — to syndicates, to cutters, once, unforgivably, to the Church — and she had never once, in eleven years of the trade, been afraid of a thing she had charted herself. Fear was for clients. The mapmaker stood outside the map.

She was standing inside this one. They all were. The spiral's thirteenth ring, two nights old, passed through the Saltstairs. The fourteenth passed through the clinic's foundations. The fifteenth ring was due to close somewhere under the Throne shaft, and the word at its edge had been written by a nine-year-old who had never in her life exaggerated a count.

Roya picked up the chalk, and below the word, in her own hand, in the flat metal script the mask had given her, she added the only term her trade had for it, the one she used to write on charts she couldn't price:

*datum confirmed. not for sale.*
