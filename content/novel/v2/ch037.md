# Chapter 37 — What the Mute Carried

They put the woman from Brack in the cold cellar, in the cot nearest the stairs, because she could not say where it hurt and because everyone in the clinic had already, without discussion, stopped calling her the survivor. Vahid wanted that name entered and closed. Dar had learned not to argue with the Professor's triage until he had triaged it himself.

"She hasn't slept," Suri reported, at the cellar door, in her flat counting voice. "Four hours. She counts instead. Her fingers, the cot slats, our heartbeats — I've watched her take us all. She's not afraid, Mehran. She's auditing."

Milla Ferun sat with her for most of a day and came up the stairs looking like a woman who had seen her own trade buried at sea. "She's senior grade," Milla said. "Look at the cuff-guards, the way she rules a margin with her thumbnail before she'll write anything. In the Marrowworks we'd have paid her wage twice over and called it a bargain. They cut out the tongue of the best counter I ever stood next to." Milla's hands were salt-white at the nails, always now; she hid them in her sleeves. "Whatever they took a city's voices for, they took hers on purpose."

Vahid heard all of it, polishing his spectacles on his sleeve with the maddening one-handed economy he had learned since the amputation, and gave his ruling from the chalk ladder where he lectured like a man ascending a gallows he had built himself. "You are going to read her," he said. Not a question.

"She's the only witness the route has produced," Dar said. "And she's trying to give it to us. She's had that slate in her arms since the tide-gates. She's not guarding it. She's queuing."

"With the Mind Vein." Vahid set the spectacles back and became, for a moment, entirely the Professor of Impossible Anatomy, the gin gone out of his voice like water out of a cracked bowl. "Then hear the terms before you open the door, boy, because the instrument won't repeat them. The Mind Vein is not a lamp you shine into other people's windows. It is architecture. You will open your own halls and hers at once, and the traffic between two occupied minds is billed both ways. And the ledger that collects the fee does not take fractions. It does not take almost. It takes one memory, true and whole — and it selects."

"Can't I select?"

"You can carry an offering," Vahid said. "You cannot choose the till. That is what makes it a ledger and not a theft."

Dar stood a long moment in the cold. Below, through the floor, he could feel the deep heartbeat that had lived under the city since the salt-glass door — one beat behind his own, patient, immense, leaning. In his own mind, past the swept rooms, past the corridor where his mother's face had hung, there waited the furnished door he had never built, humming faintly, warm on the far side like a room someone had just left. He had learned to walk past it. He would walk past it again tonight.

He went down to the cellar and sat on the stool beside the cot, and he took inventory of himself the way he had taught himself to do in the burned season of his memory: what was spent, what remained, what he could afford to be asked for. Most of the big rooms were already empty — the mother's face, the father's laugh, the first kiss, the color of the childhood home; the great properties of his inner life, liquidated in one fight, at one altar. What remained was small stock. Patients. The clinic's mornings. Suri counting steps. Ilay's map of the deep. And one room he had never once needed to open since the intern years, because it contained no lesson and no comfort: the first patient he ever lost.

A boiler-man. Young, older than Dar had been then, hands burn-scarred in the crossed pattern of someone who worked pipes for a living and paid for it in skin. A borrowed gravel-heart on its last installments. Dar's sutures had been perfect — he could still say that, could still feel the even tension of them in his fingertips — and the heart had quit on the table anyway, at the hour, at the minute, like a debt called in. He had written the death notice himself, in his own intern's hand, and apologized to the body, which was not a thing they taught and not a thing that helped. He had carried that room for eight years, mostly shut, never locked.

It felt like the cheapest thing on the shelf. He was wrong, and he knew even as he priced it that he was wrong, and he offered it anyway.

The Mind Vein opened the way it always opened now: not as power but as a wound remembered. The cellar fell away. He stood in his own halls, and ahead of him, where no door had ever been, there was a door standing open into someone else's dark — and her dark was a counting-house.

That was his first knowledge of her, and it never afterwards left him: the woman from Brack kept, inside herself, a counting-house. Long benches. High shelves of ledgers gone. Every shelf had been stripped — not looted; audited — and where the ledgers had stood there were pale leaves now, pressed flat and thin as vellum, filed in their thousands, each one bearing the ghost of a pressure like a flower kept between pages. Her memories had been here. He understood that without knowing how he knew it: her memories had been here, and something had pressed them the way you press a claim.

He walked the stripped aisles, and the aisles went down, and at the bottom, behind a grille of iron that a strong room wears, there was one room still lit.

He went in through the grille the way the Mind Vein permits, which is to say: he was suddenly, without transition, on the other side.

The harvest of Brack. What remains of it, he corrected himself — what she kept, what survived the pressing by being too close to the wall to take. Fragments, and the fragments had edges like cut paper.

White gloves. That first. White gloves on every hand of them, moving down the counting-house benches in an order, an orderliness so complete it read as kindness, gloves lifting each finished ledger into a brass satchel and pressing it flat. Their faces, where the memory allowed faces, were finished — closed, settled, like pages at the end of a book — and they did not hurry, because nothing in the room was theirs to fear. On a side bench, in a pan of scored brass, a row of tongues lay tagged like keys. Redactions, said the part of his mind that filed horror in column form, so it could be carried. The counters' speech was entered into evidence and collected. Everything that could have spoken the count was taken out of the count.

Then the harbor-temple. Brack's Throne.

She had seen it from a high window, through her own city's last storm of grace — the white-out that Brack's children must have called by the same lying name. The Throne of Brack was grown into the temple dais the way a nail is grown into a beam: black lace and vascular bloom, a human figure long since exceeded by its own garden, its face closed and tallied, its chest — where a seed sits, where the Lily sat in him — cut open along a line of surgical exactness. They had taken the seed out of it like a tooth. He watched the gloved hands draw it, wet at the root, black and pulsing faintly, heavy as a pulled heart, and he watched the Throne's many-veiled face turn toward the window where a clerk stood watching — toward her — and he felt, across eight years and two cities, the Throne of Brack recognize the last open ledger in the room and look away, deliberately, spending its final act of will on the smallest mercy on the books.

The city went silent through the wound. Not slowly. Through it — as if silence were a tide and the seed had been the door.

The final accounting was signed on the dais. One page, left-handed, the glove moving with the unhurried fluency of long habit — and the signature was not a name. It was a flower. Five petals, blooming from a crack, drawn exactly, stamped down over and over in a column of identical signatures like a clerk who had long ago stopped being anyone.

Then the last room, the one she had saved the way misers save: herself.

The audit reaching the counting-house benches. The pressing. The gloves coming up her own aisle. And Vell — he had the name now, off the strong-room door-plate in her memory's own hand: VELL, SENIOR COUNTER, SECOND GRADE — Vell going to the wall where the death-column stood chalked, the list of the city's dead that some last clerk had kept to the end, and writing herself into it. Her own name, in her own careful tally-built letters. VELL. And beside it, the figure every machine on the barge had printed since: 0.

She had made herself a closed account, and the audit, which read the written world and nothing else, had passed over the one live entry in the building.

Counters, Dar thought, standing in her strong room with her whole city's silence in his ears, will outlast the auditors. It was not a comforting thought, and he filed it anyway.

And on the wall, scored deep across the plaster of the counting-house, over the death-column, by a hand that knew tallies better than letters, the last salvaged image rose to meet him as the Mind Vein began, gently, mercilessly, to close:

WE WERE COUNTED WORTH EATING.

He came back into the cellar with his face wet and did not know at which point in the reading the cold had entered his teeth. The Lily was pressed up under his sternum, molten and attentive, and her hum ran through his molars like a struck fork — she had drunk something from the walking, some lees of it, and he made himself breathe until she settled.

Suri was at the foot of the stairs with the lamp, counting his pulse from where she stood, out loud, to keep him honest. "Fifty-one," she said. "Fifty-two. You're back. What did it cost?"

He went to pay it and found the account already closed.

The room where the boiler-man had lived was swept. Not stripped like hers — swept, courteous, final. He reached for the man and had the shape of having reached for someone: the guilt was still hanging on its peg. The fact stood in the doorway of him — I lost a patient, my first, my own table, my own notice — the fact stood there like a name-plate over an empty office. The sutures he could feel in his fingertips, even their tension. The burned, crossed pattern of the hands — and even that, as he watched, going grey at the edges, like a figure in fog.

The name went first. He never had it again.

"It took the first patient I ever lost," he said. "All of it. Whole."

Vahid, from the ladder, one hand steady on the rail: "That's what whole means, boy. That's the rate."

On the cot, the woman from Brack was watching him with her counting eyes, and she had read the payment off his face the way she read everything, in figures. She turned her slate over. She spat a chalk-stub into her palm — they had learned to leave chalk within her reach the way you leave water — and on the slate's clean back she wrote, in the tally-built letters he now knew her by, her first address to the living city that had taken her in:

WATER MARKS. I KNOW THE WAY. I WILL COUNT YOU THERE.

Then, beneath it, smaller, the way a clerk signs a working file:

VELL. OF BRACK. REMAINDER: ONE.
