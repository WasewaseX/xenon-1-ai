# Chapter 45 — True Currency

The Ashkarad box came up out of the vault on a dumbwaiter of brass and bone, and it was smaller than Dar had spent a day and a night expecting it to be.

The counting-annex lay behind the readings-room, down a hall of open arches, and the hall was the Registry in miniature: shelf after shelf of grey leaves in lead boxes, each box lettered and numbered in the vertical counting-house hand, each leaf somebody's pressed and filed remainder. Orlo walked the aisle with the lamplight bending after him, and stopped at a box marked with the route's own filing code, and set it on the counter, and opened it, and there was Ashkarad — not the city, the city's paper, the deposit-copies of everything the city's one carrier had ever spent.

The index leaf lay on top. MEHRAN, DARIUS, it read, in entries as clean as surgery notes, and Dar read his own account down the page like a man reading his autopsy.

MOTHER — FACE. FATHER — LAUGH. FIRST KISS. HOME — COLOR. PATIENT, FIRST — LOSS. The last of those had a second notation beside it, in smaller script: SPENT ONCE — READING, BRACK — and was priced here all over again. Everything you paid the Registry, Dar saw, could be charged to you twice: once to spend it, once to look at having spent it.

Five entries. Five withdrawals from the only account he had ever been afraid to overdraw, each one pressed out of him by fire or by his own hand at the moment he had needed the credit most, and each one priced now in the house's denomination, in the margin, in Orlo's vertical script. The patient was priced at one true memory, small. The first kiss was priced higher. The laugh was priced the same as the patient. The face — and he knew which entry the face was, because his eye went to it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth, MOTHER — FACE, two words, no date, the only entry on the page that had been written in the black bone pen rather than the registrar's chalk — the face was priced at a decade.

"A decade of a life," Orlo said, following his eye with the serenity of a man quoting a wall-chart. "The house prices memories in memories, carrier. The face is worth ten years of living, pressed at the standard rate. No one pays it twice."

"I don't want to buy it," Dar said. "Not yet. I want a playing. The laugh."

"A playing." Orlo turned the index leaf over, found the tariff, recited it flat. "The house lends the deposit into your senses. You will hold it as if it were yours, because for the term of the loan it is. Eleven heartbeats for the small rate. One true memory pays. Small and whole," he added, and the phrase had worn a groove in him, "and yours."

"I know the rate."

"I don't think you do," Orlo said, without heat. "But the press will teach you. It always does."

The cradle waited by the press like a pair of cupped hands. Suri stood at the end of the counter with her arms folded over the ruined strap of her graft, and Vell stood by the door with her slate held against her chest the way other people hold prayers, and Dar put his hands into the velum rim and went hunting through his own architecture for the thing he intended to spend.

He offered the woman in the salt garden.

It was the oldest piece of somebody else he owned — the memory bleed from the first weeks, before he had ever heard the word Gardener: rows of sleepers planted like bulbs, the tower with its windows lit like heartbeats, and the woman in white gloves moving down the rows and humming, and the hum so much his mother's lullaby that for a year he had not been able to tell whose memory it actually was. It was a real memory. It had weight, and texture, and a morning's worth of light in it. He held it whole in the front of his mind, the way Orlo had taught the paying, and felt the press descend.

The press refused it.

There was no violence in the refusal; there was something worse, a bureaucratic convulsion. The brass screw stopped. From the counter's flank the little printer coughed, and a slip came out, and Dar read it upside down before Orlo could turn it.

RHYTHM UNRESOLVED. NOT NATIVE TO ACCOUNT. FILING FAULT.

And beneath that, in a second strike, harder, the way a stamp comes down when the clerk is insulted: COUNTERFEIT — ALREADY FILED.

Orlo took the slip, read it, and slid it across the counter with two fingers, and for the first time since the spar-stair his face lost its priced serenity and became what it had probably been before the pricing, which was careful.

"You tried to pay with another person's morning," he said. "The house knows its own paper, carrier. That memory is on deposit somewhere already — pressed, filed, owned. You cannot spend what was only ever lent to you. If it had gone through, you would have paid your debt with counterfeit and the house would have collected the difference out of the rest of you." He let that sit. "Now. A true memory. Yours. Small."

Dar stood in the cradle with his hands in the velum rim and did the one thing he had promised himself in the dark of the dive-house that he would not do, which was to think about it first. Thinking about it was how you chose the cheap thing, and there was no cheap thing left that was true. His inventory ran itself the way the Nerve Vein ran the city: the Saltstairs stairwell, the fight pit, the barge, the cistern, Vahid's chalk ladder, Ilay's copper head bent over charcoal, the roof in the first storm, the seawalls opening — and then the mornings, because he had a trader's instinct for the exchange rate even now, even here.

There was one morning that was his. The first one. The clinic's first morning, three days after the Blacktide, when the silence was still so loud it had a texture, and he had unlocked the door of the salt-rotted chandlery off the Saltstairs stair and put Vahid's chalk ladder on the wall and the one chair by the table, and nobody had come for an hour, and then everybody had come for the rest of his life. Suri had counted the floor tiles out loud, forty-one, because the counting was hers and no one could repossess it. Ilay had sat in the corner drawing spirals that tightened inward, room by room, like a search that was also a grief. The light had come through the high barred window the color of dirty pewter, and it had been, against every probability the city offered, an unpriced morning, the first one he had ever owned.

He paid it.

The press took it room by room. That was the cruelty of the mechanism — it did not take the whole at once; it took the door, then the stair, then the chalk, then the light, and each taking was clean, a tooth coming out whole, and Dar stood in the cradle with his eyes open and filed nothing, because there was nothing left in him that could object loudly enough. The morning left the way the tide had left the flats. Gradually. Then all at once. Then not at all.

The receipt came out of the counter's flank: RECEIVED — ONE (1) MORNING, TRUE. CONDITION: WHOLE. FILED.

"There is a piece of that in me too," Suri said. Her voice did not rise; it did the opposite of rising, it went down into its flattest register, the one she used for totals. "Forty-one tiles, Mehran. I counted them. That was mine as much as yours." A pause, exactly as long as a ledger line. "You didn't ask."

"I know," Dar said. "I'm not going to pretend that makes it clean. It makes it spent, and spent is the only tense the Registry keeps."

Orlo drew the deposit-box across the counter and opened the index to the second entry and lifted out a leaf no bigger than his palm, and set it in the reading-tray, and said, "Eleven heartbeats. The house counts."

Suri's hand was on Dar's wrist before he touched the leaf. Two fingers, the counting position, her eyes on his pulse rather than his face, because someone in the room had to keep the books and he was about to be away.

The laugh came back into him like a lamp being lit in a swept room.

His father's laugh. The whole of it — not the sound alone, the sound is the least of a laugh — the doorway he had stood in, the coat over the chair, the big hands mid-gesture with a bottle and a story, the sense a child has that the largest thing in the house is amused, and the laugh itself, rolling, unhurried, a laugh that had never once in its life been performed. For eleven heartbeats it was his, and he stood inside it, a grown man with two hearts, ten years of surgeries and one spent childhood, and listened.

And inside the laugh — under it, woven into it the way a second voice is woven into a round, so quiet he would have missed it if the Registry's press had not pressed the morning whole, nothing skimmed, nothing left out — a woman was humming.

The lullaby.

The laugh withdrew on the eleventh heartbeat, exactly as billed. The leaf went back into its slot. The room was the room again, lamps and lead and the tick of the counter, and Dar stood with Suri's fingers still on his wrist and did the arithmetic that the deposit had just made unavoidable, entry by entry, in the vertical hand of his own mind.

His father had been a clerk of the seawall works who could not carry a tune and had never, in any memory Dar retained — in any memory the Registry still held — sung, hummed, whistled, or kept company with anyone who did. His father's world was tide-tables and pint measures and the names of other men's boats. The lullaby did not live in that world. It could not have entered it by itself.

It entered it carried. Sung where he could hear. Night after night, in the rooms above the clerk's books, close enough and constant enough to soak into the walls of an ordinary evening, into the coat on the chair, into the pause before a laugh — his mother's song, present in his father's morning the way salt is present in everything the sea has touched.

"That song is not from here," Dar said. He was not aware he had said it aloud until the room answered.

The answer was not words. She never spent words. But the hum rose in his bones, in the marrow the second Vein had made dense as coral — the lullaby's opening bars, felt not heard, keyed to the exact phrase that had been woven under his father's laugh — and she held the denial inside the music itself, a flat sustained tone under the melody, the sound a ledger makes when a column refuses to total. No, she said, in the only voice she had. Not from here.

His mother had sung the Gardener's song in a seawall clerk's rooms above the works, a lifetime before the Blacktide, before the Choratorium, before the seed in his chest had ever heard of Darius Mehran. The Registry had just shown him the receipt.

Suri opened her ledger against the counter and wrote, in her flat bookkeeper's hand, one line under the day's column, and turned the page so he could see it filed: THE CLINIC'S FIRST MORNING — SPENT. WORTH IT: UNKNOWN. KEEP COUNTING.

"Come away from the counter," she said. "You've bought everything they're selling today."
