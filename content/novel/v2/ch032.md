# Chapter 32 — Petal Against Glass

The last circle of Ilay's map lay behind them at six hundred and eighty steps, and everything past it was territory no one had drawn — forty-one steps of corridor where the tide in the walls made seeing useless and counting a courtesy. They had crossed it the way you cross the last stretch before a sickroom: slowly, lamps low, the tincture of hawthorn and salt-wort stoppered in Dar's coat like an argument he had promised to keep on hand. Now they stood in the chamber of the door, and the door stood at its end, and the petal did not move for a long time, and neither did they.

It lay against the far side of the salt-glass like a hand pressed flat to a window from inside a lit room — wide as Dar's palm, black as the underside of the tongue, veined in lace that was fine as the ledger-work on a first-edition debt-bond. Level with his chest. Level, exactly, with the place where his own second heart kept its books, and he had been a surgeon long enough to know that nothing in nature is level by accident. She had measured him through the dark. She knew where he kept her.

"Eleven steps down and it's colder than the First Vessel's floor," Suri said quietly. She had not touched her count since the door; her fingers were still curled closed around the number she had been robbed of. "It's not cold, though. Is it. It's *held.* Like breath held by something that never learned it had to stop."

The petal began to tap.

Soft at first — the tip of it against the glass, once, twice, a sound like a fingernail on the skin of frozen water — and then it settled, and the settling was the horror and the miracle both, because the rhythm was not random and it was not strange. Dar knew it the way you know your own stairwell in the dark. Three notes up, one hanging pause, two notes down, and the pause again: the opening bars of a lullaby in a language understood without hearing, the song the woman in white gloves had hummed over a garden of planted sleepers, the song Vey had hummed across a dining table between two cups, the song the dead First Singer had answered him with through a hundred feet of salt. The song he had spent his mother's face to pay for and could no longer say whose it was first. It came through the glass in taps, patient, small, unbearably careful — the way a debtor knocks at a relative's door. Dar stood with his hand not yet raised and his pulse knocking back, and let the song finish its first phrase, because it was also his — that was the part the Mind Vein had taught him and the cellar weeks had made him price: the lullaby did not come to him from outside. It had lived in him longer than the Lily had. It had survived the spending of four rooms. It hummed at the bottom of his swept halls with its ownership disputed, and every claimant he had ever identified for it was dead, mad, or dissolved into black lace on an altar rail.

"It knows the song," Suri said. Her voice stayed flat. Her knuckles did not. "The one you hum when you think you're asleep."

*One of us learned it from the other,* Dar thought, and could not for his life have said which one that sentence meant.

His chest answered before he did.

The Lily had been leaning for a week — toward the deep, toward the dark, her whole rooted weight tipping like cargo in a swell — but leaning was talking; this was *singing.* The hum rose through his ribs from the roots she had laid along his sternum, and it was the lullaby, the same three notes up, the same hanging pause, and it met the tapping through the glass and fit it — not echoed it, not answered it, *fit* it, the way two column-figures fit when the ledger finally reconciles, line against line, entry against entry, a reconciliation a century in arrears closing itself with a sound like a long-held breath let go.

The frost on the door bloomed.

It came up the salt-glass in whorls and petals, grey lace climbing in the shape of five-petaled flowers, and the flowers did not open toward them but outward — through, he realized; the pattern was one pattern seen from both sides, her frost and his, the same design pressed between two panes of the world. The tapping quickened. The tide of the heartbeat behind the glass swelled, and for the first time since the canal, since the barge, since the altar, Dar felt the thing beneath the city not as weather and not as a creditor but as what the word he had never said aloud had always claimed: a sister. Real. Alive. Aware — aware of *him*, because the taps had stopped matching the song and started matching his pulse, landing on his offbeats now, her one-behind rhythm threading through his own like a figure entered one line down, and when he exhaled, the tapping paused. When he breathed again, it resumed.

She was listening to him breathe. She had been listening for a hundred years.

Suri stepped back — one step, exact, her boot finding old salt without a sound — and said, in the voice she used for figures that frightened her, "That's not a machine."

"No," Dar said. "It isn't."

The Lily pressed.

He felt it as forward lean gone to forward *need*, the whole black weight of her in his chest climbing toward the glass the way water climbs toward the hole in a hull, and her hum rose toward the octave where the tapping lived, and the petal flattened wider against the door, and the frost-flowers raced, and every instinct the Veins had ever put in his body said: *open it. Open it now, while the song is right, before the song is wrong.* The dark behind the glass was warm as a body. The gap he could not see was audible — the door, he understood with a lurch, was not sealed; it breathed at its edges; it had breath to breathe.

Dar put his hand flat on the salt-glass, and everything stopped.

The tapping stopped. The frost stopped mid-climb. The hum in his chest hung on its note like a struck bell forbidden to decay, and behind the glass the vast slow tide of the heartbeat went quiet, one beat, two beats, three — the silence of an animal that has heard the door of the cage click and does not yet know what the hand intends.

He had refused the Throne twice. He knew the anatomy of refusal by now, the way he knew the anatomy of a wound: where it hurts, where it tears, what it costs. At the Blacktide she had offered him the salt garden itself — rows of planted sleepers, a tower of lantern heartbeats, rest without end — and he had said no, and she had said, in the first words she had ever spent on him, *then carry me a little further*, and he had carried her, and the carrying had brought them here. This was not that refusal. This was the other discipline, the older one, the one they had never needed to teach him in any hospital because Ashkarad taught it free with every cup of soup sold on credit: *you do not corner the thing you came to meet.* She had knocked. She had sung. She had never, in a hundred years, opened — and a creature that lives alone behind an unlocked door for a century is not preserved by the lock. She was preserved by the fear. The door stood ajar because she could not make herself open it, and if he opened it for her, the opening would be his act and not hers, a transaction she had not signed, and he had built everything he was now on a single refusal to push paper under other people's hands.

"Not yet," he said. Not to Suri. To the glass, to the dark, to the listening — and the Lily's hum bent against the word, but she had yielded to him before, twice, at prices that had nearly ended him, and she yielded now, the need easing out of his ribs like a tide respecting a seawall. *Then carry me a little further,* she had said once, and he had, and here they were, a little further.

The petal hesitated. Then it withdrew.

It peeled off the glass the way a hand withdraws from a table when the deal is not yet signed — slowly at first, politely, then quickly, gone — sliding back into the dark like lace drawn off a mirror, and the last black edge of it took the frost with it, and the door stood empty and shining and dumb.

And where the petal had been pressed, the keyhole showed.

It was cut through the salt-glass like a flaw cut deliberately, and it was not a keyhole in any locksmith's grammar: it was a five-petaled flower, small, exact, the sigil from the registry page, the sigil off the syndicate banners, the chalk mark of the census, blooming through a crack in the door of the world. The edge of the door nearest it stood a hair's-width ajar. Air moved through the gap — warm, slow, breathing — carrying a smell that had no business being warm or slow a hundred fathoms under a drowned city: loam. Living loam, and beneath the loam, faint as a signature under a signature, the ghost of a lullaby sung a long time ago to soil.

"It was never locked," Suri said. Her count stayed closed in her fist. "The whole way down — the warning on the landing, the margin, *do not count the heartbeats* — and it was never locked."

"It was locked from the far side," Dar said. "The lock is a decision. She made it every day for a hundred years." He took his hand off the glass. His palm-print stayed in the frost a moment, five-fingered, beside the flower, the two marks together like a page from a ledger no one would ever file. "She wants to be found. And she is afraid of being found. Both entries are true, and neither cancels the other."

They stood a moment longer in the ringing dark, two small balances on seven hundred and eleven steps of salt, while the tide behind the glass resumed — patient, immense, one beat behind him, following — and the warm air breathed through the gap against his chest like a question asked at a door by someone who could not make herself knock again.

The climb up was seven hundred and eleven steps out, and Suri counted every one of them aloud, and the vaults rang her count back a quarter-tone flat, and Dar did not correct her, and the corrected rhythm — his steps, her numbers, the deep following one beat behind — carried them up through the dead First Vessel's cathedral and past the racked stamps and out into air that tasted of iron and salt and ordinary smoke. Dawn was a rumor over the storm-walls. The city above went on defaulting and enduring and being counted by whoever still bothered to count it.

And above all of it, on an attic desk in the Saltstairs, folded in a sterile wrap under a dead lamp, the page waited: eight names, one sigil, one circled city, one word. *RECLAIM.* The sister had been the question. Dar was beginning to understand that the Gardener was the answer, and answers, in Ashkarad, arrived with their invoices already attached.
