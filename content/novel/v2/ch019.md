# Chapter 19 — The Tithe-Clerk's Day

Milla Ferun's fingers were always cold. They had to be. Cold fingers didn't tremble when the pulse-reader whined, didn't sweat when the debtor's pulse thinned under the clamp, didn't leave damp prints on the clean white forms that were the only things in the Marrowworks never permitted to smudge. Today they were colder than usual, the air from the vents carrying salt and the faint, sweet scent of preserved tissue — a smell she had long ago stopped naming, the way you stop noticing a weight carried daily. She stamped another form — T-7B, lung lobe, standard tithe — and slid it into the growing pile, where it settled against its fellows with the soft obedience of paper. The quota for the day was thirty-three hearts. She'd processed twenty-seven. Six more to go.

She knew the six the way she knew everything in this room: not as people, but as line items awaiting their stamps.

"Morning, Milla," said Elara, dropping a stack of fresh forms on her desk. The ink still smelled wet. "Saltstairs shipment coming in at noon. Better clear space."

Milla nodded and began arranging her stamps in their designated slots, an order she could have kept blind, in her sleep. The Heart-Stamp. The Debt-Stamp. The Voluntary-Stamp. Each one made a different sound when it hit the paper, and she had come to know those sounds the way a priest knows bells. The Heart-Stamp was the heaviest, a solid thud that felt final, the sound of an account being closed in a body. The Debt-Stamp was lighter, a dry click, because debt was never finished with anyone and the stamp knew it. The Voluntary-Stamp was the lightest of all — barely a whisper — which had always struck her as the machine's one piece of honesty. She'd started counting the sounds at the beginning of the month, because counting was the only prayer the Chorus had left her. Twelve thousand, seven hundred and forty-three. That was how many hearts she'd stamped in her five years as a tithe-clerk. She knew the number as well as she knew the sound of her own pulse, and she was no longer certain which of the two she trusted less.

The clinic was a long, white room with rows of examining tables where people lay still as sacrifices, sheeted to the chin, their arms turned palm-up as if in surrender or appeal. Beyond the glass wall, the nurses moved with practiced efficiency, their gloved hands disappearing into body cavities and emerging with organs nestled in coolers, each cooler labeled, each label correct, everything audited down to the milliliter. Milla never looked directly at the tables. She looked at the forms. Forms were clean. Forms had names and debt ledgers and next of kin. Forms were people before they became inventory — people condensed, refined, reduced to their salable parts and their outstanding balances. A form never screamed. A form never asked what would become of its sister's lung operation. A form was the story the Chorus preferred to read.

"Name's Kaelen," said the man sliding into the chair opposite her desk. He was young, maybe nineteen, with the hollow cheeks of someone who'd already sold something valuable, and he sat the way debtors sat — spine straight out of principle, hands still out of terror. "Here for the heart tithe."

Milla took his census chit, the small brass disc that held all his information, warm from his pocket and warmer for it. She slotted it into the reader, and the machine did what the machine always did: it made him small. His face appeared on the little screen, then his debt ledger, and there it was, the number the Chorus had spent nineteen years arranging. Eighteen thousand, four hundred and ninety-seven marks. For his sister's lung operation. The system was designed to be inescapable; she had understood that on her first day, and five years of stamps had only deepened the understanding. A debt paid with blood was a debt that grew. The body shrank, the ledger swelled, and the difference was called interest.

"Standard procedure," Milla said, her voice flat as the page in front of her. She slid a consent form across the table. "Read section seven. The one about the grace period."

Kaelen's eyes were wide. "They said... they said I wouldn't feel it. The withdrawal."

Milla's fingers paused over the Voluntary-Stamp. It was the closest thing to hesitation her hands were still permitted, and she spent it here, every time, a thirty-second tax she levied against the system on the behalf of no one. "The heart doesn't hurt when it's gone," she said, which was true. "What hurts is the living without it." She stamped the form. The whisper of the Voluntary-Stamp was very loud in the little office. "Table seven. Nurse Jory will prep you."

As he walked away, Milla made a small note in her ledger: *Kaelen, L. — Heart Tithed — Grace Period: 72 hours.* She knew what would happen, because the form told her and because the form had never once been wrong. In seventy-two hours, the grace period would expire, and his debt would be recalculated based on his new, diminished capacity to work — a heart shy of the labor the number assumed, so the number would grow to cover the difference. He'd come back. People always came back. The Chorus was patient. They had to be. A garden couldn't be rushed.

The afternoon bled into evening, the light through the high windows going from white to grey to the color of old bone. The last shipment from the Saltstairs arrived — a dozen debtors chained together at the wrists, their faces grey with resignation, some of them still carrying the salt of the storm in their hair. Milla processed them quickly, her stamps clicking like a metronome, and the metronome kept time for a room that had no other music. She didn't learn their names. Names were dangerous. They made you imagine them as people who had lives outside this room — meals, arguments, someone waiting up — and imagination was the one expense the Chorus had never figured out how to deduct from her wages. She gave them numbers instead. The numbers behaved.

When the last form was stamped and filed, Milla stretched her cold fingers, and the small motion felt like insubordination. Her supervisor, a man named Borin whose eyes were as flat as the clinic's polished floors, approached her desk without hurrying, because nothing in this building ever needed to hurry.

"Milla," he said, not looking at her. "A pulse-printout came in from the Saltstairs. Flagged RHYTHM UNRESOLVED. File it with the anomalies. And lose it."

Milla's stomach tightened, an old reflex from a part of her that had not signed anything. Anomalies were bad. They were things the Chorus didn't like, things that didn't fit the neat patterns of their ledgers — and patterns were all the Chorus loved. "What's wrong with it?"

"None of your concern," Borin said. "Just make it disappear. Permanently."

He handed her a thin strip of paper covered in jagged, irregular lines. Most pulse-readouts were smooth, predictable waves — the Chorus's own harmony, written out in grey. This one was a mess of spikes and dips, like a heart trying to find a rhythm and failing, or like a heart trying to carry two rhythms at once and refusing to drop either. Milla took it, her fingers brushing Borin's as the paper passed between them. His skin was cold, too. Everything in the Marrowworks was cold. She had once thought that was the refrigeration.

After Borin left, Milla sat at her desk for a long time, staring at the printout while the lamps hummed and the building counted something in the walls. She could just file it, as instructed. The chute was ten steps away; she had made the walk a thousand times. Or she could do what she sometimes did when the weight of the ledgers became too much — she could make a copy. It was a small crime, the smallest she knew: a second copy of a truth the Chorus wanted unrecorded. She slipped a blank sheet of paper into the clinic's old thermal copier, fed the printout through, and watched the jagged lines appear in grey on the new sheet, like a storm translated into language. Then she took the original and fed it into the disposal chute, where it would be incinerated along with the biological waste — the Chorus filed truth and flesh in the same furnace.

She folded the copy and slid it into her pocket, against her hip, where it warmed to her temperature. It was a small thing, a tiny act of defiance that would be forgotten by morning. But it was hers. She had entered it into no ledger, and that made it the only thing in the building she truly owned.

On her way out, Milla passed the nursery wing. The doors were always closed, but she sometimes heard singing behind them, faint and beautiful, small voices in close harmony, drilled and lovely and wrong. She never stopped to listen. It was better not to know what they were growing in there. Better for the report she would have to write about herself, at the end of every day, in the ledger she kept behind her eyes.

The walk to her apartment in the Saltstairs was a descent into fog. The salt storm had begun, a thick white mist that swallowed the upper levels and left the streets below in a perpetual twilight, lamps haloed, lines softened, the whole district reduced to suggestions. Milla pulled her coat tight, the cold seeping through the fabric with the patience of interest accruing. The storm was called a "grace" by those who didn't have to breathe it. For the poor it was just another way the city tried to kill you slowly — politely, in installments, with your signature on file.

Her apartment was small and damp, with a single window that looked out onto a brick wall so close she could have counted its courses by touch. She lit a lamp, its flame casting long shadows that danced on the ceiling, and took the pulse-printout from her pocket and spread it on the table, weighting the corners with two cups and a tin spoon. The rhythm was strange. If she read it not as a single pulse but as a sequence, the pattern began to emerge: five spikes, then a dip, then five more spikes. She read it again. It held. It was a shape. A five-petaled flower, drawn in jagged lines, blooming out of a stranger's heartbeat.

Milla traced the pattern with her finger, and the chill in her fingertips felt suddenly like recognition. It was the same flower she sometimes saw in the margins of her ledgers, a small thing she drew when she was tired of counting hearts — five petals, a center, always the same, arriving on the page without her permission. She'd never known why she drew it. She had assumed it was a habit, and habits, in her experience, were just debts the mind paid automatically. Now, looking at the pulse-printout, it seemed less like a coincidence and more like a message — a form the city had filed against her, in a hand she didn't know, and the signature was her own.

That night, Milla dreamed. She was in a garden made of salt, the ground crunching beneath her feet like old porcelain, and the air was thick with the scent of something sweet and metallic. In the distance, rows of people lay sleeping on stone tables, their chests rising and falling in perfect unison, an entire ward breathing to a single metronome. Above them, a figure in a white robe hummed a low, tuneless song. The figure turned, and Milla saw that its face was smooth and blank, like a porcelain doll's — a face with no entries on it at all. It smiled, and the smile was too wide, stretching across the smooth face in a way that wasn't possible, and she understood, with dream-flat certainty, that it was smiling at a number.

Milla tried to scream, but no sound came out. She tried to run, but her feet were rooted to the salt ground, planted like bulbs in a bed she had not chosen. The figure hummed louder, and the sleeping figures began to stir. Their eyes opened, all at once, and they all looked at her.

Milla woke with a start, her heart pounding against her ribs — one heart, one rhythm, she checked, she always checked now. She sat up in bed, gasping for air. It was dark, but she could see her hand clearly where it lay on the blanket, pale against the weave. On her fingertips was a fine dust, white and chalky. Salt. She rubbed her fingers together, but the dust wouldn't come off. It was under her nails, in the creases of her skin. It was in her dreams.
