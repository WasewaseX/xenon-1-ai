# Chapter 29 — The Ninth Petal

The Saint Ferrous archive kept its census clerks the way it kept everything: filed, and lying about it.

Milla Ferun had been billeted there for eleven days — reassigned, the posting said, from the Marrowworks to a *reconciliation detail*, which meant eleven cots between the shelves and a work quota of pages re-copied from memory, because the halls had burned and the Index wanted the city's books rebuilt by the people who had kept them. She was good at the work. That was the indictment of her whole life: she was good at the work. Give her a ledger and her cold fingers would make it true, and the truest thing she had ever copied was a strip of pulse-printout she had stolen one evening five years into stamping hearts, and she had sewn it into her coat lining the same night, against her hip, where it had warmed to her temperature ever since.

The raid came at the third bell, which told her the raiders had read the archive's own shift-logs, and she was awake for it because she had stopped sleeping much. Boots in the stacks — not the Warden shuffle, the Index walk, in step, economical — and lamplight in lines under the shelf-doors, and a voice reading names off a list with the mild, terrible patience of a man serving notices: clerks, out, in order, by rank of access. Hostage-taking, but hostage-taking with paperwork; the Index was conscripting every clerk who could rebuild a census, and a census could not be built by volunteers.

She heard the terms through the stacks, delivered to the assembled clerks in the tone of a man doing them a favor: their debts were void, their billets were comfortable, their families would be found and reassured. Only their memories were required — forty years of stamps, filed and re-filed — and their signatures on new rolls. The Archive was to become an Index annex. The clerks were to become its hands. Refusal was not stated as punished; it was stated as *unavailable*, which was how the worst things in Ashkarad had always been priced. Milla listened to her colleagues agree, one by one, in the small relieved voices of people being permitted to keep doing the only work they knew, and understood that this was how the Choir had been built too — not out of monsters, but out of clerks reconciling themselves, page by page, to the latest set of terms.

Milla's fingers did not tremble. That was what they were for. She counted the boots past her shelf — eight, then two more at the end room where Elara slept; she heard Elara's name read, and Elara's voice answer, obedient, clerk-trained, and the sound of her colleague's compliance was the last cost she needed entered. Milla lay still on her cot in the dark of the stacks and made the only transaction her life had been preparing her for: she defaulted. She had never once in her life failed to appear on a roster. She committed the omission the way other people committed crimes — thoroughly, in advance, with the paperwork of her own disappearance already filed in her head.

She had heard the bounty on the sixth day. The Index men talked over the copy-desk the way clerks talk, in figures, and one of them had named the figure: a season's wages for a single sheet, flagged RHYTHM UNRESOLVED, anomaly class, origin Saltstairs — *the Ferun printout.* She had heard her own surname in a bounty the way you hear a death notice for someone who shares your street, and she had understood two things at once: that the paper against her hip had become the most expensive thing in the city, and that she was the only person alive who knew where it slept.

She rose, sewed coat, boots in hand, and went out through the coal chute that the archive's fire-detail used for ash, because she had counted the guard rotations on her first day — it was not mistrust, it was habit, habit was all she had ever kept — and she dropped into the yard and walked into the Saltstairs fog with the most wanted paper in Ashkarad warming her hip.

The porcelain face came with her, the way it always did now. It had followed her out of the dream years ago and never quite left: smooth, blank, smiling too wide, smiling at a number. In the dream it stood in the salt garden with its white-cuffed hands folded, and behind it the sleepers breathed in unison like a ward, and she had understood, with the flat dream-certainty she trusted more than most of her waking judgments, that the smile was the truest ledger she had ever been shown. She dreamed it less since the Blacktide. What she dreamed now was the flower.

The clinic had a card on its door. Milla had never seen the word *sanctuary* posted on any building in Ashkarad that wasn't being sold, and she stood in front of it in the grey of the morning reading the terms twice — both seals, no names, no prices — with the fixed attention she had once spent on consent forms, because she had spent her life watching buildings lie through their doors and this one, impossibly, appeared to be doing the opposite.

The door opened before she knocked. A woman with a ruined carbon graft on one arm looked at her, then at the coat she was holding closed with both hands like a wound.

"You're a clerk," the woman said. Not a question. "Marrowworks, by the stamp-callus. Index is paying by name for clerks tonight, and you walked here from the east, which means you're not selling."

"I'm buying," Milla said. Her voice came out flat as a filed page, which was her voice, always, except at the end of the sentence, where something cracked along a line she had not known was in her. "Sanctuary. I want to be uncounted. And I have something for the surgeon — for Mehran. He doesn't know me. It doesn't matter. It's his."

She unwound the coat lining in the ward, on the scarred table, under the lamp, with the courier woman watching and the surgeon coming across the room with the wariness of a man who has learned what arrives in his building unannounced.

Up close, Milla saw, the surgeon had two pulses. She had known it for five years — had read it first in a strip of thermal-grey paper, origin Saltstairs, flagged and ordered destroyed — and then the whole city had learned what her one stolen page already knew: the Saltstairs cutter with the black lily, the man who broke the Blacktide. The knowing had sat in her like a second ledger ever since, unfiled, accruing nothing. Now the knowing and the man were in the same room, and her cold fingers were steady, because they had been rehearsing this appointment for half a decade without ever daring to schedule it.

The printout was five years old and perfect. Thermal-grey, machine-crisp, the jagged lines unmistakable — she had read it a thousand times in her own room, weighting the corners with two cups and a tin spoon. At the top, the flag: RHYTHM UNRESOLVED. And beneath the flag, in the sequence she had been the first person in the city to see, the pattern: five spikes, a dip, five spikes. A two-rhythm pulse, layered, unresolved, refusing the machine's arithmetic. A heart carrying a second rhythm and declining to surrender either.

"That's mine," Dar said quietly. He did not touch the paper. "That's the first reader I ever practiced on. Before I knew what I was carrying. This is—" he stopped, and Milla watched a surgeon price an artifact in real time: a recording of his own chest from the earliest days, made before the Blacktide, before the Veins, before he had spent his mother's face to survive it. A document of what he had been before any of it. The only one.

"The Chorus original went into the incinerator the same night I copied it," Milla said. "My supervisor ordered it destroyed. I fed the furnace myself and kept the copy. It's the only print of that rhythm left in the world — I've told no one, until now. I'm telling you because there's a bounty on it. They're calling it the Ferun printout." She heard her own voice continue with the detachment of a clerk reading another clerk's file. "I'm the Ferun in question. They priced me before I even knew I was for sale. I'd like, at least, to pick the buyer."

Suri had not blinked through any of it. Now she said, "The Index pays in futures, not coin. If you'd sold, you'd have been paid in a posting somewhere far away and a grudge that compounds. This was better bargained."

"Why bring it to me?" Dar asked. "You could have drowned it. Ash is cheap."

Milla looked at the table. Then she took out her pocket ledger — her own book, the one she kept behind her eyes made flesh, five years of margin-flower drawings — and opened it to the last page, and turned it around.

"I draw the flower," she said. "In the margins, when the counting gets heavy. In my sleep, lately, which is worse — sleep is the one room I never signed anything in. Five petals. Always five. It took me years to admit the five was a pattern and not a tic, and longer to admit the pattern wasn't mine." The page in her ledger was last night's drawing, made in sleep, in a hand that was hers and steadier than her waking hand ever was: a flower, centered, symmetrical, its petals drawn with the assurance of anatomy. "Last night it had nine."

The ward was quiet. Somewhere above, a patient's cough; the ordinary noise of the sanctuary doing its work.

"Nine," Dar said. His hand was flat on the table by the printout, and Milla — who had spent five years watching pulse-lines for a living — saw the second rhythm in him flicker under the skin of his wrist, one beat out of step, like a neighbor lighting a lamp.

"The garden is changing its filing system," she said, and heard the absurdity and let it stand, because there was no accurate way to say it. "Or it's counting something new."

Dar folded the printout along its old creases and put it inside his coat, with whatever else he kept next to his heart, and Milla realized with a small cold shock that she had just handed over the only valuable thing she would ever own and felt lighter for it, which the ledgers had no column for.

The courier woman showed her a cot, a shelf, the terms of the sanctuary spoken plainly: "You work the front desk. You're good at figures — I can tell by your hands. You count for us now. Freely." The word *freely* was said like a coin being set down.

Milla sat on the cot in the sanctuary of strangers, and did the last audit of her old life: salt on her fingers, fine and white and chalky, worked in under the nails — though she had not been underground in weeks, had not touched the Marrowworks since the fall, had not been within a mile of the salt gardens or the shafts. She rubbed at it. It did not come off. It smelled, faintly, sweetly, like a garden waiting for the right kind of weather.

She put her hands in her lap where she could watch them, and counted her own pulse until the counting was a lullaby, and waited to see what the flower would draw tonight.
