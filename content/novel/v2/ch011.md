# Chapter 11 — The Girl Who Heard the Singing

The bell tolled again, deeper this time, a vibration that traveled through the canal-works' iron supports and into Dar's bones. It didn't ring so much as accumulate, one bronze note laid over another the way arrears are laid over a name in a lender's book. Somewhere under his sternum, the Lily turned in its sleep and counted every note like interest coming due. Suri's hand tightened around his wrist, her carbon scales pressing into his skin like cool coins. "They know," she whispered, her breath fogging in the damp air. "They know we have the registry."

Dar didn't answer. Answering would have meant deciding what to be afraid of first, and there were too many candidates for the position. His focus was elsewhere — on the small bundle of rags they'd pulled from the pump vanes twenty minutes ago. A child, no older than nine, with eyes the color of river stones and hair the texture of spun copper. She hadn't spoken since they'd found her, hadn't even flinched when Suri had lifted her from the canal's oily embrace, as if being taken out of the water had been nothing more than a change of rooms. Now she sat huddled in the corner of their hideout, a converted cistern beneath the Saltstairs, tracing patterns in the condensation on the wall with a grimy fingernail. Spirals, Dar noticed. Circles inside circles, each one smaller than the last, like a sound being swallowed.

"Her name's Ilay," Dar said, finally looking at Suri. "She was in the registry. Perfect vessel. Reserved for the Throne."

He hated how the page had rendered her: not a name, not a child — an entry, a grade of tissue, a column headed disposition, all of it in the cramped, contented hand of an accountant who had never been required to look at what he counted.

Suri's gaze shifted to the child. "The Chorus lost their prize. That's more dangerous than us having a page." She knelt, keeping a careful distance, the way you kneel near a bird with a broken wing. "How long have you been hiding here, little thistle?"

Ilay looked up, not at Suri, but at the ceiling, where a single naked bulb flickered weakly, buzzing to itself like an insect trapped in glass. "Since the singing stopped," she said, her voice thin as ice over a drain. "In the walls. They called me the garden's daughter."

Dar felt a cold dread seep into his chest, a familiar ache that wasn't his gravel-heart but the Lily responding to something. It pressed outward against his ribs, attentive, the way a sleeper turns toward distant music. The garden's daughter. That was what the choir organs had called him in his dreams — before he'd known about Ilay. Before he'd known about siblings.

The hideout smelled of damp stone, old oil, and something vaguely sweet that Suri called "heat-berries" — small, bioluminescent fungi that grew in the city's warmer pipes, glowing a faint bruised amber along the seam where wall met floor. Dar had rigged a small burner to boil water, and now he watched the steam curl toward the ceiling, feeling detached from his own body, the way he sometimes felt over an open chest cavity: present, essential, and somehow not entirely in the room. The Breath Vein was still active, a constant hum beneath his skin, but he'd learned to control it, to separate himself from the need for air. It was like being underwater without the water, a quiet space inside himself where he could think. He kept returning to it tonight. The alternative was to feel everything at once.

Suri was counting again, her fingers tapping against her thigh. He'd noticed it before — she counted the way other people prayed. "Three days before the salt-moon eclipse. Fourteen days since the Lily took root. Sixty-seven heartbeats since the bell tolled first." She looked at Dar, and in the bruised amber light her eyes looked like they'd been billed for everything they'd ever seen. "The Chorus won't wait. They'll tear the Saltstairs apart brick by brick to find her."

Ilay sat on a crate, watching them with unnerving stillness. Children weren't supposed to be still like that. This one sat like a ledger waiting to be audited. "They'll find me anyway," she said. "The singing knows where I am."

Dar knelt beside her, keeping his movements slow, non-threatening, the way he approached a wound that might still be alive. "What singing, Ilay?"

"The singing in the walls," she repeated. "Before. When they took me away from the quiet room. It said I was the garden's daughter. That I had to go back to the garden."

"The garden," Dar murmured, thinking of the Umbra Lily, of its roots and petals and the black lace that had bloomed across his skin. "You mean the Choratorium?"

Ilay shook her head, her copper hair catching the dim light. "Deeper. Where the singing is loudest. Where the First Singer sleeps."

Dar's blood ran cold. The First Singer. Anra Vey's buried host. The thing beneath the Marrowworks that was being fed forty years of organs — forty years of debtors rendered down into monthly installments of meat. He'd read about it in Vahid's notes, seen the architectural schematics of the buried shaft that led down to it: numbers, load ratings, feed schedules, every figure annotated in that patient graveyard hand. But Ilay had never seen those schematics. She'd drawn it herself.

Two days later, Vahid arrived smelling of gin and old paper, his coat stained with what looked like ink and something darker. He'd been drinking, as usual, but his eyes sharpened when he saw Ilay, the way a lens sharpens when it finally finds its focal length.

"She's the one," he said, his voice rough as a file drawn across bone. "The registry said 'perfect vessel.' I didn't expect..."

He didn't finish. Ilay held up a piece of folded paper covered in crayon drawings. Vahid took it, unfolding it with the care of a man defusing something. His breath hitched.

The drawing showed a cross-section of the city, but not as any map Dar had ever seen. It showed layers — streets, pipes, foundations — stacked like the pages of a ledger, and at the bottom, a vast, bulbous shape surrounded by roots that reached up through the earth like grasping fingers, tapping every layer, holding every street the way a creditor holds collateral. Beside it, Ilay had drawn a series of concentric circles, each labeled with a number. Her numbers were neat, neater than any nine-year-old's had a right to be.

"The singing told me," Ilay said quietly. "The numbers are how many steps down. The circles are how loud it gets."

Vahid stared at the drawing, then at Ilay, then back at the drawing, as if the two were connected by a thread only he could see. "She's mapped the shaft," he said to Dar, awe and terror sharing his voice like a room neither wanted to pay for. "By ear. The girl who hears the singing has mapped the path to the First Singer."

Dar looked at Ilay, at the calm certainty in her eyes, and felt the ground shift beneath all his plans. This changes everything, he thought. Not just their escape, but everything. The Chorus wasn't just after him anymore. They were after her. And if they got her back, the ledger would balance itself with whatever was left of the city.

· · ·

That evening, Dar sat with Ilay in the corner of the hideout, sharing a piece of bread dipped in heat-berry jam, the jam leaving faint glowing smears on their fingers. Suri was on watch, her silhouette sharp against the doorway, lamplight breaking into blades on her scaled arm. Dar had his shirt unbuttoned, the Lily a faint pattern beneath his skin — black veins like roots spreading across his chest — and he had stopped pretending he didn't track their growth the way a debtor tracks a balance.

"Why do they call you the garden's daughter?" Dar asked softly.

Ilay took a small bite of bread, chewing with the deliberation of someone who had learned that food could be irregular. "Because I don't mind the dark. Because I like the singing. Because when they put me in the quiet room, I could still hear it through the walls. The garden wants to sing, but it's too deep. Too big." She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "It's lonely down there."

Dar thought of the Lily in his chest, of its patience, its curiosity. It wasn't evil. It just wanted to bloom. And Ilay was part of that. A sibling vessel. A perfect one. He felt the thing stir at the thought of her — a slow, warm bloom of attention that frightened him more than any hunger had, because hunger he understood, and this was closer to tenderness.

He looked at his hands — precise hands that had saved lives and now carried this thing inside him. Survival, then protection, then refusal. That was the shape his debts had drawn him into. He'd been surviving since the Lily took root. He'd started protecting Suri. Now, with Ilay, it was different. This was hope. Fragile, dangerous hope, but hope nonetheless — the most expensive thing a man in Ashkarad could carry, and the only thing no one could repossess.

· · ·

The knock came at dawn, sharp and deliberate. Three raps, then a pause, then three more. Suri was on her feet in an instant, carbon scales gleaming in the dim light, her whole body arranged around the knife at her belt like a sentence around its verb. "Chorus," she spat. "Too soon. Too clean."

Dar put a hand on her arm. "Wait."

The knock came again — two raps, this time. Ilay stirred where she slept, her eyes fluttering open, and for the first time since the pump vanes she looked afraid. "It's her," she whispered, sitting up. "The one who hums."

Dar exchanged a look with Suri. The one who hums. Anra Vey.

The door creaked open, not kicked or forced — an unhurried sound, in a city where doors were usually arguments. Standing in the doorway was Custodian Anra Vey, dressed in simple grey robes, her face unmasked, her hair pulled back in a neat silver bun. She held a lantern in one hand and a wicker basket in the other. No soldiers. No bone-plate champions. Just her, and a calm that seemed to absorb the room's tension the way sand absorbs blood.

"Good morning," she said, her voice warm as tea. "I do hope I'm not intruding. The bell must have woken you." She stepped inside, unhurried, and set the basket on the floor. From it, she produced a thermos and three ceramic cups — three, Dar noted, exactly three; she had counted them before she came — and poured with the steadiness of a surgeon. "I brought tea. Chamomile. Soothing for the nerves."

Suri's hand was still on her knife, but she didn't move. Dar stood between her and Anra Vey, his body a shield, the Lily pressing outward against his ribs as if it recognized the doorway. "What do you want, Custodian."

Anra Vey poured the tea, the steam curling around her like a living thing. "To talk," she said, offering Dar a cup. "And to offer a solution. A way out of this for all of you."

Dar didn't take the cup. "There's no way out. Not with her." He nodded at Ilay without looking at her, because looking would have been a concession.

Anra Vey's gaze shifted to the child, then back to Dar, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind her face — not cruelty, but appetite wearing cruelty's clothes. "The girl is safe with me. Safer than she is here, running from the very thing that will complete her." She took a sip of tea, her eyes never leaving his. "I can unmake the Lily, Dar Mehran. I can remove it from your chest and give you back your mortal heart. The gravel-heart that was failing you before. You'll be free of it. Free of the debt. Free of the bloom."

Dar felt the Lily stir in his chest, a pulse of fear. It knew. It always knew. And beneath his fear, shamefully, something in him leaned toward the offer like a starving man toward a lit window.

"All you have to do," Anra Vey continued, her voice soft as the steam rising from her cup, "is give me the perfect bloom. The girl. Ilay. She is the key. The garden's daughter. With her, the Ninth Vein is possible. Without her... well. We both know what happens then."

She set her cup down and looked at Dar, her expression one of gentle understanding — the face of a lender explaining that this is the last time, truly, the very last time. "The tea is poured, Dar. It's still warm. You just have to decide which cup to drink from."
