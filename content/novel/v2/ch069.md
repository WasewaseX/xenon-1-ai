# Chapter 69 — Sold in Bulk

The final push had a price list, and Suri wrote it on the clinic wall in her flat counted hand, because the first rule of funding anything in Ashkarad was to know what anything was.

The list read: vault watches, fed and paid through the ration, six hands a night, forty nights. Chorus-free doses, Vahid's distillation, materials the city no longer made and the League still carried — glass, spirit, silver salt, four kinds of wax. Lamp-oil for the deep, where oil was life and the darkness fed things that were not them. Grain against the ration's arithmetic, because the clinic's neutrality bought no one's hunger. And water, at Gavel's pump price, every day, for a house that had spent its coin four days ago retiring a courier and would spend it again the same way.

Against the list, in the second column, Suri had written the clinic's assets: one free clinic, one sanctuary census, one reputation. And then, because the column would not balance and she was not a woman who left a column unbalanced, she had added the only line that could close it, in smaller letters, as if the ink could be made responsible: HIS.

Dar read the wall and understood the accounting perfectly, because it was his own accounting read back to him by someone who had never once let him lie in it. The clinic had one asset left that the market priced. He had spent a mother's face and a father's laugh and a first kiss and the color of home to buy a war, and the war had not closed, and the registry was still buying.

"I know the exchange," he said. "I've used the counter at Lowmark. The press takes true currency, refuses the false, files what it takes. It cost me the smell of carbolic soap and a dead bride's last morning, and it bought me a look at the Gardener's hand." He rolled his sleeve. "I'm not selling the load-bearing walls. I'm selling furniture."

Suri looked at him with the expression of a woman auditing a smuggler's manifest. "Furniture," she said. "I'll be the judge of the furniture. You don't touch the list I keep. You bring me every slip before it goes, and I strike it from the ledger of you, and if the ledger of you says stop, we stop."

The League kept a counting-room by the tide-gates — a narrow brick office where caravan clerks billed storm-freight, and behind the counter, in a cage of brass bars, sat the thing Dar had crossed a drowned city to use: a press of bone and brass, small, the clear child of the mother-house machine in Lowmark, with a feeding slot and a print-tray and a clay basin underneath for the lees. The League sustained the route; the route was the Gardener's; every League office was a registry counter wearing freight like a coat. Dar had understood that for a year. He used it anyway. That was the trade, and he priced the trade and took the trade, and hated the trade, and the trade did not care.

Vell came with him the first morning, unasked, chalk in hand, because Vell of Brack had worked for a counting-house that sold to the registry and knew its grammar the way fish know water. She stood at the counter while the League clerk inked the docket, and she read the posted rates — rates that moved like tides, re-priced by the week against the fleet's approach — and she wrote on the counter's slate, in tall steady letters:

BRACK SOLD HERE TOO.

She underlined it twice. Then she wrote: I WILL COUNT YOUR SLIPS. NO ONE SHOULD SELL ALONE.

The first deposit was small, and true, and his: rain on the Saltstairs the night of his seventh birthday, drumming the cistern lids, his father's coat over his shoulders, the smell of wet wool. The press took it the way the room below took tithes — a settling, a click of weight accepted — and the clerk logged the catalog line in the docket, and the counterfoil came back printed, and the price of a night of rain on a cistern lid, at that week's rates, was enough to buy the clinic six days of water.

He stood in the counting-room a moment after, the way you stand after setting a bone, waiting for the hurt to file itself where hurts go. The rain-night was gone. Not dimmed — gone, the way the Mind Vein's spends were gone, except that the Mind Vein had stripped a room and this had taken a room entire and put a stranger's seal on it, and somewhere up a drowned coast a press had made a leaf of him and filed it under assets. The Lily shifted against his heart, once, like a sleeper turning at a door.

They pinned the counterfoil to the clinic wall. Then they pinned the next, and the next.

It went on for eleven days, and the wall filled, and the wall was the most terrible ledger Dar had ever seen, and he had seen the Choratorium's.

Deposit-slips, each priced and priced again — the registry re-priced its holdings upward as the fleet came, the way any market re-prices whatever is leaving it, so that every morning Suri walked the wall with her stick of wax and corrected the totals, and the totals only rose. The taste of storm-tea. The weight of a sleeping cat from his childhood street. The hour Vahid lectured sober, once, on the anatomy of the hyoid, for no reason anyone ever learned. The first time he held a retractor steady enough for a senior to stop watching. The green of the canal in spring. The sound of Milla's stamps, from the next room, on his first day at Saint Ferrous — he sold that one sitting down.

The patients read the wall the way the poor read anything priced in a window they cannot buy from: without expression, and completely. One of the freed vessels, a girl of twelve with graded blood and careful eyes, asked him what the slips were, and Dar, mid-stitch, gave her the only honest version. "They're days of mine," he said. "Sold so the house stays warm." She considered that with the gravity the nursery had taught her, and asked the question the nursery had also taught her, the one that priced everything: "What do you get, when you sell a day?" And Dar said, "Six days of water for eleven people," and the girl nodded slowly, filed it, and never asked again, because in Ashkarad the arithmetic had always been that honest, and that obscene.

Milla audited the wall on the fourth day, registrar to market, cold fingers flying down the catalog lines, and rendered her verdict at the desk in front of the whole ward: "Your rain is underpriced. Your retractor is overpriced. Whoever is buying knows the rain is worth more than you think it is, and is letting you think the retractor is the prize." Dar asked her how she knew. Milla said, "Because I've been priced by professionals," and went back to her census, and he sat with that longer than he sat with any slip.

Suri kept the other ledger. She called it the ledger of self, and it lived in her lap, and it had two columns: SOLD, and BEARING. Every slip he brought her went into the first column, described in her flat hand, and then she made him walk the second column with her — the load-bearing inventory, the walls the house stood on: Suri's stitching, and the first unpriced thing; Ilay's hand in his; Vahid's voice saying the word control; the feel of a pulse under two fingers; the refusal at the Blacktide; the word no, said in a theater, to a god. She audited the walls after every sale, and her audits got shorter, and her silences got longer.

Vahid, consulted, gave the doctrine in one of his lectures that started drunk and ended surgical, chalked one-handed on the clinic ladder: "A mind hollows from the connections out, boy. Not from the edges — the edges are where the junk lives and the junk can burn all day. Hollow starts where the walls meet. You sell the meeting and the wall still stands and holds nothing. Corridors first. Then the corridors forget where they were going. I have opened the last body of a man who sold his meetings, and there is nothing in the box but shelf. You are not selling furniture. You are selling joinery, and calling it furniture because the word is kinder." He put down the chalk with his one hand, precisely. "Bring me the slips before you sell them. I will not watch another student hollow. I watched Shirin's city do it to a generation."

On the ninth day Suri closed the ledger and did not open it, and said the thing she had been holding since the first slip.

"Three memories from hollow," she said. "Not three slips. Three walls. You're selling furniture, and the furniture's holding up the roof, Mehran. That's how houses go — not wall by wall; all at once, with the right wall gone. You're three walls from hollow, and the market keeps raising your price, and the market is not on your side."

"The market is funding the last climb of my life," Dar said. "I can't be choosy about whose coin."

"You can be choosy about whose hand it comes out of," Suri said, and marked the day's total, and the total had risen again.

On the eleventh evening the counterfoils came back from clearing at the mother house — the caravan turnaround had shortened from weeks to days, the route clearing its own throat — and Vell spread them on the ward table in rows, and Suri counted them, and stopped at the fourth, and set it down on the table with the care you use for something that has been in a fire.

It was pre-stamped.

Every cleared slip came back bearing the registry's flower and the word CLEARED, inked at the mother house, routine as weather. This one bore the flower and one word more, stamped in the black bone-pen hand Dar had seen sign a page of the World Registry, pressed into the counterfoil before the slip had even finished its route — before the caravan left, the timing said; before the ink of the deposit was dry on the docket:

RECLAIMED.

Bought at the source. Read off the docket as it was written. The Gardener was not clearing the market and taking what came. The Gardener was standing at the counter of the route, everywhere, at once, buying on the open market — buying his memories as he sold them, in real time, at his own asking prices — funding the last climb of the ninth seed's carrier out of her own account, one rain-fall and one retractor and one sober lecture at a time.

"She's paying for her own delivery," Suri said. Her voice was flat, and slower than flat. "We sold the furniture. She's the buyer, the courier, and the house. Every coin in the clinic's box right now passed through her hands coming and going." She looked at the stamped slip the way she had once looked at a granary ledger that would not balance. "That's not funding. That's feeding."

Dar stood in front of the wall of slips with the RECLAIMED slip in his hand and did the audit he should have done on the first day. The cleared slips, all of them, row by row — Vell had transcribed the catalog lines from the docket in her tall chalk letters, a clerk's insurance, and he read the line for every slip the black hand had bought, and the reading took the room's breath and did not give it back.

Not one of the cleared slips was random.

Rain on cistern lids — drummed, in his memory, in the meter the rain kept. The sober lecture — Vahid's voice turning on the anatomy of the hyoid like a tune. The green of the canal in spring — with the canal-singer's far voice caught in it, one street over, singing. The stamps, the tea, the cat — each one carrying, somewhere in its floorboards, some fragment of sound that rhymed with a single song, and he knew the song, and the song knew him, and every cleared slip was a memory of music.

He looked at Suri. Suri was already looking at the rows.

"She's not buying memories," Dar said. "She's buying the song. One bar at a time. Out of me."

Vell, silent, tongueless, took up her chalk and wrote it on the ward table's edge, and underlined it twice, the way she had underlined BRACK SOLD HERE TOO — a clerk's insurance, a counter's alarm, rung from a counting-house that had been eaten by the thing now buying:

SHE IS COLLECTING IT.
