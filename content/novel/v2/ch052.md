# Chapter 52 — A Door Shared Between Two

Dar came back down every night for a week, because the crack in the pipe-seat needed tending and because the answer to her question needed a hospital of its own.

The week, on the surface, ran on rails. Suri closed the audit and gave Doane her totals in the flat voice she saved for verdicts, and Doane copied them into the company's own books at the factor's instruction, the only instruction the reef honored; Vell's chalk copy of the book of the light stayed on the counting-house wall like a notice of seizure, and the workers began to walk past it on purpose; and Dar's ward behind the baleen kept its hours, and the reef kept its shifts, and the Cow moaned at every change, and nobody priced any of it differently, which Suri recorded as the reef's most expensive line item: THE BOOKS ARE CORRECT AND THE CITY IS UNSAVED.

The chamber he entered after dark was a different room. The lamp-crews left their oil-lamps at the stair; the works throttled; and in the dark the third seed came up out of its century-long bank and filled the glass womb with presence, the way the Lily filled a cellar — warmth first, then the hum, then the attention. The old woman had them dim the panes further with felt. "She's shy," Tally said. "Hundred years of audiences. She'd rather be listened to than looked at." Dar lay on a rope pallet Coss's people smuggled down rather than be caught caring, and the two seeds conversed across the room at a frequency below hearing, and he lay between them like a man lying between two conversations in two languages he was fluent in and had never been taught.

Tally talked the way old people talk who have been saving it up: nothing wasted, everything filed.

Her question, when she finally put it to him — she had carried it down a hundred years like a coal — was this: "My sister went up against the eighth door, alone. Is there anything of her behind it, or did it all end against the stone?" And Dar gave her the only honest answer the trade had: "There's no honest text. I don't know." Tally nodded, the way you nod at a bill you had already priced, and began at the beginning, because if the ninth did not know the door, then somebody had to tell him what the door had already cost.

Her sister had come seventy years ago, walking out of the wastes with the frost rising to meet her boots. A carrier — like Tally, like Dar; the word the founder had crossed out and she said plain — and not of her seed but of a sister seed, one of the middle girls, sown in a city whose name Tally had never learned because the woman had not known it herself. She had been born to the seed the hard way, the way Dar had. She had felt Tally's Cow sing across a hundred miles of flat, one night, at shift-change, and she had gotten up from a dying town and walked toward the sound, because when something sings in your own key you go to it or you die of not going.

"That's the truth of us," Tally said. "The seeds call. Everything else is paperwork."

She had hidden the woman in the chain-lockers for six weeks, in the dark the seed liked, and they had been six weeks of family. Two carriers, grown in two cities by two seeds out of the same first garden, and neither of them had ever had anyone to ask. What do you do when it wants the light gone. What do you do when it wants the blood. What do you do with the dreams — the rows of planted sleepers, the tower of heartbeats, the woman humming in a language you know without hearing. Two women in a locker under a whale, comparing the contents of a condition, and for six weeks the reef's books had balanced without them.

"She had a name before," Tally said, once, in the middle of that telling. "Before her seed took her. Never gave it to me. Said the seed had filed it, and what's filed can't be owed. I've thought about that a hundred years, little ninth. That's carrier arithmetic, that is. The road files your name so it can't be used against you, and then you go through the rest of your life unable to be called anything but what the seed calls you." Her chains took up the shrug. "She called me sister. I never even knew her name. You tell me which of those was the truer title."

"She wanted the eighth," Tally said. "That was her whole ledger. She'd climbed to the seventh on her own seed's credit — walked her mind-ruins the way your professor's papers tell it, spent what had to be spent, and she was standing at the top of the ladder with one rung left and no honest text for it. Nobody has an honest text for it. The honest texts died with the First Burning, and everything since is folklore, and folklore is what the road leaves in the pantry." The old woman's eyes went to the dark glass, to the presence inside it. "My girl told her what my girl knew, which was nothing. But the woman was past nothing. She went down into the salt under the carcass, alone, the way you'd go down to close a big account, and she tried the eighth alone."

The Cow had gone mute for a day.

"I thought the light would fail with her," Tally said. "The whole reef thought it. They run the pans by her and they didn't know it, and for one day the tank-lamps burned brown and the foremen walked soft, and under everything I felt what she felt, because that's the other truth of us: the seeds share what happens to their carriers. She came up against the door of the Soul and threw herself at it the way you throw yourself at a locked ledger — every key she had, the whole spent fortune of her, all seven rungs of her — and the door did not open, and it did not need to be cruel, because it did not even notice her. You cannot break a door that isn't shut, little ninth. You can only die against the idea of it."

"What is it, then," Dar said. "The eighth."

Tally was quiet for a while. Above them the reef breathed its industry; below them, under a mile of salt and carcass, the deep places held their own appointments. When she spoke again, the counting-voice was gone and something older was in it, the voice of a woman reciting the only inheritance the road had never managed to confiscate.

"The Soul Vein is not a rung. The rungs below it you climb, because flesh and bone and blood and breath and nerve are single, and a single thing can be forced. Mind you can walk, because a mind is one room even when the rooms are ruins. But the Soul was never one. It doesn't open to strength and it doesn't open to payment and it doesn't open to spending, because it isn't checking your fortune at the door. It's checking your company." The chains took up her slow shrug. "It is a door shared between two. It opens from the far side — by someone who knows the shape of your silence. Never by one. That's the whole doctrine, and my sister died for the lack of one sentence of it, and I have had seventy years to learn the sentence by heart and no ninth to tell it to."

Dar lay still on the pallet while the heartbeats kept their time — his own second heartbeat, and the deep one, the one that was not in the room but was always lately in his hearing, patient, immense, one beat behind.

One beat behind. It had been one beat behind since a rooftop in Ashkarad, copying him, the way a copyist reproduces a hand one line down. It had tapped the lullaby's opening bars through salt-glass and set its taps on his offbeats. On the road its frost had turned for him; its footprints had kept his stride; it had leaned when he leaned and stopped when he stopped, for a year, learning him — and he saw it with the clarity of a diagnosis confirmed: something had spent that year learning the shape of his silence, and the doctrine said that was the only credential the eighth door honored.

"The far side," he said aloud, working it like a knot. "Someone who knows your silence opens it from the far side. That's why it can't be climbed. It isn't climbed. It's answered."

"Then you already know the address," Tally said, "and all that's left is the walk. Provided," and the old warmth came back into her voice, dry as the flats, "you don't die against it alone on the way. My sister's mistake wasn't the trying, little ninth. It was the alone. The road teaches alone. Alone is the road's whole curriculum. Unlearn it somewhere soft."

She reached up then, into the housing where the garden-arm met the glass, and worked — her face steady, the petals parting around her fingers, the weep running brighter for a moment, the hum dipping one full note and coming back — and brought out a petal. Black, five-veined, cool as a filed receipt, lighter than a coin. One of her seed's own, freed at cost from the living machinery of a century.

She pressed it into his hand and closed his fingers over it, and her grip was very strong, and her eyes were family.

"To show your Sister," she said. "She'll know the third's line; we pressed petals in the dark before your city had a name. Tell her the third kept the light burning. Tell her we're all still counting. And tell her — " for one moment the recitation-voice cracked, a century of shift-change calls under it — "tell her the Cow is sorry. We didn't know. We were never told what we were singing to."

The warning came with the gift, because Tally of Gullet settled her accounts in one sitting. "The wain passed here a week ago, little ninth. Bound for Ashkarad. It doesn't stop at Gullet — we render for it; our tallow is its keep — but it passed, and it's ahead of you, and it doesn't hurry, because it has never once been late."

Dar asked the only question a surgeon could act on: what unchaining would cost. Tally answered honestly: the extraction was her circulation now; the seed's weep had no other channel; the city's light would fail in a season and the seed would follow it, and she would follow the seed, and the books would not notice for a week. He heard the whole account. He filed it, unpayable, next to the other unpayables, and said the one thing worth saying: "I owe you a door's asking, then, and I pay my debts at the door."

"Pay the sister," Tally said, settling back into the chains, closing the ledger of him. "I've been paid in counting for a hundred years. I'm rich."

At shift-change that night, with Dar up on the gantries and the reef throttling down around him, the deep heartbeat came.

It rolled in under the reef from the south, through a mile of salt and stone and dead leviathan, and the whole carcass felt it pass — every rib of the gantries taking the count like a struck train of bells — and it was moving the wrong way to be anything but what it was. Toward Ashkarad. Past them. Ahead of them. The Sister was ahead of them, running home.

Below, in the engine-room, the third seed answered — one note, laid over the dark in one direction, a hundred years of calling answered for the first time by the sound of the called-for passing close. The moan broke pitch. Frost came up through the render-yard boards in a single concerted breath, grey petals by the thousand, every one of them pointing back along the road the way the party had come — and Dar stood on the gantry with Tally's petal in his pocket and his balance gone suddenly and entirely homeward, while beneath his feet the light of Gullet burned on, rendered from a caged sister who had just watched her own kind run by, at last, toward the door that could not be climbed alone.

He went down the stair to the pallet-room, once more, to tell her: she passed. She's ahead of us. She's going home.

The old woman was already counting again, softly, and this time Dar understood the column: the beats as they passed, every one, a tally a hundred years long, with, at last, an end to count toward.
