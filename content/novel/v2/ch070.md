# Chapter 70 — She Is Buying the Lullaby

The cross-reference took one night and cost nothing but sleep, and that was the obscenity of it: the answer had been hanging on the clinic wall for eleven days, priced and re-priced, in his own hand's counterfoils.

Dar laid the cleared slips out on the ward table in the order of their clearing, and Vell chalked the catalog lines beside them, and Roya — who could no longer hear the city's song and had never once stopped being able to see its shape — stood over the rows with her brass mask catching the lamp, and did to the slips what she had once done to dreams. She mapped them. Not the contents. The intervals.

"Rain on the cistern lids," she said, tapping the first. "Meter's nine against four. The lecture — the hyoid, the sober hour — his cadence lands on nine against four at the turn. The canal in spring — the singer a street over, the phrase she repeats, it's nine against four." Her gloved finger moved down the rows, and the rows confessed. "Everything this house sold has a spine in it. The same spine. Bar and bar and bar. Somebody has been buying one song, Mehran, sold to her in pieces, in the floorboards of a hundred ordinary days."

Dar had known it since the ward table, since Suri's flat voice over the rows: every cleared slip a memory of music. What he had not done, because the not-doing was the last kindness he had left himself, was name the song. He named it now. There was no version of the night where he did not. The lullaby.

Not the whole of it — the slips were fragments, bars, meters, a hum caught one street over — but the Gardener was not buying fragments to shelve them. The registry pressed memories into leaves, and leaves could be laid edge to edge, and the Gardener had spent a century signing her mark over and over like a stamp; assembly was her native art. Piece by piece, out of the floorboards of his life, she was fitting the complete lullaby together — and the complete lullaby was not a keepsake.

The Choirmaster had told them what the Choratorium's foundations were: a locking-collar, the lid-maintenance machine, forty years of harvested organs one long lullaby fed to the deep. The choir organs were silent since the Blacktide; the collar was running on stored song, on resonance, on habit — a lock maintained by a music nobody was singing anymore, which meant a lock running down. A completed lullaby, sung from the city side, would recharge the collar. Or the reverse: a completed lullaby, sung with intent, was the key-side of the same instrument — the chorus that would open the deep from above at the exact hour he opened the door from below, so that the door would open into her hand. She was not collecting his past. She was building the hinge, and the hinge was his mother's song, and he had been carrying it to her counter in armloads, grateful for the coin.

"Cut the trade," Suri said. There was no audit in it, no column. "Now. Tonight. Burn the route."

Cutting the trade took until morning, and every hour of it was negotiation with a machine that did not recognize the word.

The registry's terms were the registry's terms, learned in a drowned city and printed on every counterfoil: deposits, once made, are the registry's to clear. What a depositor could do was close the account — stop selling, withdraw what was uncleared — and what it cost to close an account was an account-closing fee, priced like everything else at that week's rates, which was to say like extortion, which was to say Dar paid it, in the last coin the clinic had, standing at the League counter while the clerk inked the docket with the expression of a man witnessing something the paperwork had no column for. Behind the brass bars the little press sat with its feeding slot open, patient as a mouth. Vell stood at his shoulder and chalked one word on the counter, small, where only the clerk would read it: GOOD.

The account closed. Thirty-one slips remained uncleared, withdrawn, struck back into the ledger of self by Suri's own hand, one at a time, with her counting each one back into him aloud — "Rain. Retractor. Tea—" — and stopped.

"Tea," she said again. She was holding the last counterfoil in the withdrawal stack, and the ledger in her lap had gone somewhere she was not looking at it. "Mehran. This one cleared before the withdrawal. It's stamped."

Dar took it. The counterfoil was warm from her hand, and the registry's flower stood on it in ink, and beside the flower, in the black bone-pen hand he had seen sign the World Registry, pressed into the slip before the caravan ever left Ashkarad, one word:

RECLAIMED.

He remembered the deposit. That was the cruelty of a good memory: it played once more on the way out, like light holding in glass after the lamp is gone. The Choratorium dining hall, a year and a lifetime ago. Candles down a table long enough to seat a parish. His own regrown heart beating in its glass between them, the offer she had made him — his old life back, in a jar, for a child — and the two cups, always the two cups, the ritual she had taught Hesh and Hesh had taught the city's ruins: decide which cup to drink from. And Vey — Custodian Anra Vey, salt-and-ash hair, white gloves — pouring tea the way she poured everything, like an administration, and talking, because Vey always talked, warm and maternal and absolute, the endearments that were threats; and Dar, twenty-six years old and mortgaged to his own chest, filing it all under enemy and surviving.

He had filed one sentence wrong.

Vey, pouring the second cup, not to him, to the room, in the tone of a woman correcting a small error of ceremony: "She takes hers with the leaves left in. The Gardener. I pour this the way she taught me, and I have never once been asked whose pour it is." A pause, the steam moving. "The Gardener drinks alone. That is the whole of the liturgy."

The word. The name. Spoken by the branch office's own Custodian, in her own voice, over her own tea, with the intimacy of a hand on a shoulder — filed away in Dar's memory for a year like a hair in a ledger-book, and sold by him for the price of lamp-oil, and cleared, and pressed, and bought by the hand that taught the pour. The Gardener now owned Vey's voice saying her name. She owned the sound of her own authority confirmed by the woman who had run her Ashkarad office for forty years and never once been asked.

And the memory had held more than the name. He made himself hear the rest of it, because Suri was waiting and the audit had to be complete: beneath the talk, under the pour, Vey humming. Vey had always hummed at her tea; the whole Choratorium had been built on a humming woman. And the humming that night had run the lullaby — the child's thread, the mother's under-line, the turn, the verses — and the ending. The ending he had never heard from any other living mouth, because his mother's ending was spent and the Lily's was copy and the deep below kept its own counsel. Vey, pouring tea for a debtor she meant to harvest, had sung the lullaby whole, once, carelessly, the way rich people handle coin — and the press had drawn it out of him to the last bar, and the black hand had bought it, and somewhere on the Reclaim road the song was complete.

That night, in the cold room over the salt-glass door, the Lily could not remember the lullaby's ending.

It was not a thing Dar could have explained to anyone who had not spent a year learning her weather. She did not speak; she had spent her whole budget of words on him across two years and was not spending again. But she hummed — in the bones, in the teeth, in the space behind the sternum where a second heartbeat keeps its office — and the hum was hers, and on bad nights it was the lullaby, and this was a bad night, the wall of slips downstairs still reeling with what they had fed through it.

She began. The child's thread, low. The mother's under-line arriving under it from the dark below the floor, the Sister holding her harmony the way she had begun holding it, session after session, a century of loneliness answering a kitchen. The turn came. The verses came. And then the hum reached for the ending and there was nothing there.

Dar felt the loss before he understood it — a wrongness in his chest like a stair that ends one step early, the body's own arithmetic objecting before the mind files the paperwork. Frost crept up the salt wall beside the rig in a half-finished pattern, five petals that would not close, blooming and un-blooming at the lip of where the last bars should have carried them.

Not a wrong note. Not a hesitation. A nothing, with the exact shape of the ending — the way a room holds the shape of furniture after the furniture is gone, dust-edges on the floorboards where a thing stood for years. Her hum stood at the threshold of the last bars and the last bars were not in her, and she stood there, and did not fall, and did not repeat, and the silence below the floor held its breath with her.

Then, softly, the Sister's under-line laid itself across the gap — the harmony going on without the melody, the way a hand goes on holding a rope after the load is cut — and the Lily drew a breath Dar felt in his own lungs, and let the ending go.

He sat in the cold with both heartbeats quiet and did the accounting, because accounting was all he had ever been good for, and the account came out worse than any he had ever filed. He had thought the ending was his — bought back in a father's laugh, confirmed in a kitchen, resident. It had never been his. It had entered him through Vey's careless mouth, a thing he was holding in trust for a woman he had hated and a seed he had never met, and the press could not have known trust from property — the press does not price intention, only truth — and he had sold it for lamp-oil, and the hand that taught the pour had bought the pour. Downstairs, Suri was entering it in the ledger of what they owed the road, because that was her way of grieving: you wrote the loss down or the loss wrote you.

Part of it was never his to lose. He had lost it anyway. That was the whole of the arithmetic, and it did not total, and in the dark below the floor the two of them — the ninth seed and the first, the carrier and the keeper — sat in the gap where the ending used to be, and no one sang it, because the only complete copy left in the world was riding the Reclaim road in a wain of pressed leaves, going to a house where a hand with a ring of old burn-scars was fitting the bars together like panes, one by one, by lamplight, patient as interest.
