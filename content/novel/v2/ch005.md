# Chapter 5 — The Professor of Impossible Anatomy

The door to the anatomy theater groaned open on hinges rusted shut by salt, revealing a staircase that descended into darkness. Suri nudged Dar forward with her elbow, the carbon scales of her prosthetic arm scraping against the stone wall. "He's down there. Smells like gin and regret." She pressed a coin into Dar's hand. "For the door. And don't mention how you got that chest wound. His prices triple for interesting stories."

The air grew thick with the scent of formaldehyde and spilled alcohol as they descended. The theater was a circular room with tiered seating rising around a central stage, now littered with empty bottles and anatomical models. At the center stood a man with wild grey hair and a stained lab coat, gesturing wildly at a human skeleton propped in a teaching harness. His voice echoed in the enclosed space, slurred but precise.

"—and you see here, the coccyx isn't just a remnant! It's the anchor point for what might have been, the evolutionary echo of a tail we never needed but the body remembers! We are walking archives of what could have been!"

He was, Dar realized, lecturing no one. He had probably been lecturing no one for years, and he had not let that change a single syllable.

Suri cleared her throat, and the man spun around, eyes wide behind thick spectacles. "Ah! Visitors! Prospective students? Or have you come to mock my exile? The Syndic Wardens took my accreditation but they can't take my vision!"

"Professor Vahid Anousheh?" Suri asked, her tone businesslike despite the surroundings.

"The same, at your service. Though 'disgraced' is the adjective they prefer in the Marrowworks journals. What can I do for you? Need a diagnosis? A modification? Or perhaps just a lecture on the futility of human form?"

Dar stepped forward, unbuttoning his hospital coat to reveal the bandages wrapped around his chest. "I need to know what's growing in me."

The professor's eyes widened, not at the wound, but at the way Dar moved—too steady, too coordinated for someone with such an injury. He circled Dar slowly, peering through his spectacles. "Fascinating. The vital signs are... anomalous. Like a machine with a soul. Or a soul with a machine." He gestured to a stool. "Sit. Tell me everything. And if you've got gin, I'll tell you twice as much."

Suri produced a small flask from her pocket, which the professor snatched with trembling hands. He took a long swig before speaking again. "Good. Good. Now, the wound. The organ. Start there."

Dar described the heist, the glass reliquary, the shot, and the impossible way the Lily had rooted itself in his chest. As he spoke, the professor's initial drunken demeanor sharpened into rapt attention. He pulled a magnifying glass from his coat pocket and leaned closer to Dar's chest, ignoring the bandages.

"Extraordinary," he whispered. "The graft points... the neural interface... this isn't just a transplant. It's a replacement. Your heart is gone, isn't it? Replaced by something that wanted the position."

Dar nodded. "It pulses differently. Thirsts for blood in the dark."

"Of course," the professor said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "The Umbra Lily. I should have recognized the pattern. The Church's masterwork. They've distilled the entire process of Vein-engineering into a single, portable organ. It's not just a heart—it's a key. A key to what, I'm not entirely sure, but the mechanism is breathtaking."

He stumbled to a chalkboard covered in equations and began to draw, his handwriting surprisingly clear despite his intoxication, each stroke laid down with the economy of a man who had drawn this diagram a thousand times. "The Nine Veins. The biological ladder of transcendence. Flesh, Marrow, Blood, Breath, Nerve, Bone, Mind, Soul, and... Throne." He wrote each name with reverence. "Most anatomists consider them theoretical. Metaphors for human potential. But they're real. And your Lily is a shortcut—a cheat code to climb them."

Dar touched the bandages over his chest. "I'm already changing. Things heal too fast. My bones feel... denser."

"First Vein—Flesh," the professor confirmed, tapping the chalkboard. "The body's basic resilience. Second is Marrow—bone density, enhanced senses. You're already there, or very close. The Lily accelerates the process. But there are costs."

He turned back to Dar, his eyes serious. "Feeding requirements. The Lily needs specific nutrients, drawn from your blood. Sensory overload—each Vein opens new channels of perception, and the world becomes too loud, too bright, too much. Memory bleed—when you ascend, you don't just gain abilities. You gain echoes. Lived moments that belong to other people bleed into your consciousness. And the final cost..." The professor's hand shook as he wrote the last word on the board: BLOOM-THROUGH.

"What is that?" Dar asked, though he thought he already knew.

"It's when the transcendence stops being something you have and becomes something you are being turned into. The Lily doesn't want to live in you. It wants to become through you. Most carriers die of the process. The rest... stop being people. They become gardens."

The professor slumped against the chalkboard, suddenly looking old and tired. "I know this because I helped create the ancestor strain of the Lily. Before the Academy expelled me for 'unethical anatomy.' My partner, Dr. Shirin Bahar, believed we were building a heart that couldn't break. One that could patch itself, adapt, evolve. We succeeded. But we never considered the will of the organism itself. It wasn't a heart. It was a seed. And seeds are patient."

He walked to a dusty cabinet and pulled out a wax-cylinder recording and a sheaf of yellowed photographs. "This is our first volunteer. A dockworker with a failing heart. We grafted the prototype Lily into him. He lasted ninety days. This is what happened."

The first photograph showed a man in his thirties, smiling weakly. The next showed the same man, his skin beginning to sprout delicate black veins. The third showed his face almost obscured by what looked like petals of vascular lace. The final photograph showed only a figure standing in a dark room, covered in what could only be described as a garden of living tissue—black petals, pulsing vines, and at the center, where a heart should be, a flower that seemed to drink the shadows.

"He was serene at the end," the professor said, his voice cracking. "He said he could feel the city through the roots in his feet. That he was becoming part of something larger. Then he stopped speaking. Just... bloomed. The Church took him, of course. They've been refining the process ever since."

Dar stared at the photographs, a cold dread spreading through him. The Lily in his chest pulsed in response, a familiar ache that was becoming too intimate. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're already in too deep to turn back," the professor said, his eyes meeting Dar's. "And because I owe you the truth. The Lily isn't a parasite and it isn't a gift. It's a seed. And seeds are patient."

He placed the wax cylinder on an old phonograph and lowered the needle. A crackling voice filled the room, soft and melodic, singing in a language that made the hairs on Dar's arms stand on end. It was the same song he'd heard in the canal wall, but clearer, older. The Lily in his chest responded with a violent shudder, and for a moment, Dar felt a presence behind his eyes—not his own, but something ancient and waiting.

The phonograph sputtered to a silence that felt heavier than any noise. Dar stood frozen, the phantom song still echoing in his bones. The professor watched him, his expression unreadable through the smudges on his spectacles.

"That was Shirin," he said, his voice barely a whisper. "Her voice. They kept it. After they harvested her. Said it was too perfect to let go. Now it sings for them in the Choratorium."

Dar's hand went to his chest, pressing against the bandages. The Lily pulsed in time with his own frantic heartbeat, a counter-rhythm to the memory of that song. "It recognizes her."

"The Lily remembers its creators," the professor said, his voice hollow. "And it remembers what they became. The Church doesn't just harvest organs. They harvest potential. They take pieces of people and try to stitch them together into something greater than the sum. But they're using the wrong thread. They're using scissors instead of needles."

Suri shifted beside Dar, the carbon scales of her arm clinking softly. "What do you want from us, Professor? You've told us a terrifying story. Now what?"

The professor straightened up, the weight of his grief momentarily lifting. "I want to help you understand what you're carrying. And I want to help you stop it from blooming through." He gestured to the chalkboard, where the Nine Veins were still written in stark white. "Each Vein has a cost. But each also has a key to resisting the bloom-through. For Flesh, it's control—learning to direct the healing. For Marrow, it's grounding—using the enhanced senses to connect to the world, not be overwhelmed by it. For Blood..." He paused, thinking. "For Blood, you must learn that your will extends beyond your skin, but it must remain yours to command."

He turned back to Dar, his eyes intense. "The Church is planning something. Something that requires all the Lilies to be in bloom at once. A ritual during the Blacktide. The salt-moon eclipse. They're building something beneath the Marrowworks. Something vast. Something ancient."

"The First Vessel," Dar said, the words coming to him unbidden. He hadn't planned to speak them, but they felt true. "I saw it. When I was in the Choratorium."

The professor's breath caught. "You've seen the First Vessel? How?"

"During my escape. There's a chamber beneath the clinic. A... thing. Sleeping. Fed by pipes."

The professor's hands trembled with an excitement that bordered on terror. "The First Vessel. The prototype for the Throne. Forty years of harvested organs, grafted onto a single, dreaming host. Custodian Anra Vey isn't just leading the Church. She's trying to become the Ninth Vein. To become Ashkarad itself." He looked at Dar, then at the photographs of the bloomed man, a dawning horror on his face. "And she's using the Lilies to do it. Each one is a key. Each one is a piece of the puzzle."

Dar thought of the message that had broadcast from the Choratorium speakers, Custodian Anra Vey's voice calm and maternal, addressing the Lily within him. "She knows about me. She called it 'child.'"

"She called it 'child'?" the professor repeated, a strange look on his face. "That's... not right. The Lily isn't hers to claim. It's ancient. Older than the Church. Older than Ashkarad itself." He paced the room, his mind racing. "If she's trying to ascend during the Blacktide, and she has the First Vessel, and she's collecting Lilies... she's not just trying to become the Throne. She's trying to become the garden. The whole city."

Suri stepped between them, her arm held defensively. "Enough riddles. What do we do? How do we stop her?"

The professor stopped pacing and looked at them both, his expression grave. "We find the other carriers. We find out how many there are. We find out what she's planning. And we stop her before the Blacktide." He turned to Dar, his eyes pleading. "You're the key, Dar. The Lily chose you. It recognizes you. Maybe you can reason with it. Maybe you can control it."

Dar looked down at his hands, the surgeon's hands that had saved lives and now carried this impossible burden. The Lily pulsed in his chest, a silent promise of power and loss. "I don't know if I can control it," he admitted. "But I know I can't let it bloom through. Not like that."

The professor nodded slowly, a flicker of hope in his eyes. "Then we begin. We start with the song. The one you heard in the canal wall. The one that came from Shirin. If the Lily responds to it, maybe it responds to other things. Maybe there's a way to communicate. To negotiate." He picked up the wax cylinder again, holding it like a relic. "We start with the truth."

Dar reached out and took the cylinder from his hands, the weight of it feeling significant. The song still echoed in his mind, a melody that was both beautiful and terrible. The Lily in his chest pulsed once, as if in agreement. And in that pulse, Dar felt something else—not just the presence of the symbiote, but a question. A silent query hanging in the darkness between them.

What are you willing to become?
