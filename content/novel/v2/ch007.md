# Chapter 7 — Memory Bleed

The tithe-barge groaned through the canal, its hull scraping against the salt-crusted stone as it passed beneath the Choratorium's shadow. The sound rolled down the waterway like a promise being broken slowly, plank by plank. Forty debtors stood in harvest-racks, their wrists bound to the rusted iron railing, paperwork nailed to the mast like a death warrant. "Voluntary," it read, in the fine print that always omitted the part about how voluntary stopped when the ledgermen came calling. Dar could make out the columns from the water's edge — name after name, each with a figure beside it, each figure a balance no one had consented to but everyone had signed. Suri crouched in the fog, counting heartbeats with her pulse-reader. "Sixteen crew, eight Wardens. Tide's turning — we've got three minutes before the current pins us against the seawall."

Dar adjusted the carbon-fiber blade strapped to his forearm, its weight familiar as debt. Three minutes. In Ashkarad, three minutes was either a lifetime or nothing at all, depending on which side of the ledger you stood on. The fog off the canal had a taste tonight — brine and engine oil and, beneath them, the faint iron sweetness of harvested blood. It clung to the back of his throat like a signature awaiting ink. The Lily in his chest pulsed in time with the barge's engine, a rhythm that wasn't his own, patient and mechanical, as though it already knew how tonight's accounts would close. He watched the Choratorium's bone-white spires pierce the salt-fog above the waterline, pale as teeth in a gum of mist. "They're taking them to the upper sanctums," he said. "That's not standard tithe procedure."

"Nothing standard about this job," Suri muttered, her carbon-scaled fingers tightening around a length of monofilament. "Cartographer wants the census ledgers from the Warden's cabin. Says they list names not in the public registry." Names not in the registry. In a city that counted everything — breath, marrow, hours of sleep — the uncounted were either debts too ugly to write down or assets too valuable to declare.

Ahead, the barge's engine churned the black water into a slow boil. The debtors in their racks did not look like cargo; that was the obscenity of it. They looked like people. One of them — a thin man with a cooper's forearms — was mouthing numbers to himself, the way Suri did, the way everyone in Ashkarad did when the sums stopped balancing. Counting what he was worth. Counting what he'd been sold for. The harvest-racks were priced instruments, and somewhere above them a clerk had already entered the night's yield in triplicate.

The first grappling hook sailed over the railing, biting into the deck. Dar moved with the current, his body remembering the weight of the gravel-heart he'd lost, the Lily's strange lightness in its place — as if some inner scale had been rebalanced without his permission, stone traded for something that weighed nothing and cost everything. A Warden spotted them, his grey coat flapping as he raised his hand-cranked pulse-reader. "Defectors! Audit them!"

Chaos erupted. Dar ducked under a swinging baton, the blade on his arm extending with a whisper. He caught a Warden across the throat, the blade sinking deep, hot blood spattering across his face. The taste of it — metallic, coppery — triggered something deep within him. His blood answered.

It wasn't pain that hit him first, but pressure. A thousand tiny hammers behind his eyes, each heartbeat in earshot amplified into a drum. He heard the whole barge at once: the crew's stampeding pulses, the debtors' arrhythmic panic, Suri's heart racing like a trapped bird. Every body on the water was an open account, and something in him had begun to read them. His own blood surged beneath his skin like a dark tide, responding to his will even as his vision swam.

The surgeon in him hated it and admired it at once. He knew blood — its lazy architecture, its one-way tolls, the patient mathematics of pressure and release. Now it moved for him like a hand answering its own name. He could feel the branching map of every artery he touched, the tollgates of the heart, the small collateral routes the body kept for emergencies it hoped never to bill. His knowledge and the Lily's hunger were merging into a single instrument, and there was no line on any ledger marking where one ended and the other began.

He fought blind, the blade in his hand an extension of his panic. A Warden charged, and Dar's blood lashed out, a tendril of crimson that wrapped around the man's throat, lifting him off his feet. The sound of the man's strangled gasp was drowned out by the drumbeat in Dar's skull. Through the red haze he saw Suri's carbon scales flash as she disarmed another crewman, her movements economical, precise. She was counting something — steps, blows, debts to be paid — her lips moving over numbers only she could hear, while his own numbers had broken loose and started counting him.

The deck slick with blood, the Lily in his chest pounding like a second heart. He felt it then, the Vein opening, not as pain but as expansion. His blood was no longer just his — it was a force, a weather system he could command. He directed it, a dark storm that swept across the deck, knocking men off their feet, tangling limbs in crimson strands. The debtors in their racks screamed, a chorus of relief and terror, and beneath the screaming Dar could hear every heart present, forty small accounts chattering in the rain.

And then the memories came.

Not his own. A woman in white gloves tending a salt-garden. Rows of sleeping people planted in the earth like seeds, their heads barely visible above the crystalline soil. She hummed a lullaby in a dead language, her movements tender, pruning the sleepers with the same care a gardener might give to roses. Her face was serene, certain, but loneliness clung to her like the salt on the garden's surface, a loneliness so old it had calcified into calm. She knelt beside one sleeper, her gloved hand resting on the person's forehead, whispering words that weren't meant for waking — and Dar understood, with a certainty that lived in his marrow rather than his mind, that the whisper was a promise the sleeper would keep long after she was gone.

The dream was not a window but a loan. The longer he stood in the garden, the more he understood it was lending him the keeper's certainty along with the keeper's sight: the rows were not graves, the hum was not a song, the sleepers were not resting — they were compounding. Interest accruing in the dark, head by salted head. And somewhere beneath his feet, beneath even the garden, something vast turned over in its sleep and almost — almost — noticed him back.

Dar gasped, stumbling backward, the blood-storm receding as his vision swam. The drumbeat in his ears softened, replaced by the woman's humming. He was standing on the deck of the barge, Suri shouting his name, the last Warden falling to his knees. Victory. But the memory lingered, salt on his tongue, the lullaby echoing in his bones. He had been somewhere else. He had been someone else's hands.

"Wake up, Mehran!" Suri's voice cut through the fog. She grabbed his arm, her carbon scales cold against his skin. "We've got the ledgers. Let's move before the backup arrives."

The debtors were free, stumbling off the barge into the canal's shallows, their chains discarded. No one thanked them; thanks was a currency the Saltstairs had devalued generations ago. The debtors waded ashore and scattered into the fog, already becoming rumors, and behind them the harvest-racks swung empty — forty withdrawals made in a single night against an account that had never been theirs. Dar followed, his legs unsteady, the woman's face superimposed over the salt-fogged sky. He didn't know how long he'd stood there, lost in her garden. Seconds? Minutes? The time had bled away, borrowed from someone else, and no ledger in the city could tell him the interest owed.

They met the Cartographer in the Undergleam, the maps exchanged for the dream-mapping. The brass-masked figure listened as Dar described the salt-garden, the sleeping rows, the humming woman, her quill moving in short, certain strokes while the ink-lamp hissed between them. When he finished, she set the quill down with a click that sounded like a small verdict. "Custodian Anra Vey," the Cartographer said, the name like a stone dropped in still water. "Her private sanctum. Beneath the Choratorium. Registered to her alone." The brass mask tilted, considering him. "Understand what you carry, dreamer. The Lily is not new to her world. What you saw tonight, it has seen before."

Dar stared at the map the Cartographer handed him, the salt-garden marked in precise, careful strokes. The woman in the memory wasn't just any gardener. She was the Church's leader. The Lily inside him hadn't just replaced his heart — it had been inside Vey's world before. Carried, perhaps, the way a debt is carried from one book to another when a lender dies and the accounts must go on.

Later, Suri cleaned her blade in the canal's brackish water while Dar sat on the damp stone, the Lily in his chest quiet but not still. The tide had turned; the water pushed at the pilings like something impatient to be paid. "You saw something back there," she said, not asking. "Something that wasn't the fight."

"Memories," Dar said, rubbing his temples. "Not mine. The woman in the garden — she was humming a lullaby. I've never heard it before, but I know every note." He caught himself humming a bar of it under his breath and stopped, unsettled by how naturally it fit his own voice.

"The Lily bleeds memories," Suri said, counting the steps back to their bolt-hole. "Professor Vahid said it. At the higher Veins, you carry more than just blood."

"Carry," Dar repeated. The word sat wrong in his mouth, too small for what it held. "You make it sound like freight."

"In this city everything is freight," Suri said, wringing the blade dry. "Question is who's paying the portage."

The Cartographer's map lay on Dar's lap, the salt-garden marked with a single, perfect circle. "It's Vey's private sanctum," he said, tracing the lines with his finger. "The sleeping rows — she's been growing something down there. Something that needs to be tended."

Suri's pulse-reader beeped softly. "The Blacktide is three days off. The eclipse. That's when she plans to ascend. To the Ninth Vein."

Three days. In Ashkarad, that was forty debts called in, a hundred appointments missed, a thousand small agreements broken. Long enough for the whole city to feel the moon's thumb pressing on its scale. Dar looked at the map, then at his own hands. The blood that had answered his will was still beneath his skin, a dark weather he couldn't control, holding its breath between storms. "The Lily wants to bloom," he said, the words tasting like salt. "But the memories it gave me — they weren't random. They were a warning."

The Lily pulsed once, a rhythm that felt like agreement. The woman's lullaby echoed in Dar's mind, a song of certainty and loneliness. He had seen inside the Church's leader, and what he'd seen was a garden of sleeping people, tended by a hand that believed it was doing God's work.

The Blacktide was coming. And the Lily in his chest had been to the garden before.
