# Chapter 61 — The Claiming-Hymn

The countermeasure filled six numbered vials, and Vahid Anousheh had brewed every one of them from a dead woman's voice.

Not a metaphor — he was precise about this even when he was drunk, and tonight he was not drunk, because tonight the vials were going out of his cellar and into the world, and the world had a way of auditing whatever you sent it. Nine weeks of nights it had taken, one hand on the needle, the wax hissing under the stylus while he transcribed, spliced, and brewed — brine from the salt vaults for the base, iron for the carrier, and over it all the notation itself, the rests she had left in the tune, concentrated the way you concentrate anything in this city, by refusing to waste it. He had ruined two batches learning that the silence could not be synthesized, only harvested. It had to be cut from the living recording, from her breath, from the places where Shirin Bahar had declined, in her own throat, to sing.

Shirin Bahar's fourteen wax cylinders stood ranked on their brass spindle like a choir at rest, and what he had taken from them was a notation: the shape of the Church's compliance-hymn, transcribed off her recordings where she had once, in the last spring of her life, hummed it to herself the way a locksmith hums over a lock she is learning to pick. Between the formal passages of her notation — the graft-tolerances, the tissue-gradients, the lecture voice she used when she knew the machine was the only audience she would ever get — her voice dropped, and she talked to herself, and in one of those lowered passages, forty years old and pressed into wax, she had sung the claiming-hymn through twice, slowly, contemptuous, marking its cadences the way an auditor marks a fraud.

"It's a key of seven notes," he had told the council, holding up his one hand with the fingers spread. "The nursery poured it into their blood before they could speak, and blood is a filing system — it keeps what it is given. The hymn doesn't compel. It *requests*, in the only language the stock was ever taught to answer. So we don't fight the request." He had closed the spread fingers into a fist. "We pharmacy the silence between the fourth and fifth note. A rest, distilled. Brewed in brine and iron and one reagent this city has never had before — a counter-singing that sings to nothing. Chorus-free medicine. The first honest prescription Ashkarad has filled in forty years."

The council had found the budget in an afternoon, which told him more about their fear than any debate could have.

The order to move the children came down at dusk. After the herald team walked through the city's toll gates like auditors through an unlocked door, the arithmetic of the marked district stopped balancing: the clinic sat under the circled flower on Hesh's master map, the Index was hunting vessel registry page nine through the ruins of every census hall in the city, and eleven stock children and Ilay were sleeping in the one building in Ashkarad whose every wall was a coordinate. The council voted the evacuation before the lamps were lit. Destination: the deep counting-rooms under the Choratorium's lower corridors — the Choirmaster's ground, the one stretch of architecture in the city lined floor to ceiling with dead glass, where the jars that had held forty years of harvested voices stood empty and silent, drinking every resonance that came near them. The quiet way. The one road in Ashkarad where hymn-trained blood could pass without ringing like dropped coin.

The Choirmaster walked rear guard. He had put on weight since the winter, filled out the black coat he had been half-starved inside of, and he carried a fire-axe over his shoulder the way other men carry a shepherd's crook, and he moved down his relic corridors naming things to the children in a voice like warm oil — *this jar sang bass, this one lied, this one only knew the one note* — because he had decided, in whatever country inside himself he kept, that the way you take children past a graveyard of voices is you introduce them. He did not look at Elias more than three times in the whole descent, which Vahid, who counted everything a man's eyes refused to do, noted and filed: the last time those two had shared a building, the Choirmaster had been introducing the boy to a harvest table, and a stranger with two heartbeats had dislocated his wrist over it. Debts between the two of them were being serviced, silently, in kind.

Vahid walked mid-column with the vials strapped against his chest in a padded brass frame his left stump had learned to hold. Elias walked beside him, breathing in counted fours the way the clinic taught, a small boy with straight shoulders and the Church's own grading written into his lungs — PERFECT, the old harvest-files said, as if lungs could be a grade — and around them the dead reliquary corridor did its work. The glass drank their footfalls. The bone walls ate the echo. Even the deep, that vast slow pressure beneath everything, came through here thinned to almost nothing, a tide behind glass.

The strike came at both ends of the corridor at once, which was the whole point of it, and told Vahid that the heralds had read the city's disposition the way he had — correctly.

From below, toward the shaft mouth, a runner came up the Undergleam spur at a dead sprint with the report: torches in the Marrowworks underbelly, six white gloves at the mouth of the Throne shaft, making no move to descend, marking the head of the deep road the way a surveyor marks a boundary — chalk, and patience, and a book opened on a knee. Holding the top, as Roya would say, was going to be a figure of speech until the wain itself arrived.

And from ahead, at the corridor's far end, the barred door that led up into the ruined graft-theater levels opened without a sound.

Three of the six came through it. They did not rush. Rushing was for bailiffs; these walked with the unhurried economy of clerks who know the deadline is theirs, and the one in front stepped to the center of the corridor, faced the column of children down its whole ringing length, and began to sing.

The claiming-hymn. Vahid had read it off wax and heard it in his dreams for nine weeks, and it was worse in the original than in the transcription — thirty-one bars of patient, reasonable melody, a lullaby's grammar with a lien's intention, the tune the Church had poured into stock blood before speech so that the blood would know how to answer when asked. The nursery compliance-song, activated now in the open air of a ruin by a voice with white gloves on its hands.

Elias answered.

The boy's mouth opened and the hymn came out of him like water finding a grade — no terror in the face of it at first, only the terrible obedience of tissue doing what it was filed to do, his perfect Church-graded lungs producing the melody in a voice doubled and trebled by the dead jars along the walls until the corridor itself seemed to be singing the child. And down the column, one by one, the other children's lips began to move. Ilay clamped her hands over her own mouth and hummed against her palms, hard, off-key, her copper hair shaking — a girl bailing out her own blood with both hands. The younger ones stirred like sleepers being called in a voice they trust. If the verse completed — if the question finished being asked — the whole cohort would answer, every stock child in Ashkarad at once, a census taken from inside their veins, and the herald would not need to touch one of them.

Vahid was already moving. He had planned this the way he planned dissections, on the assumption his hands would shake, and his hands were shaking, and the plan held. The brass frame braced against his chest took the weight of the vial; his teeth pulled the stopper; his one good hand found the vein at Elias's wrist, the counting position, and the boy looked down at him mid-verse with his eyes full of a terror that had finally, blessedly, become his own.

"Permission granted," Vahid said. "Sing something of your own afterward. That's an order from the Chair of Impossible Anatomy, and I fail every student who disobeys me."

The needle went in on the fourth bar, into the silence between the fourth note and the fifth, and the first dose of chorus-free medicine in the history of medicine entered the bloodstream of a boy who had been property since before he was born.

The hymn did not stop the way songs stop, dying bar by bar. It stopped the way debts stop — no cry, no struggle, just the flat, administrative finality of a stamp coming down. Elias's voice quit mid-syllable; his lips kept their shape around the fifth note for one full breath, and then his whole body forgot the tune, a forgetting visible like a wave, passing down the column child by child, lips going still, shoulders dropping, the corridor's dead glass drinking the last of the melody out of the air. The boy breathed. Four counts in, four counts out. He sang to nothing. He sounded like nothing at all, which was, Vahid thought, the most beautiful clinical finding of his entire discredited career.

The herald in the center of the corridor finished its own verse alone, into a silence that had stopped answering it. It stood a moment, listening — taking the reading, Vahid thought, that was the whole expedition: *does the stock still answer* — and then it closed its mouth with the small finality of a ledger being shut, and the three of them withdrew the way they had come, unhurried, correct, backward through the door that opened without a sound. They had not touched a child. They had not needed to. They had come to check an investment's yield, and the yield had just been foreclosed in front of them by a dead woman's distilled contempt, and they wanted their masters to know it, and they wanted Ashkarad to know that they knew it, and both messages arrived intact.

They left the tally-book.

It lay on a jar plinth midway down the corridor, open, squared to the plinth's edges with a clerk's neatness, weighted at the corner with a spanner as if even its abandonment had been filed. Vahid got to it first, because his legs remembered being young. The page it was open to was not the first page. It was Ashkarad's — the city's own account, the round's current entry, written in that vertical, terribly patient hand the tally-book's own earlier pages had already taught him to dread, and beside the entry, in the column where, pages back, Brack's account carried its stamp, the record of Ashkarad's standing:

LIEN PENDING — COLLATERAL: THE LID.

The Choirmaster read it over his shoulder, and for a long moment the corridor's silence held both of them, and forty years of dead glass, and eleven children breathing in counted fours, and one boy humming — tunelessly, raggedly, gloriously off-key — something that was nobody's hymn at all, just a small boy's own noise, scored to nothing, owned by no one, priceless.

"So that's what we are on their books," the Choirmaster said at last, and his voice, for once, was not smooth at all. "Not a city. Collateral."

"Every city is collateral somewhere," Vahid said, and closed the book, and was surprised at how much it weighed, which was not a property of books. "The difference between us and Brack, choirmaster, is that our lien is pending. Pending is the only word in that whole line we have any vote in."

He carried the tally-book up out of the deep in the brass frame, against his chest, where the vials had ridden, and above him the city was waking to the news in its usual way — as a price adjustment.
