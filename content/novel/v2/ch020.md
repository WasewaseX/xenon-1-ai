# Chapter 20 — The Weight of Scales

The carbon scales on Suri Kaveh's right arm were worth twelve years and seven months of her life, plus interest that compounded every salt-moon. She knew this because she had counted it every day since the day she'd sold the skin off her own bone, and counting was the one discipline no one could repossess. The scales were cool to the touch, like holding a piece of night sky, and they shifted minutely when she flexed her fingers, whispering against each other in a language only her bones understood. Some nights the whispering was the only voice in the room that didn't want anything from her.

Her mother's gambling debts had been the first ledger entry that mattered, written in blood and desperation when Suri was nineteen. The Gavel had come for her father's heart first — too many losses, too many promises broken — and her father had signed at the warehouse counter the way men sign for deliveries, because the alternative was watching it happen anyway. Then they came for her mother's liver when the debts tripled, and there was no one left to sign but Suri. She had stood in the Marrowworks tithe-warehouse, smelling of antiseptic and despair, and signed the papers for her own arm. The scales were payment in advance, a graft that would keep the ledgermen at bay for a while. She'd been told it was a fair exchange: skin for life. What they hadn't told her was that the scales were also a receipt. Every time she passed a Syndic Warden with his hand-cranked pulse-reader, they'd note the graft's registry number, and somewhere her name moved down a column she would never be shown. The debt wasn't paid; it was just moved to a different column, resold three times over to syndicates who charged more for the inconvenience of harvesting from a partially-gifted vessel. Her body had been an asset changing hands for years. She was the only party to the transaction who had never been paid.

The lamp on her desk cast long shadows across the ledger pages. Salt fog pressed against the windowpanes, muffling the city's usual grumble to a distant murmur, and the fog was thick enough tonight that even the Choratorium's spires had been excused from glowing. Suri's fingers traced the columns she'd maintained for seven years: debts, deliveries, distances, lies told, favors owed. The numbers were her shield, her compass, her truth in a city that traded in illusions. When she stopped counting, she realized, was when she started owing. The year she turned twenty-four had been a blank page in her ledger — a season of not counting, of trusting someone who turned out to be another kind of ledgerman — and that emptiness had cost her more than any debt she'd ever carried. She did not permit blank pages anymore. Paper was cheap. Blankness was expensive.

She splinted her left arm with a rolled-up medical text from Saint Ferrous Hospital — something about arterial grafts that Dar had left behind, its margins annotated in his small precise hand — and tied the twine with her teeth. The Choratorium raid had been expensive. Three vials of coagulant, two doses of painkiller, one borrowed pulse-mask from Vahid's collection, and the cost of eleven stomachs she couldn't resell — eleven children fed on credit, their debt transferred to her ledger by an act of arithmetic she hadn't agreed to and would never reverse. She'd calculate the syndicate rates later. For now, she needed to audit the physical damage. Her left arm was bruised but intact. Her right, the scaled one, had taken a glancing blow from a Chorus bone-plate enforcer's pauldron. A hairline fracture in the ulna, maybe; she could feel it the way you feel a wrong figure in a column, small and false and insisting. The scales had absorbed most of the impact, but the bone beneath remembered. The scales would still be there when the bone knitted. That was the thing about receipts. They outlasted what they were receipts for.

The room smelled of lamp oil, dried blood, and paper dust. It was a Saltstairs hideout — three flights up, with a window that faced the seawall, rented from a landlady who accepted silence as rent. From here, she could see the faint glow of the Choratorium's bone-spires when the salt fog thinned, a smear of pale light on the horizon like a scar on the sky. She'd found the place after Dar had stitched her arm in the alley behind Saint Ferrous, when the Gavel's men had cornered her over a missed delivery. He'd worked without gloves, his hands steady and sure, counting his stitches as he went — not for payment, she'd understood later, but the way a mason counts courses. "Eighteen," he'd said when he was done. "That's what you owe me." She'd paid him three days later with a delivery of black-market adrenaline, and the debt had been settled. Or so she'd thought. Ledgers had a way of multiplying.

Dar was different. He understood the economy of the body in a way that wasn't about profit margins. When he'd sewn her up, he hadn't been thinking about the exchange rate for skin or the interest on blood. He'd been thinking about the geometry of a wound, the tension of tendon, the poetry of a clean closure. It was the first unpriced thing anyone had ever given her, and she had been auditing it ever since: first as a debt, then as an investment, then as something else entirely — something she didn't have a column for in her ledgers, and the absence of the column bothered her more than any total.

She poured a cup of water from the clay pitcher on her desk. The water tasted of rust and salt, like everything in Ashkarad. Outside, a bell tolled — curfew. Syndic Wardens would be sweeping the streets with their pulse-readers, checking for debtors out after hours, and the readers would find what the graft-numbers told them to find. Suri counted the strokes: twelve. Enough time to finish her work before they reached this district. She entered the count without thinking. The counting entered her back.

Her ledger was bound in leather, the pages thick and yellowed with use, its spine sewn twice where she'd broken it. She opened it to the section marked "CURRENT DEBTS." The first entry was for Vahid Anousheh: three vials of ethanol, borrowed pulse-mask, information about the Choratorium's layout. The second was for Ilay: food, water, the blanket she'd stolen from a laundry cart — theft entered honestly, because a ledger that lied to itself was worse than no ledger. The third was for herself: painkillers, coagulant, the splint. She added them up, carrying the fractions forward to the next page. Mathematics was honest in a way people weren't. Two plus two always equaled four, regardless of how much you wished it were five, and wishing was the one currency Ashkarad had never honored.

The door creaked open, and Dar stepped inside, careful not to brush against the frame — a courtesy she'd never asked for and had never once been able to price. He moved with a new fluidity she hadn't noticed before, something in the set of his shoulders, the way his breath seemed to pause at just the right moments, conserving itself like a man who knew exactly what his air cost. The Blood Vein, she supposed. He'd been different since the Choratorium raid, too. More present, somehow. Like he'd finally stopped owing something to himself.

"Ledger time?" he asked, nodding toward the book.

Suri didn't look up. "Auditing. The raid cost more than I budgeted."

"Vahid said he'd cover the ethanol," Dar said, sitting across from her. His hands rested on his knees, palms up — empty, as always, a man showing his accounts. "And the mask was his."

"He also said he'd pay me back in 'favors,'" Suri said, making air quotes with the splinted arm and regretting the flourish. "Favors don't buy coagulant."

Dar smiled faintly. "No. But they buy trust."

Suri finally looked at him. The lamp light caught the new lines around his eyes — veins of silver that hadn't been there before, fine as thread. The Lily, she knew. It was blooming through him, changing him from the inside out, and it was doing the work the way institutions did work: slowly, structurally, without asking permission twice. She'd seen the black vascular lace spreading across his collarbone during the raid, hidden by his shirt collar but visible when he'd bent to lift Ilay — lace like a promissory note written on skin.

"Trust is a column with no numbers," she said, closing the ledger. "I can't audit it."

"You don't have to," Dar said. "Not everything is a transaction, Suri."

She snorted. "In Ashkarad? Everything is a transaction. Even breathing costs if the Wardens are feeling particularly thorough that day."

Dar was quiet for a moment, and the quiet had weight to it, the way silence in a salt cellar has weight. "When I stitched your arm," he said, "I didn't count the stitches because I wanted something in return. I counted because... because it mattered. The way the tissue met. The tension on the suture. It was precise. Clean."

Suri stared at him. Precision. Cleanliness. These were the things she valued, too — the things that made the chaos of the world bearable, that made a ledger more trustworthy than a friend. But she'd never heard anyone describe them like that, as if they were worth something beyond their utility, as if the counting itself were a kind of care.

"The scales," she said suddenly, pushing back her sleeve to reveal the carbon plating, dull and gleaming at once, night-sky black with a faint oil-sheen of violet where the lamp caught it. "They're worth more than the skin they replaced. Everyone knows that. But the graft-bank never told me why."

Dar's gaze fixed on the scales, and she watched him look the way he looked at a wound before he opened it. "Vahid mentioned something. Partially harvested vessels. The Chorus specializes in them. They take what they need, leave the rest, and let the market do the rest."

Suri's fingers tightened around the ledger, and the old spine creaked. "There's a page. With my name on it. I know it. The graft-bank clerk looked at me differently when I signed the papers. Like I was inventory."

"The Chorus keeps a registry," Dar said. "Of perfect vessels. I saw it. In the reliquary archive. Ilay's name was on it."

Suri swallowed. "And mine?"

"I don't know," Dar said. "But if they're building something... something like the First Vessel... they'd need parts. Specific parts."

The room felt smaller suddenly, the walls drawing in like an itemized bill. The salt fog outside pressed against the glass, as if trying to get in, as if the storm had a claim on her too. The scales on her arm seemed heavier, colder. She'd always known the debt wasn't paid. But she'd never imagined it was a down payment on something worse — that she was not a debtor at all but stock, entered years ago in a book she'd never see, appreciating quietly in the dark.

She opened the ledger to a fresh page. Under the column marked "SYNDICATES," she wrote: CHORUS — UNKNOWN LIABILITY. The letters sat on the page like a debt she could feel but not total, and the not-totaling was the cruelest part. Then she turned to the back, where she kept personal notes — things that didn't fit in the columns, couldn't be quantified. Things like the weight of a child's hand in hers, or the sound of Dar's laugh when he thought no one was listening. She kept them small, kept them at the back, kept them anyway.

Under everyone she tracked — Vahid, Ilay, the Gavel, the Wardens, even herself — she wrote:

DAR MEHRAN — PAID IN FULL

The ink bled slightly on the paper, a dark flower blooming in the lamplight. She stared at it for a long time, the words both true and impossible. Nothing in this city was paid in full. Then, beneath it, in letters so small they were almost invisible, she added:

(keep counting anyway)

The door creaked again, and Suri looked up. Ilay stood in the doorway, holding a piece of charcoal, her eyes the color of river stones and wide as wells. Her gaze was fixed on the ledger page — on the ink flower, still wet, still darkening.

"The singing," the little girl said, pointing a small finger at the paper. "It's louder here."
