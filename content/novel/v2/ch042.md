# Chapter 42 — The Ledger of the Road

The seawalls closed behind them the way a book closes — two leaves of salt-black stone swinging on hinges older than the Church — and then Ashkarad was gone, and there was nothing in any direction but the flats.

The flats were a ledger of their own. White salting ran out ahead of them until the world lost its seam, sky and ground meeting at a line too even to be honest, like a column summed by someone who wanted the number clean. The fog-sea had withdrawn to a grey smear at the bottom edge of the horizon, and it kept withdrawing. From the roof of the last tanner's shed Dar could still see the tide going out, mile on mile of seabed coming up into the air, black mud drying to grey at its edges the way a wound dries. The water had not come back on any schedule the old marks allowed. It left the way a creditor leaves a failing account — gradually, then all at once, then not at all — and what it left behind was the shape of its absence, held in mud, exact.

Suri walked point with the porter's frame strapped across her shoulders. The frame held a padded crate, and the crate held Shirin Bahar's wax cylinders, forty of them, each one wrapped in oilcloth like a small expensive bone. She had appointed herself porter at the seawalls and had not asked anyone's permission, which was how Suri did everything. Her ruined arm hung in the strap where the carbon scales had split and burned, dull as a night sky with a hole kicked through it, and her voice came back over her shoulder at even intervals, flat as a clerk reading a column.

"Nine thousand, four hundred and ten."

Nobody had asked her to count aloud. She counted because the counting was hers, and out here there was no one to repossess it.

Vell walked the route stones. Every mile the registry's old surveyors had set a post, knee-high, salt-eaten, and on each post the same mark: a five-petaled flower carved as if blooming out of a crack in the stone, weathered to a shadow of itself and still legible, the way a stamp stays legible long after the hand that held it is filed. Vell stood at each one longer than she needed to. She was a small woman with a clerk's posture and a counting-house's stillness, and her tongue had been cut out of her in Brack, so the world got her opinions in chalk. She wet her stub of chalk against her lip, considered the post, and wrote on the stone itself, under the flower: two lines, a number, and an arrow pointing back the way they had come.

"What's she marking?" Suri asked, though she had already guessed.

Dar read it. Vell's hand was a counting-house hand, narrow, vertical, meant for ledgers. WATER AT POST 41: FORTY-ONE HANDS BELOW OLD MARK. She moved to the next post and chalked the same observation with a new number, and at the next, and by the third post the numbers had a direction, and the direction was down. She was charting the sea's retreat the way an auditor charts a shortfall — hands per mile, mile per day — and she did not like the total she was arriving at. Her face said so. Vell's face said very little, but against that background of very little, the amount it said was a shout.

They walked. The flats gave them their entries to read as they went. Cairns stood out on the white in loose families — stacked stone, knee-high to chest-high, each one a traveler's estate closed out by whoever came behind. Some were old enough that the salt had welded them into single grey teeth. Some were days old. None of them had names on them; out here you did not file the name, you filed the number of stones, and the desert kept the count. Farther on, the masts began: ships that had died standing, hulls swallowed to the gunwales by decades of salting, masts left up like grave-posts with notches cut into the wood — crew counts, Vell decided, running her fingers down the notches and then writing the totals on her slate with a certain professional approval. Even out here, someone had kept books.

At dusk the tide-line paid its interest. Heat-berries came up along the last wet ground in strings and clumps, bioluminescent, a low green-gold radiation of light exactly where the water's edge used to run, as if the sea had left a creditor's lantern burning to show it intended to return. Dar knelt once at the line and looked at the glow without touching it. Feeding light to the dark was a transaction he understood from the wrong side now.

Because daylight was a tax. He had learned the rate of it within the first mile. The Lily ran dim under the open sky; he felt her the way you feel a fire banked behind a closed stove door — present, patient, thermostatically subdued — and every hour of sun was an hour of debit against her. When the light went, she came back into the front of his chest like warmth returning to a hand. So they walked the long daylight hours in a purposeful silence, and made camp in whatever ruin or hull-shadow the flats offered, and in the dark he paid her.

He paid her the second night in the lee of a grave-mast, in a shelter of hull planking and oilcloth sealed down on all four sides so that no light could enter. He opened his own palm with his own knife, in the total dark, and gave, and the Lily drank from him with a gentleness that was worse than violence, and outside the oilcloth, in the cold, frost bloomed. They saw it at dawn: fine grey petals feathered across the canvas in a long converging fan, and every petal pointed the same way. Not toward the sea. Back — back along the route, back toward Ashkarad, toward the deep heartbeat under the Throne shaft, toward the sister the one word had named, and gone patient again.

His balance leaned that way too. It had been leaning since the roof, a fractional tilt of the whole body toward the west and down, as if something below the salt had hooked a line to his sternum and left it slack and then, mile by mile, begun to take up the slack.

"You're listing," Suri said, on the fourth morning. "Starboard. Like a loaded barge."

"She leans," Dar said.

"Toward home."

"Toward the deep end of home." He rolled his shoulder, correcting a drift he could not correct. "I'm carrying a compass that wants what's behind us. And we're walking the other direction because the answers are printed in front of us. It's a ledger imbalance. We'll carry it."

Vell, passing, wrote on her slate and held it up: THE BODY IS NOT THE BOOK. He looked at it for a moment. "No," he agreed. "The book is not the body either. That's the problem with both of them."

The nights were for the Mind Vein's discipline, which meant the nights were for not using it. Vahid's instruction held: memory was architecture, and you did not spend what you could walk. So Dar walked his corridors in the dark of the shelter, past the swept floors, the emptied rooms, the doorway where his mother's face had hung and now hung nothing — and stopped, as he always stopped, at the furnished door at the deepest hall's end, the one he had never built, warm on the far side like a room someone had just left. He did not touch it. He stood in front of it and counted his own two heartbeats until the walking was done, and came back up the stairs of himself without paying anyone.

On the sixth day the flats changed their subject. The white gave way to stakes — old mooring-posts in rotted rows, thousands of them, marching out of the mud where no water stood, and the smell arrived before the sight did: iodine, old rope, wood rot sweetened with salt. The drowned port's outskirts. The seabed lay exposed to the horizon in every direction, flats on flats of drying mud scored with the tracks of things that had lived under the water and were learning, or failing to learn, to live without it. Heat-berries glowed out here even at noon, dim green constellations in the shadow side of every stake.

And across all of it, one line of footprints.

They came off a bank of dried sea-mud and the prints were there, and the line of them ran straight as a ruled entry, from the seaward emptiness inland — toward Ashkarad, the direction they had come from — walking through the stakes without once deviating to use them, small feet, bare, a diver's spread toes and salt-burned heels, and no second set of prints anywhere. Whoever had walked here had walked out of the sea's absence and kept walking, and the mud had held it all.

Suri crouched over the first clear print for a long time. "Small," she said. "Light. Ball of the foot first, no scuff, no drag. Whoever it is doesn't get tired." She set her own boot beside it. The print was half again smaller than hers. "Bare in this cold. That's a diver's foot, or nothing's foot."

Vell was already pacing the line, chalk out, tallying. Dar watched her measure the interval between the first print and the second, and the second and the third, and saw her stop. She went back and did it again. Then she stood in the middle of the line with her chalk hanging in her fingers, and for the first time since the tide-gates her face said something large.

Dar felt it before she finished writing it. The Lily leaned — not west now, not toward home, but down, and slightly toward the prints, the way a person leans toward a window to hear a familiar song on a far street. His balance went with her. He put his hand against a mooring-stake and stood inside the pressure of that attention and counted.

His own stride fell on his own pulse. He had never thought about it before; a body carries its meter with it, and the meter is the heart. But the prints under him had their own meter, and Vell's chalk finished the entry on her slate and she turned it around and held it up in the grey light.

ONE HEARTBEAT APART. NOT HER PULSE. NOT YOURS.

He counted the distance between the prints against the second heartbeat, the deep one, the patient one, the drum he had heard once from a clinic roof and could not unhear. It fit. Every print fell precisely into the vast slow interval, stride for beat, the walker's body keeping time to something enormous walking with her, under her, through her — a small bare woman pacing out the tempo of a buried god, headed home.

The wind came across the flats with nothing left to slow it. Somewhere behind them the tide was still going out, and somewhere ahead, down the drowned streets of a city that had married its god, a black flower the size of a district lay in a drained harbor and waited to be fed.

"Lowmark by tomorrow's dusk," Dar said. "We walk on the stones. Nobody follows the line."

They walked on. The line of prints went the other way, small and bare and patient, one heartbeat at a time, toward Ashkarad.
