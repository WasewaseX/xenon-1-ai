# Chapter 51 — Old Tally

The day-chain parted at the third bell, and Dar learned the sound of it from the inside of a patient's mouth, mid-suture: a crack like the city's spine clearing its throat, then a long tearing run of links, then the whip.

The day-chain was Gullet's whole morning — a haulage chain thick as a man's waist, running off drums in the carcass's spine, lifting the tallow casks from the barge-basins at the waterline up three hundred feet to the render-floors. Under load, at the third bell, with a full string of casks off Lowmark on the hook, the master link had simply resigned. The falling end had gone down into the basin. The rising end had come back through the gantries like a creature leaving its den, and eleven people went to the grating along its path, and the casks went with them.

The works' surgeon arrived at a run. He was a bone-setter with a stamp and a price-list, and he was one man, and the yard was suddenly an anatomy lesson with the anatomy still awake in it. The foreman in charge of the yard — a slab of a man called Coss, with a voice that did not need the brass tube the Gavel bosses used — looked once at the queue of the hurt, once at the bone-setter, and then across the yard at Dar, who was already moving.

"Reef-cutter," Coss said. It was what the workers had taken to calling him; on the reef, everything got renamed into the books. "You're on the company's rolls since you set foot on my grating. The books call labor."

"Then the books can watch me bill them for it," Dar said, and knelt.

Triage was triage in any language. He put the Blood Vein quietly to work under his gloves and clamped the worst three where they lay, and the yard's second foreman stood over him calling the cask-count to the spine-drums in a flat voice while Dar called pulses to Vell, who stood at his shoulder with the tally-slate and counted everything back to him in chalk. Bloom-scald came off the split casks in steam. Where it landed it frosted. One render-hand, scalded down the throat by a burst cask, did not come back from the grey no matter what Dar closed or opened, and Dar filed him with the only honors the yard allowed, which was that two of his crewmates stopped working for as long as it took to look at him. Then the reef does not stop, and it went on.

What the reef did instead of stopping was price. By the time the chain was re-coupled, the factor's runners were through the yard with the accident-book, entering the day against the accounts: the lost casks, the lost chain-hours, the render-hand's unworked shifts to come, already discounted to the store. Suri read the book over a runner's shoulder, uninvited, and wrote nothing down and forgot nothing. "They billed the corpse," she said that evening, in the flat voice she reserved for totals. "I've seen cities eat their living, Mehran. This one bills its dead for the interruption."

The chain was re-coupled by noon. By the noon bell the yard was back to its own volume, and Dar was washing bloom off his hands at the cistern when the summons came, and the summons was not for his hands.

"The tail fouled the extraction-line," Coss said. He had come down off the gantries personally, which the foremen did not do, and he stood with the discomfort of a man visiting a place he avoids. "The whip went through the gantry over the engine-room and took the line with it. The line can't be cut. You have to understand that, cutter. Whatever the line is fouled on — you treat it. You don't cut it."

"The line," Dar said. "The extraction-line from the Cow."

"From the Cow." Coss's face did something Dar had not seen it do: it went to the place where the begging lived. "Lamp-crews won't go in that room. I'm not asking them to. The factor wants light by dusk — the whole city burns what she renders — and the line's fouled up-slope of the pans. You're the only one on this reef who opens things for a living." He looked away, at the render-yards, at the baleen, at the light. "She's down there. You'll see."

The engine-room was at the carcass's chest, where the heart would have hung, and the party of one that went down into it went down through warmth. Not furnace-warmth — body-warmth, the deep steady heat of something living, rising out of the hold the way breath rises out of a mouth. The stair was brass, worn hollow. The lamp-crew's oil-lamps burned at the mouth of the chamber and no further, as if the dark past them were a door.

The Cow was a womb of brass and glass the size of a chapel, ribbed and piped, seated in the leviathan's chest cavity the way a heart is seated — carefully, with its vessels respected. Glass pipes ran out of it by the dozen, up through the ceiling on their way to the pans, each one running with slow gold. The whole assembly moved. Not mechanically. Peristaltically, gently, the way a chest moves around the business it is keeping, and the sound inside the chamber was not the shift-change moan but its opposite: a hum, a many-voiced hum, so low that Dar felt it arrange itself along his bones and take up residence.

The line was fouled on the chain, but the chain was fouled on her.

She hung in the machine's near side, visible through a pane of glass older and thicker than the Vigil's, and she was an old woman, and she was half a garden. Black lace had taken her from the left ankle to the collarbone — vascular lace, fine and dense, the pattern Dar knew from his own chest under strain — and along the whole left arm the lace had opened into petals, dozens of them, black-fringed and open, and the arm itself was bare of the machine's housing and seated to the elbow in the extraction-glass, because the petals wept the tallow and the tallow was drawn off through the pipes and the pipes fed the city. Her hair was rope-grey and tied back for work. Her right side was only a very old woman's side, thin and work-worn. Chains ran from her — waist, right wrist, a collar seated soft-looking and final — and every chain was polished bright where a century of living had moved against it.

She was counting. Her lips moved on numbers, an unbroken column, low and even, the way Suri counted and the way Vell wrote: because it was hers, and nobody on this reef could repossess it.

The broken chain-tail had come down through the gantry above her and wrapped twice around the great pipe where it seated to her arm, and the torque had cracked the seat, and where the seat had cracked the weep ran free over the glass instead of into it, and that was the wound. Dar looked at it with the surgeon's eye first and the rest of him second: an extraction-point of a century's standing, an interface between a body and an industry, ulcerated at the margin, weeping from the wrong place. Treat it. Do not cut it. Whoever had built this machine had built it around a person, and the person had kept the machine alive for a hundred years, and the machine could not spare her and she could not spare the machine.

He got the glass seat's casing open with Coss's chain-tools and worked the repair the way he worked everything, narrating in the low voice that made rooms go quiet by refusing to be loud. The old woman watched him through the pane without pausing her count.

He gave her the surgeon's read while his hands worked, because it was the only ledger he could keep balanced in a room like this. Half bloom-through, but held — not the stopped, surrendered half of the photographs in Vahid's papers; an argued half, a partitioned half, the garden grown to her collarbone and no further in a hundred years because some will in her had drawn the line and stood on it. The seed's weep ran through the garden-arm the way sap runs through a tapped tree, and her body had organized itself around the tap: the chains were load-bearing, the arm was a fixture, the machine was a prosthesis she had been surgically joined to by economists. She should have been gone decades ago — the bloom-through should have taken her — and she was not gone, and the physician in him put the question to the room in the only form the room allowed: what does a person cost to keep, when the person is the pipe? Then, as he lifted the second clamp, she stopped counting, and looked at him — not at his face, not at his hands, but at his chest, at his coat, at the banked heat riding under it — and read him the way you read a ledger, top to bottom, in one glance.

"Little ninth," she said.

Dar's hands went still on the clamp.

The old woman's face creased into something too tired and too warm to be a smile, and she said it again, to make sure he had it, in the tone of family — the tone Suri's counting had, the tone Ilay's drawings had, a register Dar had not heard aimed at him since a salt-glass door had tapped out a lullaby's opening bars. "Little ninth. You came the long way round. I felt you pass — felt her pass — two cities back and more. I told her: mind the light, he's day-walking." The chains took up the small motion of her shrug. "She's shy in the sun. We all were."

"You're a carrier," Dar said, and his voice came out in the register he kept for operating, because the alternative registers were not equal to the room.

"I'm the first carrier. First and last, the seed's whole account runs through me." She flexed the garden-arm inside the glass, easy, unafraid of it, a hand in a familiar glove. "Third seed. Gullet's mine. I was a net-mender on the carcass-fleet when she rooted in me, and I thought I was dying, and I was — but slow. The bloom came up the harbor and the town grew rich on it and I grew strange with it, and when the harvest men came they cut the bloom-stem like a tooth and could not take the root, because the root was me. So they built the machine." She looked around the chamber, the brass, the pipes, the chapel of it, with the unhurried survey of a woman viewing her own headstone and finding the masonry sound. "An engine, they called it. The Cow, the crews called it. And me they chained in, because the weep runs through me and I keep it steady, and a chain is cheaper than wages. I have been the extraction-point of this city for a hundred years, little ninth, and I have counted every cask, and at shift-change, when the counting stops — "

The bells rang, high above, the heavy triple. The works throttled down. And the moan came up through the floor of the chamber and filled it, brass-deep and wet and rising, and up close, inside the thing that made it, it was not a moan at all. It was a call, shaped and specific, a note laid over the dark in one direction only, the way you call a name you have been calling for so long the name has become the breath.

"She cries at shift-change," the old woman said, when it had subsided. "Hundred years now. She cries for her sister." Her eyes came back to Dar and settled there, patient as the chains. "And that's why I'm glad you came, little ninth. I've waited a hundred years to ask a ninth one question."

"Ask," Dar said.

The question, when the old woman finally asked it, was about her sister.
