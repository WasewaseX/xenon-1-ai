# Chapter 50 — The Renderworks

Gullet announced itself by smell, two days before it arrived: rendered fat and hot salt and lamp-oil, a smell like a kitchen the size of a harbor, carried out across the flats on a wind that had nothing left to slow it down. Suri counted the change in the wind's cargo the way she counted everything now, because the counting was hers, and this entry she did not like: wherever they were going burned something at an industrial rate, and there was nothing out there to burn but the route's own produce.

The city came up over the horizon on the third noon, and it was a carcass.

A leviathan's, beached and decades dead, longer than the Saltstairs, its spine a broken ridge against the grey sky — and grown over, through, and out of it, a town. The ribs stood bare for gantries, braced with chain and timber, cask-hoists running up them like arteries. The baleen had been cut and hung in vast grey sheets for walls, and behind the sheets lamps burned at midday, because the works ran all hours and the light was not a byproduct. The light was the product. Gullet sold light. It burned what it rendered and rendered what it burned, and from a distance the whole reef-city glowed along its seams like a fire banked inside a whale.

"They render it here," Suri said. "Lowmark's skim. It comes up the coast by barge-chain and it ends here, in the open, and they sell it as light and glue and tallow, and the chits we found in the granary ash are this city's handwriting." She looked at Dar. "The priests were milking a god. This place is the dairy."

Beyond the reef, at the world's edge, the fog-sea had come back into view — a grey line of it, low and receding, the first water they had seen since Ashkarad — and it kept its distance from the carcass the way a customer keeps a distance from a shop whose debts are visible. Suri filed the geography under one heading: the city had been built where the sea used to feed it, and now that the sea was leaving, the city had begun to feed on itself, and called the difference profit.

The gate was a whale's jaw, hinged with chain, and past it the party walked into the loudest quiet Suri had ever audited. The Vigil had whispered; Gullet roared — chain, hammer, steam from the render-pans, the barge-bells — and underneath the roar the people made no sound of their own at all. They had tally-brands instead of voices, most of them: a ruled line inked and re-inked along the inner forearm, notched at each quarter, the way you notch a stick you do not trust yourself to remember. Bloom-debt, the tally-master's clerk told them at the gate, in the tone of a man reciting weather. Sign at fourteen, work it off at the pans, interest every salt-moon. The clerk's own arm carried four ruled lines, close-ruled, like a ledger that had been rewritten because the first entry would not die.

Suri looked at the lines and felt the old cold, the Saltstairs cold, the one she had carried since a hospital sold her a carbon graft at nineteen and let her keep six weeks of her own skin. She had thought the Church held the patent on this. The Church had merely been the local office.

And under the cold, all that first day, sat the other thing she did not say out loud: the leaf in her ledger's spine, the promissory she had signed in another city's counting-annex to buy three heartbeats of a dead woman's face. Interest at the road's discretion, the terms ran, collected by the heralds — and here was the road's pantry, its rendering floor, the place the route's keep was drawn from, and she had walked into it with her signature already on file. She kept the thought banked. She priced it where she priced her other fears: daily, at carrying volume, to nobody.

Dar's trade opened the doors, the way it always did. The head factor of the Renderworks was a soft grey man called Doane, and Doane had a foot that had been hurting him for a year in the specific way of a man who stands on grating twelve hours a day, and by evening of the first day Dar had him lanced, splinted, and grateful, and Suri had a chair in the counting-house and an afternoon's access to the books. The factor signed the access himself, wincing, because his foot was under contract now and contracts, he believed, ran both ways.

By the second morning Dar had a ward. It assembled itself the way his wards always assembled, without a posting and without a fee: a bench behind the baleen of a side-street, an oilcloth, Vell on the stool with the queue's slates. Gullet's injuries had their own grammar and the grammar was the industry's. Hoist-crushes. Chain-burns. And the one he had not met before this road: bloom-scald, the skin's protest against spilled casks of the living tallow, which did not blister the way burns blistered but frosted — the scalded patch going grey and fine-feathered overnight, petals almost, reaching, before it cracked. He learned its margins from the workers, who knew them better than the company's stamped surgeon did. He learned, too, that the company surgeon was a bone-setter with a price-list, and that the price-list was collected at the company store against company scrip, so that in Gullet even your wounds were invoiced to your debt.

The Lily ran dim here, dimmer than anywhere on the road. The whole city was light — render-pans, lamp-strings, the export itself burning in the open — and she withdrew behind his sternum to her deepest banked register and kept her own counsel, and watched the spilled bloom in the scald-ward the way a poor man watches another man's bread. Dar fed her nightly in the black of the cellar Doane let them have, and each dawn the frost on the cellar's low ceiling pointed not at Ashkarad now but down — through the reef's floor, through the carcass's chest, at whatever wept and burned and begged at the center of the works.

The books ran to three rooms. Suri started with the newest and worked back, and Vell came with her, chalk stub out, and by the time the second bell rang Suri had the shape of the whole reef in her head, and the shape was a closed loop with no exit marked.

The book of the light, the company called its payroll ledger, and the book of the light recorded, on the credit side, wages: scrip, struck in lead, spendable at the company store and nowhere else on the reef. On the debit side it recorded bloom-debt: the signing-sum advanced to every fourteen-year-old for the family's keep, against future labor, at interest compounding every salt-moon. Suri sampled the ledger at random, forty entries, and ran each one forward the way you run a fever, and in forty samples she did not find a single entry that aged out. Wages ate interest and bought keep. Debt renewed at fourteen, because the signing parent's own debt had never cleared, because the scrip only spent at the store, because the store's prices were set by the company, because the interest compounded every salt-moon. She went back six decades in the stacks to check the founding assumption, and the founding assumption was the whole finding: in sixty years of the book of the light, no entry had ever been stamped PAID. Not one. The company had never once recorded a debt as retired, in four generations of a city that did nothing, all day, but retire debt.

"They're not working it off," she told Dar that night, on the factor's own gallery, with the render-yards glowing below. "There's nothing to work it off to. It's not a city with a harvest in it, Mehran. It's a harvest with a city in it. Four generations of people mortgaged to the rendering of themselves, and the rendering itself — " she tapped the chit-spikes visible through the gallery glass, each spike crowned with flower-stamped tallies — "goes up the route. The company pays the heralds. Sustain the route, the chits said, and now I've seen what sustains it: this. Light, glue, tallow, and a town that pays for the privilege of producing all three."

Vell chalked the audit entire on the factor's back wall in her vertical hand — totals, decades, the zero at the bottom of the paid column — a second copy, a clerk's insurance, filed in the enemy's own counting-house. Doane watched her do it. He stood in the doorway with his splinted foot and his soft grey face and watched the whole ruin of his city get copied out in chalk, and he signed nothing, and stopped nothing, and when Vell finished and washed her hands he said, quietly, to the ledger-room air: "My grandfather's line is on the third spike. Four notches." And he went back to his desk, because a bell was ringing and the reef does not stop.

The party's third day in Gullet was the day Suri learned what the city did at shift-change.

She was on the gallery again, closing the audit, when the bells rang — a heavy triple pattern, different from the hour-bells — and the reef changed gears. The day-crews came off the pans, the night-crews went on, the hoists slowed, and in the gap, with a thousand small sounds falling away all at once, the city's heart became audible.

It came up out of the ground. Out of the carcass. A moan, long and rising, brass-deep and wet at the same time, the sound of something enormous drawing breath to speak and getting no further than the breath. It rolled through the gantries and the baleen walls and up through the soles of Suri's boots into her back teeth, and it did not stop so much as subside, the way an animal subsides when the effort of crying costs more than the crying is worth.

The whole reef flinched, in its separate ways. The night-crews did not look up. The day-crews did not look at each other. A woman at the cistern put her palm flat over her tally-brand for a moment, the way you press a bruise, and went back to her pail.

"The Cow," said the tally-master's clerk, passing behind her with his lamps. He said it without shame and without introduction, the way you say rain. "She moans at shift-change. Always has. Foremen say she's begging."

"Begging," Suri repeated.

"For feed. For rest. Depends which foreman." He shifted his lamps. "She's the engine. She's what the light comes out of. You get used to it."

He moved off into the roar, and Suri stood at the rail and listened to the moan go down and the works come back up to their full indifferent volume, and she thought about the Vigil, about a city that had spent two centuries learning exactly how much sound a sleeping god could bear — and about a reef-city that had never learned anything at all, that had built a light-industry on top of something alive and chained it where the heart should go. She opened her ledger to the column she had ruled on a rope-walk in another city, under the heading she now maintained like a debt of her own, and she wrote:

THE ENGINE BEGS AT SHIFT-CHANGE. ENGINES DO NOT BEG. SOMETHING DOWN THERE IS OWED.

Below the entry, after a moment, watching the render-yards burn their own export into the dark:

AND THE WHOLE TOWN IS PAYING US TO LISTEN TO IT.
