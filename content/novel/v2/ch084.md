# Chapter 84 — Collateral Returned

The tide that had come uphill to deliver the wain went out again before dawn, and this time it did not come back. It left the Reclaim's great reliquary-wain seated against the tide-gates like a creditor who had finally stopped knocking — barnacled to its axles, furred with grey frost that pointed, every petal of it, inland — and it left Ashkarad holding the Gardener's deadline in its mouth. *You have until the tide forgets to come back.* The tide had come back wrong, delivered the wain like a coffin on a shoulder, and forgotten itself on schedule. The deadline was spent.

The white flag went up the Saltstairs road at the ninth bell.

Wen walked in front of it the way a filing date walks into a debtor's week — unhurried, expected by the arithmetic if not by anyone's wishes. A century old, half-bloomed, the boy who had fallen carrying the ninth seed and been kept alive by the Gardener's books as her first failed key: he came up through the bloom-fog with two white-gloved auditors behind him and a glass case between them, and he stopped at the clinic's threshold under the darkened lamps, courteous as ever, courteous as a filing date.

"The Warden of the Reclaim," he said, "returns what the books held."

Dar came down the stairs and stood in the doorway, and Suri stood one stair below him with her ledger open, and Vell chalked the visit into the record before anyone had said a word, because Vell had been a counting-house clerk in a city that was eaten and had opinions about courtesy.

"You'll forgive the paperwork," Wen said. "It was collected during the sealing, on a signed lien. Collateral of the ninth seed's account. The account has been reconciled." One of the auditors stepped forward and set the case on the threshold stone with both gloved hands, the way a verger sets a relic, and stood back. "Collateral restored. The books never wanted your heart, cutter. They only ever wanted it where it could be counted."

The heart was in the case.

It was smaller than Dar remembered, and it was beating. His original heart — the one he had been born with, the one that had failed at nineteen and been regrown in Vey's vats to hang between candles as an offer he had refused — beating in glass on his own threshold, in the dark, in a room where every lamp had been hooded for a year because the thing in his chest could not afford the light. It beat the way it had always beaten, which was to say slightly out of order, the arrhythmia he had grown up with filed away in the grain of the muscle, and it beat — Vahid would confirm it within the hour with his brass tube and his mended spectacles — in threes.

"One heart, three chambers, counting back," Vahid said, ear to the glass, one hand steadying it because he had only the one hand now. "Counting back in threes. That's not your rhythm, boy. That's the deep's."

"That," Wen said, "is the courtesy."

Suri did not look up from her ledger. "We have a receipt," she said. "Signed, sealed, dated the night of the sealing. *Received — one heart, human, original, beating. Held against account.* My copy." She turned the page so he could see it. "It doesn't say anything about a courtesy."

"The receipt covers custody," Wen said. "The courtesy covers contents. Your professor is holding a key, courier. I'd ask him to look again, and this time to ask what it opens."

Vahid looked again. He was a long time about it — long enough that Milla came down from the census desk with her transcription book and stood at the rail, and long enough that the fog pressed at the hooded windows like something reading over their shoulders — and when he straightened up his face had the particular stillness he saved for findings that would otherwise be shouted.

"It's been re-keyed," he said. "The re-growth was Church work and it kept Church time, whatever else it did. This isn't Church time. Someone has taught it the deep's meter — the three-count, the backward fall — grafted it into the beat, muscle by muscle, a recital learned by a heart. And there's a verse in it." His thumb moved along the glass. "The claiming-song. The water variant. The one the heralds' hymn only rehearses. It's primed in there like a charge in a well."

"Say the rest," Dar said.

"The trigger is feeding." Vahid did not look at him when he said it, which was its own kind of mercy. "The key is set to the deep's rhythm, and the deep keeps time one beat behind you. The next time the ninth seed feeds — the next supper you pay her in the dark — the resonance comes due. The heart sings the claiming-song, and the water carries it. Every cistern under the Saltstairs. The pump-works. The ration lines. Every throat in Ashkarad drinks its own activation."

The city's water was Gavel's now, held as function and never flesh, and the cisterns beneath the Saltstairs were older than any of the ledgers — a hundred drowned rooms where the district kept its drinking and its children had always been warned not to swim. The heart of a debt-bound surgeon, primed like a charge, waiting on the appetite of the seed that had replaced it.

"Return it," Dar said.

"That would defeat the reconciliation," Wen said, gently, as if correcting a column. "The collateral is restored. Restored means *returned to the account.* The account is you." He inclined his head, and there was — Dar had learned to read it by now, the centenarian's terrible patience — no threat in him at all. Threat would have been cheaper. "The Warden's regret, cutter. I argued for a warmer instrument. I was overruled by a filing cabinet."

They let him walk out under his flag, because the flag was the only mercy anyone had budgeted for, and the fog took him the way the tide had taken the city's sea: completely, and without an apology.

Suri closed her ledger on the receipt and stood at the rail with her ruined graft arm held against her, doing the arithmetic she could not stop doing, out loud, flat, counted.

"It's a lien on appetite," she said. "That's the shape of it. They didn't send a fuse, they sent a *term* — the bomb isn't aimed at a day, it's aimed at a supper. You can't starve her, Mehran, she's your second pulse. You can't feed her in the open, light taxes her. So every dark room in this city where you pay her what she's owed is a firing lever, and there's no filing this one under *never.*" She looked at the case a long moment. "They learned that from us. The heralds watch what we ration and price the copy. Censers, water, blood — we taught them our own bookkeeping, and they've written it back to us as a weapon."

"Then we retire the collateral," Dar said.

"That's tomorrow's column," Suri said, and ruled it, and wrote the heading before she went up to the war-room: *THE HEART — OPTIONS.*

They put the case on the cold cellar table, and the argument about what to do with it began in the room above and leaked down the stairs like everything else in the clinic leaked.

Dar stayed below with the heart, because it was his — that was the whole of the jurisdiction, and everyone in the building knew it — and because Vahid had said, on his way up to lecture the war-room on pyres and lime-kilns, the thing that had sent the rest of the household up out of the cellar: "A heart that beats in glass can be listened to. Before anyone burns it, someone who knows the song should hear what it's been taught." He had looked at Dar with his salt-scoured spectacles gone white in the lamplight. "And no one else in this city is going to know the difference. That's the price of what you sold, boy. You're the only ear left that can catch a forgery in that voice."

The Lily knew he was near the case before he was within a yard of it.

She came up through him the way she came up through him on the bad nights — not pain, exactly, but inventory, a full stock-taking of everything in the body she lived in — and then the craving arrived, and it arrived the way the doctrine of Vahid's lectures had always promised it would when Dar bled near her: total, ancient, and invoice-precise. The case held his blood. Twenty-seven years of it, regrown in a vat by the woman who had wanted to be the city's heart, and every drop of it was meat that had once been his, and the seed in his chest wanted it the way a drowned man wants the surface — gums aching, mouth flooding iron, the black lace stirring along his forearms like a reader leaning toward a page.

"no," Dar said aloud, to his own chest, in his own cellar, and heard how it sounded, and did not care.

The rule was older than the craving and it had never once failed him: blood craved when Dar bleeds. He drew the cellar lamp into the deepest corner until the room went almost honest dark, took his knife, and opened his own palm over the floor-drain — given, in the dark, the way she had always been fed, the first price he had ever agreed to pay her — and the craving turned like a ledger re-filed. She drank what he offered through the dark, and what she wanted beyond the offering she did not press, and by the time the hum in his ribs settled she was quiet again, fed and, he thought, ashamed, if a seed could be ashamed, of what she had wanted and from whom.

Which was when the heart began to sing.

Faint. Through the glass. A sound like a reliquary remembering — and Dar knew the song before he knew he knew it, four notes first, then the turn, then the long descending rest: the lullaby. The lullaby in a woman's voice, thin as memory through glass, and the woman's voice was a voice his bones filed without asking him — *his mother's voice* — the timbre of it, the breath before the phrase, the small catch on the third note that he had paid a decade of a life to look at once and had been given a face that was *wrong*.

And it was not her voice. That was the finding, and he made it the way he made every finding now, against his own hope, with instruments that had been sold and resold: the real one was held in a room below the world, in the common property of a door shared between two, unbuyable, unownable, beyond the reach of any market the Gardener could clear. This voice was assembled. This was the echo of the sale — every cleared slip, every memory of music the Gardener had bought off his counter-wall and out of his own chest, stitched into a composite and taught to a muscle. His mother's voice the way a forged stamp is his mother's signature: correct in every line, and never once hers.

The heart begged in it. That was the mechanism under the mechanism — under the re-keying, under the primed verse, under the three-count — a heart that beat in glass had been made to *beg*, in the lullaby, in a voice built out of everything he had ever spent, and it begged the way the fleet's engines had learned to beg at shift-change, and it was begging *him*, and the worst of it, the part he would not put in any report, was that for four full notes his body leaned toward the glass the way a child leans toward a kitchen door.

Dar closed the case. He latched it, both latches, and put his uninjured hand flat on the lid, and made the only entry the finding allowed.

"It's not her," he told the dark, in the flat triage voice he used on the dying so their families could stand up under it. "It was never her. It's the invoice for what we sold."

Under his palm, patient as interest, his original heart went on beating in threes, counting back, and sang to the cellar in the voice of a mother he had never had, and waited — primed, keyed, restored to its account — for the next time the ninth seed would be fed.
