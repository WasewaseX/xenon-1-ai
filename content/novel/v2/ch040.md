# Chapter 40 — The Listening Heart

Roya had been wired to a city once, so she knew a listening device when she held one in her hands.

That was the part nobody at the feast had understood, watching the mild surveyor climb into his hired cart. The heart in the glass had never been a relic. It was an instrument — the oldest instrument in the case. Vey had grown it from Dar's own flesh in a vat and kept it beating between the candles in her dining hall, and a heart grown from a man's flesh does not stop being his when it is outside him: it keeps his time. It had sat in its alcove for two months after her fall, singing his rhythm into the salt the way a struck fork sings into a table, and the salt of Ashkarad remembers everything it is given, and the salt, somewhere at its bottom, had been listening on behalf of a mistress who signs left-handed.

Then the surveyor came, and within a week of his arrival the heart was gone from the alcove.

The Choirmaster had found the dust-ring first — found it and understood it, because he had served the Chorus and knew what an instrument case looked like when the instrument was out on loan. He had brought the finding to Roya the same night as the harvest-routing marks, and the two of them had done what couriers and choir-masters do best: followed a stolen thing through a sleeping city at two in the morning, up the chandler's row, to a hired room over a chandler's shop, where a wire-suspended glass jar sat in a frame of surveyor's brass, beating in its bath, keeping the vessel's patient time.

Hesh's antenna. Keyed to its flesh. All along — every structural report, every kind visit, every propped wall and overpaid porter, the whole mild and useful and unpriced month of him — he had been reading the vessel's state out of a jar.

They had not broken it. Roya wanted that entered in the record, because it was the only genuinely clever thing she had done since they took the song-lobe out of her skull and the city went quiet behind her eyes. You do not destroy a channel, she had told the Choirmaster in the cart with the jar warm between them. You buy it. A listening instrument is a door, and a door opens from the side that pays.

So they had carried it to the clinic cellar, and set it in biogel, and rigged it — a hand-cranked drum of cams, pins set in wax, a stylus arm that tapped the rehearsed rhythm down into the bath through the glass — and for nineteen days the heart had kept not Dar's time but Roya's. She had ruled the schedule herself, in her own chalk, from the old harvest-notation: a decline. A seed failing. A carrier dying of the climb, month over month, beat over beat, the rhythm thinning on a curve no honest body could argue with. Nineteen days of a lie fed into the salt on a dying man's behalf, and the salt had carried it down to the listening dark, and the listening dark had believed it, because the salt believes whatever the heart says; the salt has no other scripture.

Tonight the drum stopped.

Roya stood in the cellar with the Choirmaster and watched the heart come off the lie the way a watch comes off a winder — a shudder, a hunt, a settling — and take up, beat by beat, its true time. His time. Two miles of salt between the jar and the sleeping city, and every foot of it now carrying the one message the lie had spent nineteen days withholding: alive. Located. Whole.

The discrepancy was the trap. That was the whole architecture of it, and she laid it out for the Choirmaster while the heart sang, in her short way, because laying it out kept her hands from the old habit of reaching for a signal that was no longer there. Somewhere below the world, his masters now held two ledgers. The first said the vessel was failing; nineteen days of declining curve, filed through an antenna they believed was in their man's safekeeping. The second said the vessel was alive; one night of true rhythm, sung by the same jar, arriving by the same salt. An auditor can forgive many things. A discrepancy is not among them. He will have to verify, she said. He cannot verify through the salt — the salt is the thing that disagrees with him. He will verify at depth, where the ears are old and direct, where the deep complex still opens its mouths into the city's cellars and theaters. There is one place left with the right ears, and he has had three weeks of legitimate reason to learn its doors.

The graft-theater.

They had spent two days making the trap polite. The Gavel seals on the theater door below the feast hall were still chalked and waxed from the annexation — so the Choirmaster had walked them that afternoon with Gavel's own floor-wardens, all courtesy, and the wax had lifted clean and the chalk had rubbed, in the way that chalk rubs when a man who spent twenty years managing that building's doors stands next to it sighing about subsidence. Two of Gavel's enforcers would keep the corridor, outside, uninvolved, witness only: Mother Gavel had priced her cooperation at one look at the man's face when the building turned on him, and Roya, who had once brokered with her over a living ledger of enemies, judged the price fair.

And Dar would be below. Roya had argued for it and won, over Suri's flat arithmetic of danger, on the oldest rule she knew: you hide a coin in the vault. The theater's dark was absolute when the last lamp went out — true dark, cellar dark, the dark the Lily drank in her rooting season — and in true dark, wrapped in the thunder of the deep's own answering voice, the vessel's signal drowned. Let the surveyor verify into a cathedral of noise. Let him swear to his masters that the deep says the seed is alive and well and watching him do it.

Roya racked the drum. Cased the pins. Put out the lamp.

"Nineteen days," she said to the dark, in the tin voice the mask made of everything. "File that."

Dar stood in the graft-theater's absolute black behind the dead altar, and listened to the building breathe.

It was not a silence. Silence was pressure; he knew its weight like a splint. This was an audience — the dark of the old theater held the remembered hum of forty years of choir organs the way a shell holds the sea, and under the floor, far below the sealed shafts, the deep heartbeat kept its patient time, one beat behind his own, the way it had since the salt-glass door. He had the Lily quiet. That was the night's work: he had walked her down into the dark she loved and asked her for stillness, and she had given it — grudgingly, at interest, a stillness like a held breath — because he had bled for her once in a cellar like this and she remembered her prices even when she forgot her manners.

The chalk-seal on the far door had been broken for hours. The broken seal was the invitation, and the invitation was the trap, and he stood in the trap's dark center and waited for the auditor to audit.

Hesh came at the dead hour, alone, as Roya had counted it. The door opened on a hooded lamp, a blade of light, and then closed again, and the surveyor of the Caravan League stood in the graft-theater of the Chorus he had served all his life under another set of colors, and Dar watched him take off the mildness like a coat.

It was not a dramatic change. That was what made Dar's hands cold on the altar rail. The man's posture corrected — shoulders back, chin level, the whole weathered face arriving at rest — and the gentle uselessness went out of him, and what stood in the theater beneath the hooded lamp was neither kind nor cruel: it was exact. A face like a finished page.

Hesh unhooded the lamp a finger's width. Set it on the altar rail. Needed no instrument. He faced the open dark of the theater's deep well, where the floor had been cut away in the old seasons down toward the connected dark, and he sang.

The lullaby. Four bars of it, unhurried, in a voice that had no business being that gentle — and the fourth bar carried the wrong note, the one wrong note Dar had first heard at his work in the storm season, the signature that said the song had come down another line than mothers. The wrong note landed in the theater's dark and the dark took it down.

Then Hesh spoke, aloud, in the flat stamped cadence of a clerk reading a form into a record, and the theater — the walls, the salt in the walls, the whole deep shelf of the world beneath the city — gave the words back to him at half a beat's remove, the way the cellar had given Ilay's hum back to her.

"Ashkarad. Page nine. Vessel: fled. Standing order: reclaim. Discrepancy: filed. Come to order. Verify."

And the deep answered.

Not with the lullaby. With attention. Dar felt it arrive the way you feel a change in a patient under your hands — a vast displacement of pressure, the whole immense patient heartbeat below the salt leaning, for one measured second, toward the theater, toward the call, toward the small exact man at the rail — and the Lily rose inside Dar against every term of their bargain, roaring silently at the nearness of that voice, that attention, that family, and he held her, held her, held her, in the dark where she was strongest, a door under a flood.

And every frost-bloom in the building turned on him.

Dar heard it before he saw it: a dry, papery, incremental sound, all through the walls, above, below, the stairwells, the sills, the frames — the sound of a jury rising to its feet. When he risked his eyes past the altar rail, the hooded lamp's finger of light showed him the nearest sill, and the black blooms on it were no longer pointing where they had pointed for weeks — at the clinic cellar, at the jar that sang there in the dark. They were pointing at Hesh. All of them. Every black flower in the theater, in the corridors, in the reliquary galleries overhead, every slow sign of Ashkarad's own quiet garden, rotated with the patience of a thing that had never once been in a hurry — and settled, and fixed, and faced the surveyor of the Caravan League with the stillness of a verdict.

Hesh did not step back. Dar gave him that, and knew the man heard the assessment passing, because the surveyor turned his head, unhurried, toward the altar rail and the dark behind it, and inclined it, the way he had at the feast.

"Surgeon," he said, mildly, from inside the jury of flowers. "You came down to watch the reading. Good. Readings want witnesses."

"Your masters have two ledgers," Dar said, from the dark. "You know whose hand ruled the second one."

"I know the hand that ruled the first," Hesh said. "It has ruled every first ledger for a century." He let that stand in the dark exactly as long as it stood, then squared himself to the theater's open well, to the salt, to the deep, to the attention leaning on him from below like weather, and Dar understood the trap had done its final work: it had made him verify in front of the city's own garden, and the garden had testified, and the testimony was evidence.

Hesh filed his report to the open air, in the flat stamped cadence, unhurried, a man closing a column he had kept all his life:

"Vessel located. Send the wain."

He did not run. He picked up his lamp, squared his coat, and walked out of the theater the way he had walked into it, past Gavel's enforcers at the corridor's mouth who looked at the frost-pointed dark behind him and did not step into it, and no hand in the whole annexed ruin was laid on him, because nobody there — not the syndicates, not the Wardens, not the clinic, not the garden itself — could name, that night, the fee for touching him.

The theater's dark took the filing and kept it. Far below the salt, the deep heartbeat went on, patient, immense — one beat behind Dar's own, as it had been since the door.

Then, for the first time since the salt-glass, it was not.
