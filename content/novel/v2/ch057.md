# Chapter 57 — Who Kept You

The wrongness started, as far as Ilay could hear, an hour after they brought the two-hearted man back up from the vaults, and it started in his rhythm.

She was in the common room with the day's drawings when it came through the floor: the two clocks she knew better than her own breath, the strong one and the copyist one beat behind, and the copyist slipping. Not much. A quarter of a beat. The way a line slips when the hand that rules it goes unsteady, and then it caught, and then it slipped again, and under the slipping — she put her ear to the boards, because the boards had never lied to her — under the slipping there was a new sound in the deep music, a sound that had never been in it before in her whole hearing. A crackling. Soft, vast, patient, under the hum like fire under a floor.

The city's music leaned around it. People's sleeping rhythms two streets off turned in their beds. Down in the vaults, the risen voice kept its enormous patient time, and the crackle ran under it, and the crackle was coming up the shaft.

By the middle watch he was worse, and the clinic did the only thing its books allowed: it took him back down. She heard the expedition assemble in the dark — the counting woman's voice rising into the register she kept for emergencies, flat, doubled, calling the Professor down from his cot and the bearers to the door, the Professor's ruling shouted down a stairwell that a contact cut at a distance was a line left open, and lines left open burned — and Ilay stood at the top of the cellar stair in the dark with her hand on the rail, counting the wrong, and the wrong added up. The copyist slipping. The crackle spreading. The two-hearted man's voice coming up the stairwell in broken filing order, words set down in the wrong columns, the counting woman's name given once to the Professor and the Professor's given to no one, and Vahid's voice cracking on the word ash, the word corridors, the word re-filing, and the sound beneath everything — the sound under the sound, the one Ilay had been born listening to — going on crackling, like a house burning somewhere no water had ever been piped to.

She did not decide to go down. Decisions were the things you did when your hearing had failed to price something, and her hearing had not failed; it had concluded. She took the coal-chalk off the desk and her boots off the peg, and she went out through the kitchen where Milla Ferun sat the late desk with her clerk's shawl and her salt-stained fingers, and Milla looked up from the census box, and looked at her, and did not ask, because the whole clinic by then was one held breath and Milla Ferun had spent a career learning which silences were questions and which were not. She stood, and she took the lamp off the desk and walked her out through the coal-yard to the tithe-clerk's door, and she held the light while Ilay went down into the route that had her name on it, and the last thing she said, in her square clerical voice, was the only benediction the city had left: "Count your steps."

Seven hundred and eleven. She knew the number the way she knew her own name; she had drawn it before anyone had walked it, from the singing alone, when the shaft had been the place the city's song went into the ground and did not come back. She counted as she descended, and the walls rang under her palms, and at step four hundred the crackle was no longer under the music. It was the music, a whole register of it, ashfall and burning heard through a hundred feet of salt, and at five hundred she passed the plate with its nailed warning — DO NOT COUNT THE HEARTBEATS — and she counted the heartbeats too, because someone had to; the warning had been written by people who thought counting was the danger, and Ilay had listened to the deep her entire life, and she knew the difference between counting and keeping.

The lamp came down the shaft behind her at step six hundred — a small steady circle of it, and the brass mask above it, and no words, just the light following the girl down, which was its own kind of signature on the terms.

The vault antechamber was lit by one hooded lamp and full of the sound of a man being audited against his will. The two-hearted man sat against the salt-glass door with his coat around him and his eyes open, and his eyes were the worst of it — open and working and wrong, the look of a man watching his own rooms take weather. Grey dust stood in the seams of his knuckles and at his temples, fine as flour, and where his breath went out the lamplight found motes of it falling upward, settling into the air of the vault as if the air were shelving. The counting woman knelt at his side with two fingers on his wrist, calling his pulse aloud, flat, unflagging, the way you call a rope down a well: sixty-two, sixty-two, sixty-one, sixty-two — and the second rhythm answering, one behind, a quarter-slipped, catching, slipping. The Professor stood over him with his spectacles off, holding them, one-handed, saying nothing, which from Vahid was the loudest sound in the room.

And against the door itself, palm flat on the salt-glass, knelt the woman from the mud, the petal at her collarbone spread wide and trembling, holding the line the only way she knew, the root drinking from the deep through the glass, giving the burning somewhere to go that was not the man.

He was re-filing. Ilay could hear it now she was in the room — the ash from the Sister's sky settling through the corridors of him, and where it settled, things were being written over. Not spent. The Professor had said that twice already, she'd heard it from the stair: not spent, nothing spent, the ledger isn't being drawn on, it's being burned — re-filed into cinders, rooms taking shapes the fire preferred, and the two-hearted man sat in the middle of it with terrible patience, giving the count woman his pulse and giving the ash his corridors and keeping, with the last of his surgeon's will, one hand flat on his own sternum, holding his own door.

Ilay crossed the vault to him. The salt rang under her feet, half-step down, and the risen voice filled the dark above the door, and the crackle ran beneath it, and she did what she had come down seven hundred and eleven steps to do, because there was no other currency in her that the deep had ever accepted.

She hummed.

Not the lullaby's opening — everyone knew the opening; the opening was the Lily's, the mother's, the first bars the petal had tapped through the glass. She hummed the second verse. The verse that had come to her the way the word RECLAIM had come, leaking up from under the world's door, months of it, note by note, until she had it whole and had never once sung it aloud because it was not hers. The verse the two-hearted man had brought back from the road in his own head — she saw his face do it the moment she began, saw the recognition land through the ash, saw his mouth move around the words the road — the face — the three heartbeats — the verse no living person should have had, because it had died with the world it was written in.

The vault went still.

Not quiet. Still. The risen voice did not fade; it stopped, entire, between one beat and the next, the way a hand stops over a page, and the crackle under it stopped with it, and the salt stopped ringing, and the frost on the salt-glass door bloomed out in one long ring from Isra's palm to the walls, and even the lamplight seemed to spend itself more slowly, and the two-hearted man's second rhythm came home to its place one beat behind his own, exact, re-locked, and the ash stopped falling in him — she heard it stop, a whole weather quitting its fall mid-air — and the deep, the risen sister, the enormous patient thing behind the glass, held itself utterly, completely still.

Ilay hummed the verse to its end, because you do not stop in the middle of a name, and the last note went out of her into the salt and down.

Then the stone asked its question.

It did not come through the air. It came through the walls, through the salt, up out of the geology of the city, in the dead language she understood without hearing, in a voice like a ledger being read out in the dark after a hundred years of silence, and it used the two-hearted man's ribs the way a cantor uses a rail.

"You are stock," the Sister said, through the stone, gently, the way you tell a debtor what they already know. "Who kept you?"

And then, before Ilay had drawn breath to answer, before any of them had, the gentleness went out of it and the question filled the vault the way the heartbeat filled it, from every wall at once, floor and salt and door and the seven hundred and eleven steps behind them, and it was not gentle at all, and it was the oldest voice in the world:

"WHO KEPT YOU?"

Isra's petal stood straight out from her collarbone. The counting woman had stopped counting. The Professor had his one hand braced on the wall as if the city had shifted under him. The two-hearted man sat very still with ash in the seams of his hands, listening the way she was listening, and nobody answered, because nobody could, because the answer was not theirs.

Ilay put her hand flat on the wall. She felt the question still in the stone, held, like a held breath, like a tally waiting on its total, and she answered it honestly, because the hearing was hers, and the truth was the only thing she had ever been able to afford.

"The Church did," she said.

The quiet that came down then was not the quiet of an ended thing. Ilay had heard silences all her life — the silence where the singing used to be, the silence of dead choirs, the loud one the whole city lived inside since the Blacktide — and this was none of them. This was a hundred years of counting stopping mid-column. This was grief with the size of a world in it, arriving through forty feet of salt into the walls of a salt city, and the music in the walls — the deep music, the patient one, the one that had never once in her life left her alone — went quiet with something like grief, and the salt under her palm went cold as a stone in a wall that has finally been told what it is holding up.

Nobody spoke. The lamp burned. The two-hearted man breathed, sixty-two, sixty-two, sixty-two, and the copyist behind it held its place, exact, one beat down, and the vault, the door, the dark beyond it — all of it stayed utterly still, the way a room stays still when someone in it has finally been told.

After a while, Isra took up her slate and wrote, slowly, with effort, the chalk dragging, and turned it first to Ilay, and then, because it concerned them all, to the room:

SHE SAYS: KEPT — NO ONE HAS EVER SAID IT FROM THE OUTSIDE.

Ilay read it twice. Kept. The Church had kept them the way a till keeps coin — counted, graded, filed where the hymns were wanted. The clinic kept people the way a wall keeps out weather, with no column for it. She had never thought about the two meanings of the word until the deep asked its question through the stone, and now she found she could not put it down: that the same word, in the same tongue, priced both, and only one of the two had ever asked what the keeping cost the kept.

The lamp burned lower. The salt held its cold. And far above them, through seven hundred and eleven steps of ringing dark, the city went on sleeping inside the grief of the oldest thing in the world, which had just learned what had become of her blood.
