# Chapter 89 — The Standing Count

The fleet stopped being an audit in the last hour before dawn, and became, in the fleet's own bookkeeping, an *entry.*

They came up from the tide-gates along the old canal-works, the route their heralds had scouted a season ago, and they did not come as skirmishers. They came as a procession — clerks first, then auditors two by two, then the herald-chorus in files too straight to be anything but liturgy — and behind them, dragged on rollers over the dry canal-bed by two hundred pairs of hands and one patient tide's worth of borrowed arithmetic, came the wain's engine.

Dar watched it come up the Marrowworks' loading slope and knew it before anyone told him, because Suri, counting at his shoulder, went quiet in the middle of a number.

It was a brass-and-glass womb the size of a tithe-barge, furred with grey frost, hung in a cage of leviathan rib, and it was moaning. Not mechanically. *Begging* — the shift-change moan of a reef-city's heart, the sound Suri had audited in another life and filed under ENGINES DO NOT BEG. SOMETHING DOWN THERE IS OWED. The Cow of Gullet, or the Cow's seed-core, torn out on the round and caged in the wain's belly: the engine-seed of a pruned city, carried here the way the fleet carried everything, as an exhibit and an instrument.

"They weaponized it," Vahid said, one-handed, glasses flashing in the hooded lamps of the shaft-head gallery. "A seed that has spent decades learning to sing to a chained woman is aimed at our vault-mouth to sing the claiming-song into the deep. Force the lock with a choir, instead of a ram." His jaw set. "And a conductor to beat time."

The conductor stood on the loading slope with his back to the city, and Dar's body knew him before his mind caught up — the bone-plate mask, smooth as a skull, from a prologue of blood and salt: the captain who had tasted Dar's blood on the night of the theft while Dar bled out on the dock plates. *It chose,* the captain had said, to no one, to the choir, to the dark. *As it always does. The choir must be complete.* Two years, and the man had never stopped performing the liturgy. Behind him the herald-chorus stood in ranks of forty, mouths open before the downbeat, throats the fleet had carried from city to city like tools, and below them the slope held the last thing Dar had expected the fleet to respect: a line. The census's fighting column — quarry crews, dock-gangs, couriers with their counter-weighted chains grounded, picks planted like pikes — holding the loading slope's edge at a walk's distance, not swinging, not shouting, spending nothing. Suri had trained them in one doctrine and one doctrine only, and they held it now in the fog: *you are not there to win a fight; you are there to make the fight unaffordable.* The bone-masked captain mounted the engine's cage with a conductor's baton of white bone in one white-gloved hand, raised the herald-chorus with the other, and gave them the downbeat of the claiming-hymn's activation verse — the finding-song, the waking-song, the tune the Church had spent forty years and a nursery teaching into stock blood.

The verse rolled down the shaft like water finding a drain.

And Ashkarad, which had spent a year learning what its own chest was for, answered with the only thing the city had ever made that the Church had no entry for.

The Standing Count had been a census line once — *who will sing, who will fight, who will hide* — and tonight it became what its name had always promised: a count that stood.

They came up out of the streets and the stair-canyons and the ration-lines at the sixth bell, every soul above the vault, and they did not come to fight. They came to *keep time.* Suri led it, because Suri had always led it, from the gallery rail above the shaft mouth, her ledger closed for once, her ruined graft arm raised like a choirmaster's baton — and Mother Gavel stood beside her with the brass speaking-tube she had handed over weeks ago for the day the city must be counted in, and the day had come, and the tube's brass throat carried the count down the slope and up the walls and into every ear in the fog:

*Count. One. Two. Three. Four.*

Not the hymn's meter. The city's own — hearts, given freely, in rhythm: the counted and the makers and the quarry crews and the ration-lines and the dock-hands and the tenement rows, eleven thousand chests above one vault, each of them paying the only coin the deep had never been able to tax, because it was only ever legal tender when it was *given.* The tube called the numbers. Gavel's voice, flat as a ledger page, did not once lift, and did not once break, and her crews on the slope — the debts she meant to see burned first in any new book — stood in ranks of their own and counted with the rest, because their boss had priced the third way and paid for it in advance.

Below the gallery, the children sang.

Isra had carried Ilay's transcription down from the attic in its oilcloth — *for my grandmother,* the score said on its cover, every verse, the mother's line included — and the chorus-free cohort stood in the antechamber dark above the salt-glass door with Elias's perfect lungs at their head, and they sang the true song into the stone. Not the claiming-hymn's bait. The other thing. The song the garden had taught a nine-year-old through a mile of rock, the song Vahid's medicine had freed their blood to carry, and it went down through the antechamber floor and into the vaults the way water goes into a root.

In the listening-post niche by the down-shaft, Roya mapped a battle she could not hear, exactly as she had mapped the last one, reading the night off Ilay's ears like bearings off a star — CHOIR HOLDING. COUNT HOLDING. ENGINE STRAINING — and beside her Ilay pressed her palms to the stone with tears standing in her river-stone eyes, because the city was singing her song, her transcription, the one she had titled for a grandmother no one had told her she had, and up at the parley-line by the tide-gates, where the fleet's second rank waited under a furled flag, the Warden of the Reclaim stood with one hand flat on his own collarbone scar and listened to a seed's song verified at last, a century late, from outside the walls of the only city that had ever refused him.

And under the streets, the freed voices rose to meet it — Lena Vance's freed voice up every canal and cistern at once, the once-harvested donors in the reliquary gallery, thirty throats the Church had borrowed and returned — and below *them,* below everything, the pedal-tone: the Sister, giving the deep's own under-line to the chord for the first time in a century, the mother's harmony laid under the city's count like a floor under a house.

At the center of it stood Dar, on the gallery above the shaft, holding the crack open.

It was surgery. That was the only way he could hold the account of it in his mind — not mysticism, *surgery:* a hairline wound in the world, running through him at the ninth vein, and he stood at the wound and kept it open the way he had kept chests open on a harvest floor, not one finger's width wider than the bleeding could pay for, because the bloom waited on his other side like a warm garden and the invitation had not expired. The census held him. Nine thousand signatures of agreement pressed through the crack and into the deep below, and the chord that had been a metaphor all year became, at the sixth bell, a *mechanism:* the hymn came down the shaft hunting silence to wake in, and the city above the vault was not silent. You cannot activate what is already singing. The claiming-hymn needs a sleeper, and Ashkarad, every soul above the vault, was awake, and counted, and *counting.*

The herald-chorus sang the activation verse four times, and four times it fell into the Standing Count like a debt into a ledger that was already balanced, and the count did not stumble, and the tube did not lift its voice, and the bone-masked conductor on the engine's cage beat time for a choir that was discovering, bar by bar, that its own song had been redirected.

· · ·

Mother Gavel saw the conductor's end from thirty feet, and filed it the way she filed everything, flat, in order.

She had come down to the slope to hold her crews' ground — an abandoned mine is a mine somebody owns, and tonight it was the city's — and she watched the captain on the cage try to take his choir back. He was good. She could see that even with no ear for it: a liturgy-man to his bones, a failed carrier who had never stopped performing the choosing, holding the claiming-hymn's lead like a man rowing against a tide he had personally ordered to rise. The chord took the heralds' voices out of his hands the way the deep had taken the pick out of a miner's grip — not violence, *redirection* — verse folding into verse, the hymn's own bars turning home like iron filings finding their field, until the claiming-song stood complete in the air over the slope, claiming its singer.

*It chose,* she heard him say, under the mask, in the last bar. *As it always does. The choir must be complete.*

The choir was. It came down on him the way forty years of Chorus procedure came down on everything — thorough, aboveboard, and without an appeal — and when it let go, the herald-chorus stood ragged and silent on the slope, and the bone-masked captain knelt at the engine's cage in the attitude of a man finishing a recital, and the mask was uncracked, and he did not move again, and his white gloves were empty. Not bloody. *Empty.* The choir had finally collected its keeper's voice, and the books had the only entry the deep had ever needed for him: a first-generation herald, a failed key, performed the liturgy to the end. Gavel looked at the gloves for a long moment, and thought of a woman made of black lace on an altar rail, and ruled her column, and said to her overman in the flat voice the fog would carry no further than it had to:

"Mark the line paid. Nobody touches the mask. It's a headstone now, and we don't rob graves in this city. Not anymore."

· · ·

The Cow screamed against the chord at dawn.

It had been moaning all night — begging at the count, begging at the children's song, begging at the pedal-tone rising out of the vaults, one chained engine-seed against eleven thousand freely given hearts — and when the Standing Count rolled over into its second hour, even and unhurried and impossible to bill, the engine-seed stopped begging and *screamed,* brass and glass and frost, the sound of a rendered city's heart being asked to sing against its own kind, and the herald-chorus broke and ran for the canal-works with their verses in their throats, and the wain itself shuddered an inch on its rollers like a coffin adjusting its shoulder.

And up every crack in Ashkarad — the door's hairline, the salt-glass seam, the wall-fissures, the chalked lines of Roya's living maps, the fault in a surgeon's chest — something older than the Church and the burning and the harvest came awake enough to speak. Not the Gardener's voice. Not the Sister's. Not any voice the city's ledgers held. It went up through the stone the way the lullaby had gone down through it for forty years, in the tongue understood without hearing, felt in the teeth, one word, filed everywhere at once, and every pulse-reader in Ashkarad printed it on the same stroke, and Ilay chalked it a foot high on the shaft-head gallery in her careful child's hand because someone had to, and it was not a threat and it was not a plea and it was not addressed to anyone in particular, which was the worst of it.

The Root was awake enough, at last, to say what it was.
