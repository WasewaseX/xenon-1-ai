# Chapter 30 — One Beat Behind

The clinic's roof was the last parish in Ashkarad where a man could stand still without being entered in somebody's book, and even that exemption was eroding. Twice a shift now, a Gavel tallyman marked the alley mouth below, chalk behind his ear, counting the clinic's foot-traffic with the diligence of an auditor who has been told, gently, that the account is not his — yet. Dar stood at the parapet anyway, past midnight, with a shard of slate in his coat pocket and the city breathing under him: eleven hundred heartbeats in the Saltstairs tenements, give or take the ones no one was left alive to count, and beneath all of it the great red brick wound of the Marrowworks two districts inboard, dark for the first time in forty years.

Suri came up the roof-ladder the way she came up everywhere, without wasted motion, and stopped at a precise arm's length. She had taken to leaving her left forearm unbound in the evenings — the fracture had closed at last, leaving a ridge of bone like a seam in a repaired ledger — and she flexed it once, checking the balance of herself, before she spoke.

"Four hundred and twelve tiles," she said. "Three are loose. One will go this winter and hit a Gavel man, which I have decided is a good outcome."

"Noted for the year-end accounts."

"Vahid says you're not to walk alone yet." She said it flatly, an entry, not an argument. "His word was 'prohibited.' He underlined it."

"His prohibitions are priced in gin," Dar said. "I can't afford the exchange rate."

She looked at him for a moment with those carbon-dusted eyes, and whatever she was tallying behind them came out even, because she nodded once and went back down the ladder, taking the last watch in the ward. She paused at the top rung only to say, "Milla rewashed her hands again. Fifth time since supper. If you're going to walk tonight, walk before she wears through her own skin," and was gone.

The Ferun woman had been eleven days under the clinic's roof. She still slept upright, still folded the RHYTHM UNRESOLVED printout into her sleeve before bed like a prayer she didn't believe in, and still scrubbed her hands as if the salt the city put under her nails were a debt she could sweat off. Dar had stopped telling her it wasn't grime. Some columns a debtor has to audit alone. In the ward she had taken over the medicine counts by the third day — twelve years of tithe-clerk habit do not resign, they only change employers — and her slips were exact in the way only a frightened person's slips are exact, every figure inked as if someone were reading over her shoulder. Someone always had been. It was the reading-over she was learning to live without, and it went slowly.

He took out the slate.

The Cartographer had pressed it on him outside the fighting pit, in the fog, months and a lifetime ago: a ragged roof-slate, one scratch-mark cluster cut deep and sure, reading one hundred and nine. *Something on the far side has started counting your heartbeats,* she had said, metallic and calm. *It's at one hundred and nine.* He had kept it pinned above the attic desk ever since, beside the registry page, the way a man keeps the mortgage papers of a house he refuses to sell. He held it now under the moonless dark, and the salt frost on the parapet breathed white with every gust off the sea.

Then he closed his eyes and went inside — not the clinic, the other house.

Vahid's doctrine, chalked week after week on the cellar wall in his ferocious, crooked hand, came down to two lines: *memory is architecture; you built the ruin, you may walk the ruin.* Walking costs nothing. Only doors spend. For five weeks Dar had treated the Mind Vein the way one treats a road washed out by flood — known, mapped, avoided. Tonight was the first time he had stood at its edge with his ledger balanced and chosen to cross on purpose.

He descended his own halls the way he had once descended a patient's chest: by landmarks, never by hope. The corridors were his — swept, grey, lit by no lamp and needing none. He passed the room where his mother's face had hung and did not slow. He knew what was in it now, because he had made himself learn it in the cellar under Vahid's lens: a pale rectangle on the wall where a frame had been, the plaster a shade darker inside the rectangle than out, the way a wall remembers a mirror. Nothing else. No dust, because dust is time, and that room had had all of its time withdrawn, called due and paid in one terrible installment on the night the altar broke.

The room that had held his father's laugh kept a stone hearth with no kettle on it. He had spent that laugh as ammunition, three syllables of it per burst, and the hearth had gone cold in a way stone was never meant to go. The first kiss was behind a door he kept shut from habit rather than discipline — habit was cheaper. The childhood home's color was a room washed in no shade at all, a column left blank in a ledger that hated blanks. He had spent four rooms. The house had held more than four, and he was careful not to inventory the rest too closely. Inventories invite appraisals. Appraisals invite buyers.

Ilay's chalk spiral hung over the walk the way a route hangs over a man who has promised to travel it. Roya had copied the girl's map onto good paper and pinned it in the cellar: the slow tightening gyre converging on the Throne shaft, ring inside ring, the innermost ring lettered in a nine-year-old's careful hand — soon. He had looked at that word for six nights running. It had not blinked. Vahid called it a child's projection; Roya called it a bearing; Dar, walking his own emptied halls on purpose for the first time, called it what he was afraid it was — an appointment someone else had made and neglected to invite him to.

He walked, and did not touch, and the walking cost nothing, which he trusted the way one trusts a tollgate found standing open.

At the deepest hall, the one beneath all his other halls, the furnished door stood where it had stood since the cellar weeks — hung, handled, hinged, of a dark wood that had never grown in any house he knew, set into a wall that was his own grey salt-and-bone everywhere else. It hummed, faintly, a note just under hearing. It was warm on the far side; he could feel the warmth across the eight feet of corridor like heat off a body recently vacated. A room someone had just left. And tonight — new, tonight — a thread of light moved beneath the door, slow as lamplight carried past on the far side by a hand too patient to be hurried.

Dar stopped eight feet away, as he had stopped eight feet away every time, and declined it. Not his door. Not his debt. He put the observation where he now put such things — filed, not felt — and turned back up the hall.

It was in the hush of the turning that he heard it.

It did not come from outside. It came up through the architecture, the way sound comes up a stairwell of stone, and it was a heartbeat — patient and immense, a slow deep drum under the world — and he had heard it before, on this roof, in the first storm after the Blacktide, and named it once with the only word the Lily had ever spent. But he had never heard it *clearly*, against silence, against a mind freshly swept, and clarity showed him the thing the noise of living had hidden.

It was one beat behind him.

He tested it the way he tested any figure that looked too neat: two fingers on his wrist, his own pulse counted out in the dark, the drum beneath the city answering each beat a full rest later. Beat. Rest. Beat, answered. Again. Again. Twenty counts, and the lag held to the twentieth, and his surgeon's mind did the only arithmetic available to it and came back sick: not an echo. Echoes degrade. This was reproduction — his column copied one line down, in a hand too patient to be mocking, the way a scrivener reproduces a master ledger without improving on it. *Because it counts back,* the old chart's margin had said. He had taken that for poetry. It was bookkeeping.

He surfaced onto the parapet with frost in his lashes and his own breath loud, and the night was exactly as he had left it, which suddenly felt like an allowance he hadn't earned.

The slate was still in his hand. He looked down at it because his hand had turned it over without asking him, and the scratch-marks were the same — one hundred and nine, cut deep and sure in the Cartographer's certainty — and beneath them, new.

Five strokes. Fresh. Chalk-bright against slate gone dull with a season of weather, their edges unsoftened by any rain or salt the city had thrown at the world since — and the city had thrown plenty. He had not cut them. He would have sworn that under audit, which in Ashkarad was the strongest oath there was, and the oath curdled even as he made it, because he had been standing at a door when the hush came, and the count, as far as he could ever reconstruct it, had only ever moved at thresholds. It did not run at pulse-rate. It ran at doors. And he had opened the hall tonight and walked it, deliberately, for the first time.

He did not remember making the strokes. That was the entry, and he wrote it nowhere, because some entries you keep only in the flesh.

The wind off the sea had turned while he walked his halls; he could taste the storm the wind was dragging behind it, still far out, still a rumor — grace coming, the city would call it, and batten, and endure. Below him the Gavel tallyman had gone; the alley mouth was unmarked chalk. The Saltstairs breathed. Two districts inboard, the Marrowworks lay dark over whatever lay under the Marrowworks, and under that — deeper than the dead First Vessel, deeper than Vey's cathedral of stolen years — the drum went on, one beat behind, unrushed.

Then the Lily leaned.

There was no better verb for it, and he was a man who rationed verbs. She had been still in him for weeks — guest-quiet, hum-quiet, the stillness of an account that has stopped accruing. Now the whole rooted weight of her tipped, all at once, toward the deep, the way a ship's cargo shifts in a swell, and his balance went with her because his balance was hers now whether the ledgers admitted it or not. He staggered against the parapet, caught it two-handed, slate bit and frost bit and his boots skidding an inch on the salt-glaze — an inch, on a roof, above four stories, and the inch felt like a reading, like a gauge, like proof of how much of him she could move without asking.

And then the answering pressure came.

It rose through the soles of his feet and arrived against his sternum from the inside of the world — patient, immense, unhurried — level, and deliberate, the way a counterweight is set gently on the empty pan of a scale. Not a pull. A *meeting.* The Lily pressed toward the deep, and the deep, for the first time since the day a dying thief had crawled into a canal with a cracked reliquary in his arms, pressed back.

Dar stood at the parapet with his arms braced and his heart counted by something beneath the salt, one line down, one beat late, and understood that the count on the slate was not the ledger.

It was the copy.
