# Chapter 94 — Order, or Hunger

The Choirmaster had sung in worse venues than Mother Gavel's quarry. He had sung, in his time, over harvest floors, over the amphitheater where the Church graded children like fruit, over the long glass shelves of the reliquary corridor where he now kept the dead's house ready for singing — but he had never sung in a venue with acoustics this honest. The quarry threw every voice back to its owner. A man could stand on the pit's lowest tier and hear exactly what he sounded like. He had always suspected that was why Gavel held her assemblies here. It was the only room in Ashkarad where a lie had to pay its own freight.

They convened at dusk, with five of the nine days spent and four remaining, chalked on the clinic wall where the whole city had to walk past them, and the bloom-fog banked grey over the rim like an unvisited creditor, and the pit filled the way the pit had filled once before, the night the city's vote had died mid-count. The Index came under Seft with a drafted ordinance in a leather sleeve and forty grey coats who did not hum. Mother Gavel's remnant came — not her crews entire; her crews that still kept the old arithmetic, Harl the overman at their head with the ration-books and the exact, unforgiving face of a man who has never once overpaid for anything. The Hammers' remnant came last, up the south ramp, fog-scarred men and women led by an overman named Dree who coughed between sentences in the cadence the fog taught, four beats and a rest, and behind them the fog-sick of the eastern districts came in their hundreds, called by nothing, following the hunger in the air the way iron filings follow a file.

And the city came, under both seals: the counted, the willing, the census of the roll that Suri had built and Milla had kept and Vell had chalked fair. Dar sat on the first tier with the census held in him and his face doing the thing it did in triage, which was nothing. Roya and Ilay had a bench at the rim, chalk and child, her instrument and her map. Isra came with her slate and Wen came with nothing at all, which for a warden was a kind of statement. The Choirmaster came with his thirty — the freed, once-harvested voices — and seated them on the second tier like a choir, because forty years of administration had taught him exactly one reliable fact about crowds, which was that a crowd that can hear itself is a different instrument from a crowd that cannot.

He brought the fire-axe. He leaned it against the bench, in view, not for use. A man his age owed the room his tools and his history, and both were edged.

Mother Gavel opened it, from the pit's center, with her brass tube left at home for the first time in anyone's memory. She spoke like a tariff schedule. "The fleet served its provision. Nine days. The city has voted twice and deadlocked twice. My crews' debts go in the fire first, whatever you choose; that is my one term and it stands. Count it straight." She looked at Harl, and something passed between them that had been passing between bosses and overmen since before the Church learned to stamp, and Harl stepped up and laid the remnant's case on the table like a man laying down a hand of cards he had counted to the last card.

"Option one," Harl said. "The Index's ordinance. A new Gardener."

Seft took it up in the tone of a writ being read, which after all it was: the Reclaim's schedule honored, amended, humanized. A new Gardener — the city's own, chosen, salted in the city's interest — who would feed the deep on schedule, prune the seven bloomed cities still on the books kindly, with mercy, with anesthesia, with music, at the tail of a reformed calendar; who would take the chair the terms had named and sit it with a local conscience. "Order," Seft said, "with the cruelty filed off. The ledgers keep. The ledgers have always kept. We are not voting on whether the deep eats. We are voting on who holds the fork, and how sharp it is, and I put it to the roll that a knife in an honest local hand is worth more to the counted than a hymn."

Harl seconded it with the arithmetic, because the remnant's case was arithmetic and Harl did not pretend otherwise. "The schedule is coming," he said. "It has come to seven cities ahead of us, on time, every time, for a century. I have read the true books. My boss burned a charter and kept her water-works, and I keep the ration-books, and the ration-books say a fed thing with a known appetite is cheaper than a starving thing with an unknown one. Take the chair. Hold the fork. Somebody is going to; it should be a hand that signs in this pit."

The fog-sick did not wait for Dree. They came up over the benches like weather, and their spokesman was nobody — a woman with salt-crusted lips and a craving in her eyes that the censers could no longer reach, who stood on a bench and said, in a voice with no music left in it: "Feed it kindly. That's option one. I'll tell you what option one sounds like. It sounds like the engine at Gullet. It begged at shift-change. Kindly." And then Dree gave option two its body, coughing on the rest-beats: burn the garden. Free every seed in the world into hunger and let the world take its chances — the deep starved, the Root dead, the bloomed Thrones turned loose from the schedule to eat or be eaten, Ashkarad alive because everything else was food or fire. "I have watched hunger run a city for a year," Dree said, "and I will take its honesty over your ledger's mercy. Burn it. Let the world roll its own dice. We were counted worth eating once. Never again."

The count went to the roll. Milla called the districts; Vell chalked; Suri tallied with the flat speed of a woman counting other people's heartbeats, which was what she had been doing for a year.

Dead even. The quarry made a sound the Choirmaster had heard once before in this pit, the night a skipped heartbeat had stopped a vote — a sound like a held instrument.

They counted it again. Dead even, to the soul. The fog moved a little on the rim. Below them all, under a mile of salt, the deep kept its listening quiet, and the Choirmaster, who could hear a settling chord from thirty yards, heard the settling in the ground and did not like the key it was settling into.

He stood up.

He had written sermons for the Church of the Silent Chorus. He had taught the nursery hymns — the compliance-hymns, the little tunes with the anatomical hooks, the songs built to make tissue compliant — and he had graded children by lung and larynx with the same hand that now gripped a bench, and he had burned a registry of names with thirty witnesses and meant it, and all of it was in the account, and he had never once in his life paid into this particular column, so he paid it now, into the honest acoustics of the pit, at his own freight:

"I have administered song for the order of this city for forty years," he said, and the quarry took his baritone and gave it back to him, true. "I know precisely what order harvests. I graded the harvest. I have stood at an amphitheater rail and announced a boy's lungs to a paying floor — Today we welcome little Elias to the choir — and called it liturgy. I tuned the compliance-hymns to the bloodstream; the Church called that order. I watched forty years of organs go into glass singing the lullaby to keep the deep asleep, and the Church called that order. Order, ladies and gentlemen of the counted, is a filing system for hunger. It does not reduce the eating. It issues receipts for the eating. I have signed them."

He let the pit hold that. Somewhere on the Index benches a clerk stopped writing, and he heard it, and went on.

"And I know what hunger eats, because I have heard it eat. It eats kindly, at schedule, with anesthesia and music; you have heard the engine at Gullet beg — that is what a fed hunger sounds like. Or it eats everything, if you burn the keepers off it; you have smelled Lowmark on the wind — that is what a freed hunger sounds like. I have spent my life between those two instruments and I am done being played by either." He looked down the tiers, over the Index and the remnant and the fog-sick and the counted, and he set his hand flat on the bench beside the axe. "So I will vote for the thing I have never once seen administered. Not order. Not hunger. Consent. I have heard hymns sung by force and anthems sung by fear and a city's worth of heartbeats given freely in one rhythm because they chose it, and I am a professional, and I am telling you the last of those was the finest performance I have ever heard in my life. Vote the third way. Write nothing down that a soul has not signed. I have been the administrator of order, and I am retiring from it, and my last act of administration is this: I give the roll back to the singers."

He sat down. The quarry was quiet the way the vault was quiet, and then, from the third tier, a boy's lungs did what the Choirmaster's own hymns had once been written to make lungs do — took a breath the whole pit could hear — and did not sing. Elias just breathed, once, deeply, a boy in a room full of adults deciding on purpose not to perform, and the pit heard the breath, and the breath was the Amen. The Choirmaster's thirty sat very straight on their bench, thirty throats the Church had once borrowed and returned, and not one of them made a sound, because he had trained them, and training, like order, can finally be aimed by consent.

Then Suri's voice went up the tiers, district by district, and Milla called, and Vell chalked, and the count came down the pit like water finding the drain.

The third way carried at dawn. Not by much; by a margin they counted twice and then a third time because the fog-sick benches rose to vote it too, craved and starving and counted, and their votes went into the third column because a choice made under a hunger is still a choice, and the roll was the roll. At the rim Roya chalked as the totals closed, and Ilay stood with her hands flat on the quarry rock, and said, to nobody, in her finding-voice: "It's listening to the chalk." The grey light came up over the eastern wall and found the pit tier by tier, and the fog on the rim thinned the way fog does when there is nothing left to negotiate with, and the Choirmaster stood with his axe untouched at his bench and let his forty years come down off his shoulders like a coat he had finally been billed for and finally paid.

Mother Gavel watched the chalk close, and nodded once, tariff-short, and went to tell her crews.

And beneath the quarry, beneath the salt and the vaults and the three-chambered heart that had kept the city's time since the Burning, the deep heartbeat slowed. Not stopping. Slowing — a beat drawn out, and another, like a thing leaning its weight against a door to listen, like a debtor hearing the terms of his own restructuring read aloud in a room above him, and deciding, for the length of the reading, to keep still.
