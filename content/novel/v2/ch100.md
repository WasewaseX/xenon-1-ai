# Chapter 100 — One Heartbeat, Many

Weeks later, the clinic had a waiting list, and the waiting list had manners.

The mornings began the way mornings had begun since before the Blacktide, with the kettle and the chalk and Vahid's voice carrying down the stairwell like weather — except that the voice was lecturing to a full hall now. The school of the unpriced had outgrown its first three rooms and annexed the reliquary corridor, and Vahid published the way other men bled: cheap paper, salt-scoured press, every page bearing the same line at the foot — *per S. Bahar, who heard it first* — the honest text of the shared doors, the chorus-free protocol distilled to a shelf of numbered vials and a page of instructions a literate quartermaster could follow. Students came up from the canals and down from the quarry with grease on their hands and ledger scars on their souls, and learned anatomy as bookkeeping, and triage as mercy with a budget, and the first rule of the house, chalked over the door in a hand that could only have been Vell's: NOTHING HERE IS PRICED. THAT IS THE DIFFICULT PART.

Ilay taught the singing.

She was the city's cantor now, copper-haired and ten and unnervingly exact, and she held her classes in the corridor's old acoustics where the Church had once shelved harvested throats, and she taught the true song the way her score had taught it — every verse, the mother's line included, the ending left uncounted. She sang without hearing machines. None was keyed to her blood anymore; the Index's readers had gone to the smelters with their hymn-weights and their finding-songs, and the new machines the city built — hand-cranked, honest, bound for midwife halls and quarry gates — printed only what they were told: heartbeats, breaths, the hours of the bell. She would stand in front of sixty children with her palms flat on nothing at all, conducting out of pure memory, and no instrument in Ashkarad had anything to say about it. She had decided to like that. She had also decided not to like it, some days, and Roya let her have both, because Roya was the one who had taught her that both columns could stay open. Elias, whose lungs the Church had graded perfect for its own purposes, taught the smallest ones the breathing — long, counted, unforced — and the smallest ones taught it to their parents, and somewhere in that relay the Church's last inventory of the children's bodies quietly became the city's first curriculum for their lungs.

Roya's mask was on her belt.

She carved the new city map on the Choratorium's great wall, floor to vault, and she carved it with her face bare to the whole city for the first time since the fog had taken her song — a brown scarred face with chalk dust in its lines that no one had seen in a year and a half, and the city looked at it, and got used to it, and the mask became a thing she carried the way other people carried knives: in case. The map was not the Wardens' map. It had the districts where the districts were, and where the districts ended it had the deep — one line, chalked and then cut in stone, running under everything from the seawalls to the Throne shaft, labeled in Ilay's hand: KEEPER BELOW. At the wall's corner, where the old census flowers used to be chalked, Roya left the stone blank, and told anyone who asked that the blank was the point.

Milla Ferun woke smiling on the morning the storm came in.

She had dreamed — she told no one, but the registrar's desk kept no secrets from itself — of a green garden under a sky that had weather in it, rows going down to a horizon that was only horizon, and in the dream she had walked the rows touching each plant once, the way you check a ledger you no longer fear, and the salt had been nowhere in it, and she had woken with her hands loose on the coverlet and her nails rimed white. Salt under her nails, smiling, on a morning in her own bed: the old books would have called it an omen and priced the consultation. She washed, and went to the registry, and spent the day re-stamping pages with the Index's defected clerks under her eye, turning the Church's old census line by line — VESSEL: ILAY, VESSEL: ELIAS, VESSEL: eleven more — into what they had always been: VESSEL: NO. CITIZEN: YES. The stamp was new-cut, the letters deep, and it went through the old paper with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and every child in the stack heard it done.

And in the corridor at the bell before supper, the Choirmaster heard the answer to the question he had asked in another life with a scalpel in his hand and a boy on the table.
He had not known he was still asking it. But the school had annexed his corridor, and at the bell the children sang the true song through it shelf by shelf, and the old acoustics he had re-tuned for the dead carried the living instead, and he stood in the gallery's mouth with his fire-axe retired to a rack like a relic, and the question surfaced in him — *ask yourself what the city is for* — and rose, and met the singing on the way up, and came down changed. A lid, he heard, in the pedal under the children's thread. A lock, in the verse that had been the Church's whole architecture. And latterly — in the ending nobody counted, in the hall that charged nothing, in sixty throats learning an unpriced song in a house where harvested voices had been inventory — a home. He said it aloud once, to the shelves, testing the arithmetic: "A lid. A lock. And, latterly, a home." The gallery returned it to him with the warmth of a room agreeing, and he stood there until the bell finished, and went to supper owing nothing.

The margins kept their own lights on. In the drainage canals Lena Vance sang evenings again, for no one's collection, and the water carried her down to the tide-gate where Dathan Kaveh kept the gate-clock honest and the couriers' barges loaded at hours the Warden bells no longer governed. Mother Gavel's pumps ran the water-works she had been paid for — paid in function and never flesh, per the only contract in Ashkarad she had never once tried to renegotiate — and her ledgermen walked the wards with empty books now, on Gavel's standing order, because an empty book, she had ruled, was the only honest thing a ledgerman could carry. The freed donors kept their gallery. Vell chalked the schools. The seawalls stood. The city, which had been a harvest for a hundred years, was mending itself in the messy, incremental, unbilled way that bodies mend, and every week the mending needed less of Dar and more of itself, which was, he understood, the entire point of mending.

Dar climbed to the roof at dusk, because the storm was coming in true.

The salt storms since the Blacktide had been curtsies — white squalls that stamped the rooftops and moved on with their receipts. This one settled in the way landlords settle in, a full white-out riding a wind that tasted of iron and salt, the storm-walls singing their long note, the tenements going out one by one below him like entries being erased by a hand too patient to be hurried. Grace, the city had always called it, bitterly. Tonight the word stood on its own account and paid its own way.

He stood at the roof's edge with his hand over the center of his chest, the way he had stood in another life on a creaking cot with a stolen heart in him and a ledger of vital debt on the desk and a machine's slip in his fist that read RHYTHM UNRESOLVED, MACHINE FAULT. He had said hello to her once, into a washbasin mirror, not knowing what he was greeting. He knew now. He had spent a year and a half learning what he was greeting, and the tuition had been a mother's face, a father's laugh, a first kiss, the color of a childhood door, and every coin had gone somewhere, and none of it had ever been refunded — but the city, which keeps what it loves, had made him a new mother out of its own rememberings, and given her to him unpriced, and the room where the old face hung held its corrected exhibit with the light left on.

The ladder was gone from his mind's back wall. Vahid's chalked rungs — First Vein: Flesh. Wounds knit, strength swells — had carried him as far as a man could climb on a ladder, and the chord had taken their place, rungs dissolved into intervals, a vertical made horizontal and shared. Somewhere the discredited papers were required reading, footnoted and corrected and taught by students who would never know what the word *impossible* had cost the man who wrote them. Somewhere a woman's recorded voice was the most cited text in two disciplines. The dead's house was full of singing.

One heartbeat. He could hear it if he let the Nerve widen, and he let it widen, and did not spend a thing to hold it there — that was the miracle he had stopped calling a miracle and started calling Tuesday: that the listening cost nothing now, that the door he had opened as a wound stood open as a room. One heartbeat that was somehow many. His own, sixty to the minute, a surgeon's resting rate. Hers, folded into it — the fraction of her that was his, the guest, the duet, the rest of her gone out into the song that needed her more than a chest did, and glad of it. The counted: Suri's rolls and Milla's stamps and Vell's chalk, eleven hundred souls who gave their beats at the bell because giving had gotten cheaper than hoarding. The makers: the school's hammers and the pump-works' pistons and the clinic's kettle, all of it keeping time because the city had finally hired time instead of renting it. And below all of it, so far under that hearing it was closer to remembering, the deep's slow consent — the Root asleep and fed on shares, dreaming without being driven, the low pedal of a foundation that did not lean.

There was a hole in the chord where the eldest sister's beat had been. The city sang around it the way a mouth finds a missing tooth — not forgetting, adjusting — and every sixth bell the maintenance count gave its few beats downward into the dark, and the dark kept them, and kept the books, and did not answer, and that was the shape of the price: paid in full, denominated in distance, honored forever. Isra tended the tide-gate ledger of it now. Some nights Ilay sang the second verse down the shaft anyway, unasked, the way you visit a grave with news.

Suri came up the roof stair with two cups of tea at the hour the storm closed, because she had decided some time ago that the Custodian's ritual belonged to whoever survived it, and she had survived it with the books balanced. She handed him one without marking which. Dar took it and drank, and it did not matter which cup it was, and that — she said, watching him not check — was the whole return on everything they had ever spent. Then she counted the storm's first hour aloud, because it was hers to count, and he stood beside her and let himself be counted among.

The storm took the city. Dar stood in it until the white had him entire — the Saltstairs gone, the Choratorium gone, the fog-sea gone wherever seas go when they are forgiven — and under the white the count held: one heartbeat, many, unbillable, unspent. Across the alley, on the doorframe where a census clerk had once chalked a five-petaled flower because one vessel was missing, someone had drawn the flower again with nine petals, every petal connected to every other, in chalk that the salt could not take. A census. A count. And no one missing.

And far away, under weathers of their own, in seven far cities — Lowmark, the Vigil, Gullet, Sarn, Weep, Ossuar, Hallowfen — seven seeds turned over in their sleep and began, very slowly, to wake.
