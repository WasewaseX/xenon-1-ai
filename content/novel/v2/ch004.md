# Chapter 4 — The Silent Chorus

The Chorus hired desperate cutters, and Dar was nothing if not desperate. Desperation was the one credential the Choratorium never ran out of, and never asked twice about. He walked through the Choratorium's bone-arched entrance, his borrowed Chorus white gloves pristine, his pulse steady beneath the borrowed coat—steady on the surface, at least, one heart keeping the rhythm the Wardens expected while the other kept a different one entirely, slower, patient, keeping its own books. The air here smelled of antiseptic and something else—copper and honey, like blood preserved in sugar. It was the smell of the place where he had spent seven years of his life paying off a heart, and he had never once noticed how sweet it was. That was the thing about institutions. You stopped smelling them the way you stopped hearing your own name.

A Clinic Custodian with bone-plating across her cheeks greeted him, her voice a smooth, practiced melody. "Welcome, cutter. We've been expecting you. The harvest floor awaits your skilled hands."

"Thank you, Custodian," Dar said, and was proud of how ordinary it came out.

The harvest floor was a cathedral of glass and brass, a forest of vitrines where debtors floated in nutrient fluid, their chests opened like books—laid open, Dar corrected himself, the way a ledger is laid open, spines cracked so the entries can be read at a glance. Some had multiple hearts beating in tandem, others had lungs grown in pairs where one should be, all wired to monitors that tracked their vital signs with the precision of a metronome. The monitors ticked in loose canon with one another, a hundred small clocks slightly out of phase, and beneath it all ran the sound Dar had grown up hearing through the walls of Saint Ferrous and never once been told the name of: the organ-hum, the building itself breathing through its pipes. Nurses moved between the vitrines with clipboards, making notations as if they were tallying inventory. Most of the floated faces were tranquil. A few were not, and the nurses did not look at those.

"Each vessel is a note in our grand composition," the Custodian explained, leading Dar past a man with three livers, each a different shade of crimson—wine-dark, rust-dark, one so pale it was nearly the color of the fluid he floated in. "By isolating the organs, we allow each to sing its purest song. Together, they create a harmony no single body could achieve."

"Of course," Dar said. His voice sounded very far away to him, like something shouted down a stairwell.

He nodded, his gaze fixed on a ledger wall where census pages were filed alphabetically, brass runners and glass fronts, the records of who belonged to whom arranged as publicly as a menu. His own page would be there, the one that flagged him as a missing vessel. He needed to find it, to steal it, to erase the record that would lead the Chorus to him. One page. One entry struck from one column, and the city's smallest room of clerks would have to do all its arithmetic again.

He feigned interest in a nearby vitrine, a woman with her nervous system mapped across her torso like a circuit board, silver tracery branching over her ribs and down her arms, pulsing faintly with every monitor-beep. "Fascinating," he murmured, his voice tight. "The precision required..."

"The human body is the most intricate instrument," the Custodian agreed. "We are merely the conductors, ensuring each note is played to its fullest potential."

And who, Dar wondered, conducts the conductors. He kept the question behind his teeth, where it cost nothing.

Dar's fingers brushed against the ledger wall, his heart pounding beneath the Lily's steady rhythm. He found the 'M' section and began flipping through the pages, his breath held. Mehran, Darius. There—his census page, his name in upright type above the hospital's serial, the sum of seven years paid and owing, and stamped across the foot of it in a red the Chorus reserved for delinquent accounts: MISSING. He slipped it into his coat, his movements practiced, casual, the same motion he used to pocket a scalpel at the end of a shift, the hand that moved quiet even here, in the one building in Ashkarad where quiet hands were most likely to be needed.

But then something else caught his eye—the Choratorium nursery ledger. Entries listed vessels like produce, graded on a scale of A to F, each line annotated with a small musical mark that he didn't know how to read and suspected, all the same, meant everything. One entry stood out: ILAY, PERFECT, RESERVED FOR THE THRONE. Dar's blood ran cold. This was the child Suri had mentioned, the one who had escaped. A perfect note, reserved for the throne, and the note had walked out of the Choratorium's score. Somebody, somewhere, was keeping a child off the books.

"Lost in the music, cutter?" The Custodian's voice was closer than he'd expected. Dar turned to find her standing beside him, her bone-plated face unreadable. "We keep meticulous records. Every vessel, every note, accounted for."

"Merely admiring your system," Dar said, forcing a smile. "The efficiency is... admirable."

The Custodian's gaze lingered on him, her head tilted slightly, the way a tuner's head tilts toward a string that has gone a quarter-tone wrong. "You carry the scent of something else. Something... alive. Not of the Chorus."

Dar's heart hammered against his ribs. The Lily inside him pulsed, a silent warning—give nothing. "I—I don't know what you mean," he stammered, taking a step back.

The Custodian's smile didn't reach her eyes. "Of course you don't. But we do." She raised a hand, and two Choirmasters in bone masks stepped forward, their scalpel-tipped fingers glinting in the harsh light. "Perhaps we should examine you more closely."

Dar's mind raced. He needed to stall, to buy time, and there was only one coin he had ever had that institutions always accepted. Competence. "The harvest floor," he said, gesturing vaguely. "The arrangements here... they're not optimal. The fluid ratios are off by 2.3 percent. It's affecting the organ viability."

The Choirmasters froze, their scalpel-fingers still, three hands suspended mid-air like notes on a hold. The Custodian's expression shifted, curiosity replacing suspicion. "Is that so? Explain."

Dar launched into a detailed explanation of fluid dynamics, his voice steady despite the terror coursing through him. He spoke of osmotic pressure, of nutrient absorption rates, of how even a slight imbalance could compromise the viability of harvested organs—how too much saline and the tissues swelled and sang flat, how too little and they contracted, dehydrated, went sharp. He did not invent any of it. That was the horror and the shelter of it: it was all true, learned across seven years at Saint Ferrous, knowledge that had finally found its perfect use in keeping him out of a vitrine. The Custodian listened, her head tilted, her expression thoughtful.

"An interesting observation," she said finally. "But we've been using these ratios for years."

"Times change," Dar said, his pulse hammering in his throat. "New research, new techniques. I could show you. Adjust the ratios myself. It would only take a moment."

The Custodian considered this, then nodded. "Very well. Show us. But make it quick. The choir awaits its conductor."

Dar led them to a nearby vitrine, his hands trembling as he pretended to adjust the fluid ratios—turned the brass wheel a fraction, watched the gauge drift, made small professional sounds of dissatisfaction. The Choirmasters stood watch, their scalpel-fingers poised. Dar's eyes darted around, searching for an escape route. The corridor behind him was lined with harvested organs, preserved in jars and hanging from hooks, each jar labeled in the Chorus's upright hand, each label a name reduced to a tissue type. He made himself stop reading the labels.

"Is that it?" the Custodian asked, her voice impatient.

"Almost," Dar said, his gaze falling on a row of hanging lungs. "The ventilation system needs recalibration. It's affecting the oxygenation levels."

The Custodian sighed. "Fine. Show us."

As she moved closer, Dar made his move. He grabbed a nearby jar of preserved kidneys and hurled it at the Choirmasters. Glass burst against bone, amber fluid and formaldehyde spraying in a bright arc; one mask turned away, one scalpel-hand flinched up to protect its eyes, and in that opened fraction of a score, Dar bolted, running down the corridor, his heart pounding against his ribs. The Lily inside him pulsed, urging him faster.

He ducked into a side corridor, the sound of footsteps echoing behind him—not footsteps, he realized, the flat click of scalpel-fingertips on tile, a sound like a very small audience applauding. He ran past rows of harvested hearts, livers, lungs, each one a silent testament to the Chorus's methodology. The air grew colder, the scent of antiseptic giving way to something else—something ancient and hungry, a smell with no modern name in it at all. Behind him, the Custodian's melodic voice called out, still calm, still kind, the way a scale is kind: each note in its proper place.

Dar reached a heavy metal door and pushed through it, stumbling into a dimly lit chamber. The walls were lined with shelves, each holding a different organ, each one still alive, still singing its silent song—the hum in here was louder, closer to words, a room built like the inside of a throat. In the center of the room stood a massive glass tank, filled with a viscous, amber fluid. Inside, something moved—something vast and slow, its form shifting in the depths, and the shifting was not random. It had the rhythm of respiration. The tank itself was breathing.

Dar froze, his breath catching in his throat. The Lily inside him pulsed, a strange recognition washing over him, a warmth like memory but not his memory, like a child's photo held up to a stranger's face. This was the First Vessel, the buried proto-Throne the Chorus was trying to awaken. Forty years of the city's arithmetic, filed into one sleeping body.

"Found you," a voice said from behind him.

Dar turned to see the Custodian standing in the doorway, a bone scalpel in her hand. "You shouldn't be here," she said, her voice calm. "This is sacred ground."

Dar backed away, his hand on the door handle. "I'm leaving," he said, his voice tight.

The Custodian took a step forward. "You carry something that doesn't belong to you. Something that was planted with love."

The word love, in that room, with that tank breathing behind him, did more damage than any scalpel could have. Dar hesitated, then turned and fled, pushing through the door and back into the corridor. He ran, his lungs burning, the Lily urging him onward, and it occurred to him somewhere in the burning that the thing in his chest ran with him without complaint, that it did not tire, that it was getting better at him. He reached the entrance, bursting through the bone-arched doors and into the harsh light of the Choratorium plaza. The white glare stabbed down through his squint, and under it the Lily flinched and went small. Behind him the Choratorium's doors stood open, and he understood that the building had never been trying to keep him in. Why would it need to? It had simply wanted a moment to finish reading him.

As he stumbled out, every ceiling speaker in the clinic crackled to life. A woman's voice, calm and kind, filled the air—out on the plaza, in the fog, up and down the district, carried on every horn and bell the Chorus owned.

"To whoever carries our missing garden home," the voice said. "She was planted with love. Bring her back before she roots in strangers."

Dar froze, the voice echoing in his mind. The Lily inside him shivered—whether with fear or longing, he couldn't tell.
