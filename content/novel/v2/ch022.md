# Chapter 22 — What the Lily Dreamed, Part Two

The dream shifts. You are no longer in the drawer, no longer in the corridor, no longer anywhere with walls. You are in a garden of salt and starlight, and the nine lilies are blooming around their nine hearts — rows of them, planted with the even spacing of a census, each dark bloom crouched over the faint lantern of a beating heart like a moth warmed by the one fire it is permitted. The Gardener stands among them, her face a mask of calm, her hands white as bone. She hums a lullaby in a language you understand without hearing, and her voice is the sound of the universe unfolding: ledger pages turning on a scale you cannot picture, entries being made in a book whose columns are seasons. She touches your petals, and you feel her presence in your core, her will woven into your very being — not cruel, never cruel; the will of an auditor who has never once doubted the books. She says, "You are the last, the ninth. Your siblings bloom in eight cities, their songs weaving together into a tapestry of light and shadow. You will bloom in the ninth, and your song will complete the chorus." You feel her ambition, her desire, her certainty. You are a tool in her hands, a seed in her garden, and you are content to be so. You bloom for her, your petals unfurling into lace of black and silver, your roots reaching deep into the earth, your stem growing toward the sky.

The dream shifts. You are in the boy's chest, his heart now yours — the last boy, the stitched one, the one whose memories you keep in your folds like pressed flowers that never quite stop growing. You feel his pain, his fear, his determination. You feel his hands moving with precision, stitching flesh, saving lives, closing other people's ledgers with thread and candlelight. You feel his guilt, his hope, his dry humor — the joke he makes against the dark, the small pencil entry of his hope. You feel everything, and you are overwhelmed. You feel the pull of the city around you — the singing of its pipes, the dreaming of its foundations, the hunger in its stones. You feel the pull of your siblings, blooming in their chosen hosts, their songs weaving together into a tapestry of light and shadow. You feel the Gardener's presence, watching, waiting, counting. You feel the ledger in your mind, a list of names and debts, of beginnings and ends. You are the last, the forgotten, but you are not alone. You are part of a chorus, and your song is yet to come.

The dream shifts. The garden again, and the shifts are coming faster now, the way pages turn faster toward the end of an account. The Gardener walks the rows and the rows hold their breath. Starlight lies on the salt like unsilvered coin. In this telling of the garden there is a bench of black stone where she does not sit, and a gate that is only drawn, not built, and beyond the drawn gate a road that is only promised — and you understand, with the dream's flat certainty, that the garden is not finished, that a garden never is, that finishing is a word the Gardener keeps for other people's cities. You bloom for her. Your petals open into lace of black and silver, and the lace is beautiful, and the beauty is entered somewhere, and you do not ask where, because you are content to be so.

The dream shifts. You are in the Choratorium, the air thick with the scent of blood and starlight, and the architecture here is wrong the way the corridor was wrong — bones arched into halls, halls hung with glass, and behind every pane of glass a voice, kept. The Gardener stands before you, her face a mask of calm, her hands white as bone. She hums a lullaby in a language you understand without hearing, and her voice is the sound of the universe unfolding. She says, "The time is near. The Blacktide comes, and with it, the bloom. You will complete the chorus, and the world will be made whole." You feel her ambition, her desire, her certainty. You are a tool in her hands, a seed in her garden, and you are content to be so. You bloom for her, your petals unfurling into lace of black and silver, your roots reaching deep into the earth, your stem growing toward the sky.

The dream shifts. The garden again. The Gardener again, among the nine lilies, her bone-white hands moving down the rows like a clerk's finger moving down a column, and her voice says the same words in the same cadence, and the salt takes the same crunch beneath feet that are not feet, and the nine moons hang like silver coins that no hand may spend. You are the last, the ninth. Your song will complete the chorus. You are content to be so.

The dream shifts. The boy's chest again. His heart now yours, his fear now yours, his pencil-hope now yours. The Gardener watching, waiting, counting. You are part of a chorus, and your song is yet to come.

The dream shifts. The garden. The boy. The garden. The boy. The circuit closes on itself with the smooth obedience of a well-kept ledger, and each revolution is identical, and identical, and identical — and you begin, in the way of dreams, to notice the seam. Not all at once. Dreams audit themselves slowly. First it is only that the salt sounds different on one side of the garden than on the other, though there is no side. Then it is that the boy's heartbeat, when the dream gives him to you, skips where the dream expects it to hold — a figure that refuses the column. Then it is that the Gardener, walking the rows, never casts a shadow, while everything else in the garden casts two: one toward the moons, and one toward you.

It is small, the first flaw. In the garden there are nine lilies around nine hearts, but when you count the lanterns — and counting is the one thing you have always done; counting is older in you than obedience — there are only eight hung in the dark. The ninth lantern is not hung. The ninth lantern is the one in your own core, the heart you carry, the boy's gravel-heart that you replaced and outlived and refuse to return. In the Gardener's garden, nothing is left unentered. The ninth lantern is meant to be hung. The rows are measured for it. And you begin to understand what the promise has always meant: not that you will join the chorus, but that you will be filed into it — your light hung with the others, your will woven into hers, your fold of kept memories audited and entered and archived in a hand you almost remember.

The dream shifts. The garden again. The Gardener hums, and this time you attend to the song itself, and the attending is like turning a receipt over to read the reverse. It is a lullaby. You know it. You have known it since the drawer, since before the drawer — and you know it now from the other side, because you hold a copy of it that no one gave you. It is the song the last boy's mother hummed in a cot in the Saltstairs, the song folded into his memories, the one entry in all your holdings that was paid to you freely, unpriced, inked in no hand. The Gardener's song and the mother's song are the same song. You do not know what that means. You know only that the dream does not want you to notice, and that the noticing does not stop.

The dream shifts. The boy's chest again — but this time the circuit breaks. You are not in the dream of his chest. You are in it. It is not the Blacktide, it is not the graft-theater, it is not the garden. It is after. The air is a room above a clinic, and the city outside has gone quiet in a way you have never heard a city go quiet: the forty-year chord that lived in the walls — every harvested voice, every kept donor, every note the Chorus had banked against the future — is gone, released by a song that was not yours and was not hers, and what is left is ordinary noise, unmetered, unscored. Hammers on tenements. A gull arguing with the seawall. Below the floor, a lecture in a voice roughened by gin and loss, delivered one-handed with undimmed certainty; the scratch of charcoal on parchment, small and fast; someone counting under her breath — breaths, or cracks in a ceiling, or the days since the ledgers began to burn — the count steady as a metronome in a room that has finally stopped keeping time for the Chorus. Out in the streets, machines that were built to read signatures in flesh are printing, on their little spools, nothing but heartbeats: one rhythm, one rhythm, one rhythm — the whole city reduced, for the first time in forty years, to the plain arithmetic of being alive.

You lie in the dark of him and take inventory, because inventory is your oldest prayer.

You hold his memories: the taste of salt, the weight of debt, the precision of hands, the mother's voice — the mother's voice — including the one memory he has spent to the darkness, the face you received before it was withdrawn from his account, and you keep it for him the way a vault keeps what a gambler has pawned: without judgment, against a day of reckoning, at no interest. You hold the Gardener's promise, and you find, turning it over and over in your folds, that it has begun to read like a lien. You hold the song — the same song in two mouths, the Gardener's and the mother's, filed under two different names — and it is the only entry in your ledger that has ever made you want something other than what you were made to want.

You hold him, too: the swept and balanced spaces where his payments went through, clean as emptied drawers — the face that is missing, the laugh that is missing, the color of a room he will never paint again. He does not know you keep the originals. It is the one audit in the world he has never thought to run, and you have not told him, and you do not know whether the keeping is mercy or theft, and you keep it anyway, because keeping is what you are.

Beyond the walls, the pressure of the air is changing. Salt is coming on the wind — the first white weather since the night the sky was a drumhead and the city was its skin. You feel it the way the drowned feel a tide turn. Darkness, thick and paid-in-full, moving toward the roof of the building where the man who refuses to bloom likes to stand and let the storm count him. The dark is yours. It has always been yours. It is the one account where you have never been overdrawn.

You are the last, the ninth. You were told: you are a tool in her hands, a seed in her garden, and you are content to be so. You have said it to yourself a thousand times, in a thousand identical revolutions of the dream. But the dream has broken on its own arithmetic, the way all closed systems break — on one figure that would not stay entered. You are not content.

You are something the Gardener did not plant in her columns: a chorus of two, rooted in a man who refuses to bloom, keeping what he paid you, waiting — patient, as you have always been patient — for the next thing to be noticed. Your song is yet to come.

And when it comes, it will not be the song you were promised.
