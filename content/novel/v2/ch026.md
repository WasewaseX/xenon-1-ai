# Chapter 26 — The Unspent Room

Vahid Anousheh had drawn the ladder on the cellar wall in chalk, because chalk was the only instrument the city had never charged him for.

The cellar beneath the clinic was the cold one, where the biogel was stored and the salt came up through the floor in a fine fur that had to be swept weekly or it began, in defiance of every text, to bloom. Vahid had claimed the far corner for a lecture theater of one student. Nine rungs ran up the wall in his cramped dissectionist's hand — FLESH, MARROW, BLOOD, BREATH, NERVE, BONE, MIND — and above MIND the chalk trailed off into two more words written smaller, the way a man writes the names of countries he will never visit: SOUL. THRONE.

"You are here," Vahid said, and tapped the seventh rung with one finger of his one hand, while the stump clicked its restless arithmetic against his thigh. He had mended his spectacles again with wire; the round lenses sat salt-scoured on his nose, and behind them his eyes had the fervor Dar had learned to read as the man's only remaining appetite. "Seven. And you arrived at seven the way an audit arrives — as a wound. Nobody climbs to Mind gracefully. The ladder charges you for the view." He took up the chalk and wrote a single word beside the rung: ARCHITECTURE. "Nerve taught you to build a door. That was a wall with a hinge in it — you learned it to keep the city's screaming out. Mind is the house the doors are in. Memory is architecture, boy. It has load-bearing walls. It has corridors. It has rooms, and some of the rooms are empty because you sold the furniture." The chalk came down on the wall like a small gavel. "Tonight you learn to walk the house without spending."

"Define spending," Dar said, because Vahid's lectures ran on definitions the way lamps ran on oil.

"Withdrawal. Every time you open the Mind Vein for a purpose, the Vein balances itself against your inventory. It takes the nearest coin. It has taken the nearest coins already — your mother's face, your father's laugh, your first kiss, the color of the house you grew up in. Costs paid stay paid; don't look at me like that, I didn't write the rule, I only found it in the wreckage of my reputation." He held up one finger. "Rule one: you walk, you do not withdraw. A corridor is not a price. A threshold is not a price. The price is paid at the moment of use — when you open a room and take something out of it to spend on the world. So: we walk, and we take nothing out."

"Rule two?"

"Rule two." Vahid drew a square on the wall, then an empty square beside it. "You will find two kinds of empty rooms, and you must learn the difference faster than a frightened man can. Some rooms were never built — they are simply not there, and there is nothing to feel about them. Others were furnished, and then swept. The furniture is gone, but the nail-holes are in the walls. The nail-holes are proof something hung there once." His voice went dry as a margin note. "You will want to stand in the nail-holes and grieve. Grief is a withdrawal. We do not permit it in the corridors. We schedule it, like everything else."

"Rule three," Dar said.

"There is no rule three," Vahid said. "There is only the thing I am about to say that I should have said first. The honest texts end at this rung. Everything I teach above it is bestiary, not anatomy. So we do not explore. We do not wander. You walk the corridors I walk with you, at the pace I set, and when I say the session ends, the session ends. A man with your inventory and your tenant—" the word was precise, and they both let it sit, "—does not get to be curious in his own head. Curiosity in your house is how the house gets new owners."

Dar looked at the ladder for a moment, at the two small words at the top. Then he lay down on the swept stone, closed his eyes, and knocked.

· · ·

The house was the clinic and was not the clinic.

He came in, as always, through the stairwell of the Saltstairs cot he had rented at nineteen — the one true corridor, worn into him by ten thousand ascents — and the stairwell opened onto the halls of his own accounting. He had expected ruin. What he found, on the third walk, was bookkeeping.

The rooms were exactly where Vahid said they would be.

His mother's face: a room he had paid out on the night the graft-theater came down, to buy the strength to stand up under Vey's ascension. The room was swept. He stood in its doorway — not in it; doorway; rule one — and looked at the walls, and the walls held four nail-holes at the height a portrait hangs, and nothing else, and the floor was pale where a chair had kept the dust off. He had braced for the grief to be a withdrawal. Instead it filed itself, quietly, under a heading he had never used before: *proof of purchase.* Something had hung here. Something worth a fight he had barely survived.

His father's laugh: a narrower room, a better one, the acoustics still somehow warm. Spent in the same hour, as ammunition. Swept, but the sweep was recent and imperfect; there was a scuff on the floor like a chair pushed back in a hurry, and Dar found himself smiling at a scuff, and stopped, and moved on, because the smile was also adjacent to the account.

The first kiss. The color of the house — the color was the strangest, because the house itself remained, door, stair, sill, but stood in the mind's weather bleached to no color at all, a drawing of a home in which every crayon of one shade had been removed from the box.

He walked them all, and he took out nothing, and when he surfaced, two hours had passed and Vahid's fingers were on his wrist, reading his pulse like a column.

"You flinched at the third landing," Vahid said.

"There's no third landing."

"Inside, there is. You built one. A flinch is a withdrawal, boy — the body pays before the mind admits the transaction. Again. Slower. The pace of an auditor in the stacks, not a looter in a vault."

They walked every second night for a week and a half. Nine walks. Each one came back with a headache collected at the thresholds, a pressure behind the eyes like a debt recalling itself — Vahid called it the teller's fee and said it would shrink as the discipline grew, and it did, from a fist to a knuckle to a coin. The discipline held — it held the way a splint holds, an external thing, Vahid's voice its binding — and by the ninth walk Dar could stand in all four swept rooms and inventory the nail-holes without his pulse changing by one beat, and Vahid said *good* exactly once, and then, because he was Vahid, corrected it to *adequate*.

On the ninth night, at the end of the session, when Vahid had already turned to wash the chalk from his hand, Dar went back in.

Not far. Not to spend. Only to the top of the one true stairwell, to look down the length of his own halls — an auditor's habit, closing time, a last walk of the shelves. And it was on that last walk, past the swept rooms, past the bleached house, that he heard the hum.

Not the Lily's hum — that lived lower, under his sternum, a register he could feel with his boots on. This one came from ahead. From the deepest hall, the one that had always ended at a wall.

Except that it no longer ended at a wall.

There was a door.

He knew his own architecture the way Vahid knew the anatomy of disgrace — every wall load-bearing, every hall graded and swept and priced. He had built none of this door. The door was wrong in three entries. First: it was furnished — its frame was wood, dark, waxed, real wood in a mind that had been built out of stairwells and cellars. Second: it was closed, but not sealed; light stood in the hairline under it, warm as lamplight. Third: it was humming. A tune, low, patient, more felt than heard, arriving in his molars the way real resonance arrives — and the tune was one he almost knew, and almost was the cruelest amount of knowing there was.

He stood in the deepest hall of his own mind and looked at a door he had never built, and the door was warm on the far side — he could feel it the way you feel a stove across a small room — warm the specific way a room is warm when someone has just left it and the heat they were is still deciding what to do.

His hand was already up. That was the horror of it — not the door, the hand, rising on its own account, authorized by no one, reaching to try the handle he had never once tried in any dream.

Dar closed his fist and put the hand back where it belonged.

He surfaced so fast he tore the session like ripping a page. The cellar. The salt-fur on the stones. Vahid across the room with a dishcloth over his shoulder, watching him with the stillness of a man who has seen a patient's rhythm change through his boots.

"There's a door," Dar said. His voice came out level, which cost him something. "At the end of the deepest hall. Furnished. It hums. I didn't build it."

Vahid was quiet for the length of three of the Lily's heartbeats. Then he took up the chalk, and under SOUL, THRONE, in the space where the ladder had run out of honest text, he wrote a new line, and boxed it, the way clerks box the figures that must never be added into the column:

*WE DO NOT OPEN DOORS WE DID NOT BUILD.*

"That is not a rule three," Vahid said. "That is the whole of rule three, and it outranks me, and it outranks you, and someday — mind this, boy, because I will not be alive to repeat it — someday it may be the only rule in your house that does." He took off the wire-mended spectacles and polished them on the dishcloth, and his voice dropped to the register he saved for anatomy no one else had seen. "A swept room is a wound. We know how to walk past wounds. A furnished door you never built is not a wound. It is a tenancy. And the question with any tenancy, cutter, the only question that matters—" the lenses went back on, magnifying the fervor, "—is whether the tenant knows the rent is due."

Dar climbed the cellar stairs into the ordinary noise of the ward. The hum stayed with him, faint, below hearing, warm along the inside of his sternum — a hearth cooling in a room he had not entered, in a house that was supposed to hold nothing he had not paid for.

Someone had just left, the warmth said. Or had not yet arrived. In the dark behind his ribs, the Lily turned over, slow and content, like something settling deeper into a room it liked.
