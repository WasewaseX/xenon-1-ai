# Chapter 48 — The Walking Quiet

Five days out of Lowmark, the frost changed its mind.

Every dawn of the road, Suri had walked the camp's perimeter before the light came up and read the night's frost off the canvas the way other people read a gauge — grey petals feathered fine as breath on glass, every fan of them pointing west and down, back along the route, toward the deep heartbeat under the Throne shaft. It was the Lily's compass, her appetite, and her homesickness filed in a single entry, and Suri had learned its grammar well enough that she only woke Dar when the grammar broke.

On the fifth morning she woke him.

"They've changed ledgers," she said, and held the canvas where he could see it. The petals still pointed — frost always pointed; that was the one honest thing about it — but not west. The fans had swung through a half-turn in the night, and every petal now stood aimed down the route, toward the horizon they were walking into.

Vell was already at the route stone with her chalk out. She wrote on the stone's flank, under the weathered five-petaled flower, in her narrow counting-house hand: PETALS TURNED IN THE NIGHT. CAUSE: AHEAD. And beneath it, because she was Brack to the bone: LOGGED.

All that day the Lily leaned forward instead of back, and Dar's balance went with her, so that for the first time since the seawalls his body was voting for the direction the party was already going. It should have been a relief. It was not a relief. It was the feeling of a hand that has been resting on your collar turning, gently, into a hand that is pointing.

"You're listing again," Suri said, in the afternoon. "Forward, this time. Like a barge that's decided where it wants to be."

"She leans ahead," Dar said.

"Toward what? There's nothing on this stretch. I counted the masts yesterday. Sixty-one dead ships, one living fly, no water."

"I don't know. Something that takes a whole city to say."

The mountain came over the horizon at dusk, and by noon the next day it was still coming, and by noon it had moved.

Nobody said anything for a while. A mountain that moved was a thing the eye kept filing and kept getting back stamped REFUSED. It was a walled town — walls of black stone, towers, a lantern-glow along the parapets like banked coals — and it stood on legs. Dozens of them, grey, thick as harbor pillars, jointed at the knee, taking their turn in a gait so patient it took half a minute to see that it was a gait at all. The city walked the way old men walk to a service they do not expect to outlive: slowly, correctly, without complaint.

And it made no sound. That was the part that sat worst. A town of that weight should have ground the flats to powder beneath it; the horizon should have carried its thunder the way the route carried its stones. Instead the walking arrived as a feeling in the soles, a slow double pulse through the ground, one-two, one-two, like a heart that had decided to become a pilgrimage.

"The legs are the endowment," Dar said, when he had watched long enough.

The Lily, all the while, was leaning through him toward the walking city with a want that had no manners in it at all — not the banked, patient pull she kept for the deep heartbeat, but the flat, immediate hunger of a thing that has heard a voice through a wall it has been walking toward all its life. Dar held her the way you hold a door against wind. Whatever slept in the Vigil had been shut up behind lead and silence for two hundred years, and his passenger wanted to introduce herself through his ribs at a volume he refused to imagine. He passed Vell the thought and she took it up, chalk moving: THE LEGS ARE THE LEGACY? He shook his head and pointed, and she looked — at the columns, at the regular seam-lines running up each one, at the little dark doors set into the seams at shoulder height, hundreds of them, thousands, each door the size of a coffin-lid — and her chalk stopped.

The Vigil did not rest its walking on machinery. The Order had been filing its dead into the load-bearing work for as long as the city had walked, and the city walked on its founders, on its abbots, on its schoolteachers and its bell-ringers, every one of them sealed into a cell in a leg with their name scored on the inside where only they could read it. The dead of the Vigil did not rest in the ground. The ground of the Vigil was in ongoing session.

They were seen, of course. Long before a shout could have crossed the distance, a single walker came out from the city's shadow across the flats — a grey robe, a rope belt, boots wrapped in felt so the footsteps would cost the world nothing, and a slate hanging from a strap. The monk stopped forty feet off, lifted the slate, and wrote.

YOU BRING FROST. THE CITY READS ITS WEATHER. WELCOME.

The party's own frost had spoken for them before they did. Dar looked back at their camp-canvas, at the grey fans all standing compass-true toward the walking city, and understood that out here the Lily's weather was a letter of introduction, and that it had been read.

Vell stepped forward and wrote on her own slate, and the monk read her counting-house hand, and went very still — and then wrote back, quickly, in a grammar of abbreviations that was Brack's ledger-script to the stroke. Dar watched his only mute companion conduct, in chalk, the first conversation she had been fluent in since her city drowned. Whatever grief it cost her to be understood that well, she banked it, and kept writing.

The monk's slate rose one more time. COME. WALK SOFT. NOTHING ABOVE A WHISPER, BY THE RULE.

The gate stood at ground level between two legs, and the city bent over them as they passed under it like a cathedral leaning down to a doorstep. Inside, the whisper-rule was not a sign; it was the architecture. The streets were swept stone, and the few people abroad moved close to the walls and spoke — when they spoke at all — at a volume that would not have carried across a sickroom. Bells hung muffled in felt. A market was concluding in total silence, buyers and sellers settling prices with fingers and slates, and children passed by in a line, each one speaking, when they had to, at the volume of snow. The city's walking they did not hear at all. It came up through the bones.

The Abbot was waiting at the second gate, and he was the oldest thing in a city of old things — a spare man in grey, salt-scoured, with a voice that had the grain of paper that has been folded and unfolded for decades. He did not greet them. He stepped close to Dar, bowed his grey head, and laid his ear flat against Dar's sternum, over the coat, over the cloth, with the matter-of-fact trespass of a physician and the patience of a man listening to a cellar.

He listened for a long time. Somewhere above them the city took a step.

"Two," the Abbot whispered, straightening. "One of them hers." His eyes were the eyes of a man reading a figure he had only ever seen in other people's margins. "You carry one of the nine."

"We do," Dar said, at the permitted volume.

"Then the Vigil owes you hospitality, and hospitality has one condition." The Abbot's whisper did not waver; it had been worn smooth by a lifetime below the voice-line, and it was the most frightening voice Dar had heard all year because it had clearly never once needed to rise. "Lead-glass quarters. For the world's safety. Indefinitely."

Suri heard the word and priced it where she stood. "Indefinitely," she repeated, flat, making it a column heading.

"It is not a cage," the Abbot said. "The door does not lock. But a seed hears a seed across a mile of quiet, and we keep a quiet here that the world cannot afford to spend. What rides in you would call to what sleeps in us, and what sleeps in us must not be called. The glass muffles you. The offer has no term, because the sleeping has no term. You would walk with us. Many have chosen worse."

"Many have chosen worse," Dar agreed, "and paid for the choosing forever. We'll take the glass for the nights. For the days, I'll work — I saw your necks on the road in. A city that whispers for a living wears its throats the way miners wear their lungs, and I'm the only surgeon this circuit has seen in years. Treat the trade as paid."

The Abbot considered him for a moment with the unhurried attention of a man whose calendar ran in decades.

"Guests are shown one thing," he said at last. "It is the rite. Afterward you may owe us nothing, and we may owe you, and the glass will stand open regardless. Come and see what the Vigil is for."

They went down. The guest-rite's last station was beneath the gate, behind a door of lead that two monks unbarred in absolute silence: a round chamber, walled in lead, faced in panels of glass so old and thick the world beyond it moved like slow water — and in the chamber's center, raised on a plinth of black stone, a cradle.

A cradle of lead, lined with cloth gone grey, sized for nothing larger than a large book. It had the worn, honored look of a thing that had held something precious for a very long time, the way a doorframe holds the shape of every hand that used it.

"The founder's tomb," the Abbot whispered. "He built the Order after the Burning, when the roads were still full of things that sang. He built this chamber to hold the registry's first page. Page zero. The leaf the whole ledger was written on top of — the first account, the one that came before the nine. The Order kept it four circuits. It was our one inheritance and our one duty, and we kept it the way you keep a heart: in lead, in the dark, uncounted."

Dar looked at the empty cradle, and the cold arrived in his hands before the arithmetic did.

"The tomb is empty," he said, already knowing.

"The tomb is empty. A herald came, within the decade. She presented the road's authority, which the founder's own charter obliges us to honor, and she took the page, and she left a receipt we cannot read, and the Order let her go, because we have never once learned how to refuse the route." The Abbot folded his hands. His whisper never changed volume, and that was the worst of it — a man reporting the loss of his order's one sacred thing in the tone of a clerk reading a shortfall he had already carried to his superior. "The road took its own first page back, surgeon. Whatever the collection is for, it has begun collecting the beginning."

Suri wrote nothing. Vell wrote one line and turned the slate, not to the Abbot but to Dar:

IF SHE HAS PAGE ZERO, SHE HAS THE ACCOUNT EVERYTHING ELSE WAS FILED UNDER.

"Yes," Dar said, to the slate, to the cradle, to the city above them taking its slow steps on its dead. "Then we know what she's auditing."

They were led up through the whispering streets to the lead-glass cells as the city walked on into the dark, and the frost on the windowpanes pointed all night, compass-true, at the well at the city's heart where something slept behind lead and listened to the only sound the Vigil permitted itself, which was none at all.
