# Chapter 53 — Go Home, Ninth

They left Gullet at dawn, on the tide the reef no longer believed in, and turned their backs on the light.

The reasons were tallied the night before, in the cellar where the frost pointed down, and the tally was short. The wain was ahead of them, bound for Ashkarad, and it had never once been late. The doctrine was in Dar's head now like a splinter working toward the skin: a door shared between two, opened from the far side by someone who knows the shape of your silence — and the someone had passed under the reef a week ahead of their leaving, running home. And Suri's ledger, open on her knee, held a column she had ruled in another city, and the column's collector was also ahead of them, also bound for Ashkarad, because the heralds collect on the route and the route, for them, ended at home. "We owe the road," she said, closing the book. "Fine. But I'll do my owing where the collateral lives."

Vell spent the last hour at the gate, chalking. On the route stone at the reef's landward approach she wrote the party's direction and the date of their leaving, and below it, in her vertical clerk's hand, a line she had never once written on the whole journey out, because the journey out had been the registry's round read backward, and now it was not: HOME. She wet the chalk against her lip, considered, and underlined it.

Coss saw them to the gate himself, which foremen did not do, and which neither he nor Dar remarked on. At the jaw he stopped, and looked back along the reef — the gantries, the pans, the light running in its seams — and then down, at the ground, for a long moment, as if the carcass's floor were a ceiling and he had finally wondered what room was above it. "The cutter'll be back through," he said, to the route stone, in the tone of a man entering a figure he cannot yet justify. "The books show an open item." "The books show three," Dar said. "A pipe-seat, a factor's foot, and a question I owe an answer to." Coss nodded slowly, the nod of a man who has heard a debt he understands, and went back in, and the jaw-hinge ran on its chain behind them, and the last thing Gullet gave the road was the shift-change moan, rising over the flats behind them all the way to the horizon, calling its hundred-year call after a Sister who had already passed.

The homeward flats were wrong before they had walked a day.

The fog-sea had been the road's one fixture — a grey line at the world's edge, low, receding, obscene in its patience, but present, a creditor's lantern at the boundary of the map. On the second day home Suri climbed a grave-mast with her glass and came down without saying anything, and Dar climbed it after her, and the line was not low anymore. It was gone. The seabed ran out ahead of them to a horizon as clean and dry as a rule drawn under a finished column, and there was no water behind the rule, and no light on it, and no smell of tide. The wind had changed cargo again: salt and old rope and nothing living, a wind with no sea left to remember. Heat-berry strings glowed at the old tide-lines, banked down to embers, a lantern industry serving a customer that had stopped coming.

"The drinking's done," Suri said. "Whatever stood at the horizon and drank — " she looked at Dar's chest, then west, toward Ashkarad, and did not finish, because the only ledger that total was written in was below the salt of the city they were walking toward, and they were not there yet.

On the third day home the mud began to carry writing.

Vell saw it first — she was reading the route stones as she had read them the whole road, flower-marks and water-marks, keeping her reverse-tally — and she stopped, and stood very still, the way she had stood at the mooring-posts the day she proved the footprints were keeping time. Then she pointed, and Dar saw it, and the flats stopped being flats.

Ledger-script. Scored into the drying seabed for a mile, in strokes a hand deep, each one dragged through the mud in a single pass by something that had not paused and had not hurried: a heading, and beneath the heading a column, and beneath the column entries in a grammar Vell's chalk began to transcribe before her face had finished going pale. The language was none of the route's. It was the dead language — the one Dar knew without hearing, the one the lullaby understood itself in, the one a fading composite face had mouthed at him over a reading-tray while the grey came up through it like frost.

"Little root" had been three syllables in that tongue. This was a mile of it.

He worked it the way he worked everything, with his hands, kneeling in the drying mud. Vell's transcription gave him the grammar — a heading in the address-form, columns under it, a total at the end; the notation of a counting-house, whoever had cut it — and Ilay's map gave him the rest. He unrolled the hide-cloth she had pressed into his hand at the seawalls, for an unpriced promise: her charcoal rings, her careful child's hand, the sound-map of the deep music she had been transcribing since the singing went out of the walls. He laid it on the mud beside the scored strokes, and matched them, ring against stroke, and the match was not a resemblance. It was a correspondence, exact, the scored line and the charcoal line two renderings of one text — the deep music, written down by the thing that was walking it — and Ilay's transcription, which had spent a year learning to spell that music, rendered the scored line's heading in one line, in her hand, under his finger:

GO HOME, NINTH.

Suri read it upside down, the way she read everything, and said nothing for the length of ten of her own heartbeats, which was her longest silence of the road.

"It's an address," she said at last. "Not an order. Look at the grammar Vell's got — that heading's the addressee-line. She scored you a letter, Mehran. A mile of it." Then, because she was Suri: "Or it's a stamp. I've seen the route stamp things in bigger letters than this."

They walked the line's length because it was on the way and because neither of them could get the parties to a reading without it. The column under the heading, Vell ruled, was entries: counts of some kind, tallied in the dead language's clean vertical hand, and the counts ran large. "Whatever she was counting," Vell wrote on the slate, and underlined it twice, "she counted it all the way home. Like Tally. Like me. You people count when you're afraid, and she's the biggest of you, and she counted a mile."

And under that, because she was Brack to the bone, she wrote the second thing, the one Dar was already carrying and had not filed: SHE WROTE IT WHERE THE ROAD COULD READ IT. WHATEVER ELSE SHE IS, SHE IS NOT HIDING.

The walker's prints ran beside the scored line the whole way they followed it — small, bare, a diver's spread toes, salt-burned heels, spaced not at one heartbeat's interval now but at a stride that shortened and slowed as the line went on, the stride of someone who had stopped to check her work, or lost her nerve, or both. At the line's end the prints left it, and turned, and went on toward the west and the seawalls, and the mud held them all.

They came up out of the flats on the fifth day and the seawalls were standing, and that was the last thing about them that was right.

The breach was at the foot of the wall, two miles south of the tide-gates, and it was not a breach the way Ashkarad understood breaches. No ram-marks. No burn. No rubble. The wall's base had been opened the way a ledger is opened — a section of the foundation stone sixty feet across lifted and set aside whole, cleanly, its own bedding-marks intact, as if the wall had been a page and something had wanted to read what was printed beneath the city. Through the opening, down, the dark went back into the salt in a passage the size of a cathedral door, and the air that came up out of it was warm and loam-scented and impossibly green at the back of the nose, the exact air that had breathed through an ajar door of salt-glass in the vaults beneath the Throne shaft, the last time Dar had stood in front of it and refused to open it. She had not refused. She had gone under the city while they were away, through her own door, under the walls, home — and the passage's rimed lip was furred grey with frost-petals by the ten thousand, every petal pointing inward, into the dark, down toward the vaults.

Above the breach, on the wall's walk, the tide-gates stood open on a dry harbor. The city beyond was making sounds. Smoke stood over the Saltstairs. Ashkarad had heard something go under it and was, in the city's own way, doing arithmetic about it, and the arithmetic was not finished, and Dar's chest ached suddenly and completely with the Lily leaning down through him toward the deep like a plant at the end of its light. The frost on the wall's inner face agreed with her. It had come up through the stone seams in fans, and every fan pointed down and in, toward the vaults, toward the door he had left ajar at another woman's wish because it had to be her act — and the act, it turned out, had needed only his absence to be performed.

"Not siege," Dar said, to the wall, to the party, to the ledgers. "Passage. She didn't break in. She went under, the way the route goes under — through her own door, at the bottom of her own dark — and the walls were just the last page of it." He looked at the breach, at the size of what had passed through it. "Something enormous is home."

Suri had her ledger out, over the column. Vell had her chalk out, wetting it, already transcribing the rimed stone. Dar stood a moment longer with the scored mile at his back, and then he turned around and looked at it once more, all of it, from the breach — the heading, the column, the walker's shortening stride, the whole mile of a letter scored into the seabed by the walker's hand, which by her prints had passed this way an hour ahead of them, while what had gone under the walls had gone under without waiting for her —

And the last stroke of the heading glistened. Along one edge, low on the line where the blade or the finger had dragged the mud up into its dark paste, the scored script was still wet. One edge of the final stroke shone in the flat light, un-dried, un-greyed, paste-dark, as if the writer had stood here within the hour — and Dar did the arithmetic of the flats, the pace, the tide of days since anything alive had crossed that ground, and the arithmetic came back stamped, and the stamps all said the same thing.

She had been here. She had written GO HOME, NINTH into the mud of the world for the ninth to read. And whatever of her was already ahead — under the walls, under the city, at the bottom of the dark, walking without waiting for her own feet — the ink of her summons was still drying, home was doing its arithmetic, and the ninth was expected.
