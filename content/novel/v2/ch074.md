# Chapter 74 — The Gardener Names It

The rig sat over the salt-glass door like a confession booth built by surveyors: canvas and brass, a body's width off the glass, two chairs, one hand-cranked pulse-reader, and Vahid's law chalked across the canvas in letters a child could read from the stair — NO SONGS / NOT EVEN HUMMING.

Suri counted them down the way she counted everything, which was aloud, in order, without mercy. "Climbers: two. Pulse: his, hers, hers below, one beat behind. Song: none. Debts: current. Kit: one silence, one score." She had appointed herself the door's counter back when the door was whole, and the door was cracked now, and singing in dead languages through a thread of bright nothing, and she counted anyway; that was the whole of her doctrine. The kit went across Isra's body on a diver's strap: eleven pages of sewn notation against her sternum, and beside them, in its wool and wire, a thumbnail of night — the single injectable silence, the last distillation of Shirin Bahar's voice, insurance for a verse nobody had heard begin.

Above them, the clinic held the children and the lamps. Above that, the city. Above that, weeks out on the dry seabed, the wain kept its schedule with the patience of a lien. Dar took his chair. Isra took hers. Vahid put his one hand on the crank and did not wind it yet — "I want the fault before the reading," he said, "not after" — and Roya and Ilay stood at the shaft head with chalk and slate, mapping the climb the only way it could still be mapped, through the ears of a girl who heard the deep assembling itself for company.

The way down had taken them past the dead First Vessel's cathedral, because there was no other road, and Dar had not looked at it, and had looked at it the whole way — the ribs of it, the shelved dark of it, the face with its too many eyes all closed — because he had spent a year thinking of that place as a machine that had been shut down, and the girl behind him was carrying a song for a grandmother, and the arithmetic of the two facts had begun, quietly, to add. He had counted his steps instead, four hundred past the cathedral, and given the count to Suri at the junction, and she had taken it into her columns without comment, because some figures in Ashkarad were carried so they would not have to be said.

Dar closed his eyes and went down Mind-first.

The corridors were as he had left them: the four swept rooms where his spent treasures used to hang, the stair that had no number, the furnished door he had never built at the deepest hall — and past it, the between-space, where the room they had built together stood furnished as a garden neither of them remembered. The chairs were still a bench. The table was still a planting-frame. The angles of the walls still held tended ground, rows that had never been planted here, arranged by a third heartbeat that lived under the floor like an ear pressed to a wall. The common property was on its shelf: the Blacktide refusal; the drowned flats; the memory of a faceless woman singing in a kitchen that belonged to both of them now and to neither; a brother's name, unspoken and unspeakable, held where only the room could reach it.

Isra was already there — she had gone down ahead, the way she always did, quiet as ballast. She stood at the room's far wall. There had never been a far wall. There was one now, and it was a door, and it was not made of anything; it was made of the seam where two silences fit, and Tally's hundred-year-old doctrine stood in it like a lintel: a door shared between two, opened from the far side by someone who knows the shape of your silence. Never by one.

They waited. That was the whole of the technique, and it was the hardest thing Dar had ever done in any Vein: to stand at a door with his hands empty and not spend, not pay, not force, not open — to be company, and let the door check it. Isra's hand found his. Her fingers were cold; the root-toll had been charging her all week, and the slate-lines had been costing more warmth each night, and she stood at the seam of their two silences like a diver on a ledge, spending the last of her body's warmth simply by being known.

The door opened from the far side.

It did not swing. It admitted, the way a host admits — and the shape that knew their silences was the shape that had been learning them for a year, one beat behind, tapping the offbeats, listening under the floor of the room they built. The Sister opened her own door from her own side, and the burned garden came around them like a tide: rows that would not take root, ash falling in a sky the color of a snuffed lamp, the white-gloved figure at the center going on with its endless tending, over and over, forever.

And the Eighth Vein opened.

Not in either of them. Between them. It came on like a graft along a seam — the place where his architecture ended and hers began knitting itself into a shared organ, a pulse with two addresses — and it hurt the way intimacy hurts when it is finally priced: not a wound in a body but a wound between bodies, closing over something that would never again be single. Two hundred steps overhead, in the rig, Isra's spine bowed and Dar's hands locked on the chair arms, and the hand-cranked machine that had printed RHYTHM UNRESOLVED for Dar's two hearts since the beginning of everything tried to file the new rhythm, and failed, and printed, in its small stacked type, the only honest entry it had: RHYTHM: HELD IN COMMON. Frost came up through the rig's floor in matched rings, ring answering ring, one set keyed to his pulse and one to hers, and where the rings crossed, the frost made a pattern Vahid would spend the rest of his life failing to draw: nine points, every point touching every other. In the shaft head's niche, Ilay's hands flew off the stone and covered her ears, not because the sound was loud but because it was plural — the whole city, she signed to Roya's slate, has mouths in it now — and Roya chalked it, and looked at what she had chalked, and did not cross it out.

Every bloomed thing in Ashkarad opened at once, like a book.

The frost on the vault walls spoke first — grey petals turning from the stone like faces. Then the truce-petal in Dar's pocket, and Tally's token warm against his ribs, and the petal folded at Isra's collarbone, and two hundred steps up, in a counting-room, a confiscated pick with its bloom of grey pointing down. And beneath everything, far to the south, up through the stone and the salt and the long dead route, the Cow's moan arrived — the brass-and-glass womb in Gullet's carcass that had begged at shift-change for a hundred years — and for the first time in its bonded, burdened existence, the moan was articulate. The voice was one voice, and it came from all of them, and it was not the Lily's. Dar felt her then, felt her clench small and lean away inside his chest, every petal straining back from her own words — through her, not from her — and the voice that used her petals the way a stamp uses ink was dry, and vast, and patient, and warm the way a ledger is warm, from the friction of a century of entries.

"I am told you have opened the door between you. Good. Doors want keeping. I have kept mine alone for a long time."

"You sold me the last bars of my own song, by the way. I have them filed. I had rather not spend them."

The frost pressed a five-petaled flower into the rime, unhurried, the signature that was not a name, and then the naming came, entry by entry, in the cadence of an audit that had been running for a hundred years and was pleased, at last, to read one aloud.

"Beneath your salt sleeps my garden's heart. It was buried alive at the First Burning, when the world before yours was closed out, and everything I had tended was filed under loss. It does not wake, and it does not die, and it dreams, and it must go on dreaming, and everything I have done for a century I have done to keep it dreaming. It is called the Root."

"Above it I locked my daughter. The first vessel of that burned world — the last living piece of the garden — the lid that held the dream shut. The city was her collar. The harvest was her lullaby. Forty years of borrowed throats, fed down through her floor, one long song to keep the sleeper sleeping."

"My daughter is dead."

"You killed her kindly, which is the only way anything down there has ever been killed, and I have audited the mercy and found it real. But her death left the lid held by songs alone, and songs wear out. The collar is cracked. I hear it, in every language that ever died above it. I have heard it all the way down the route — through Lowmark's water, through the Vigil's glass, through the reef they render at Gullet — and I will tell you what I have told no one in a hundred years: I do not know how long a song holds a door. No one has ever let one get this far. I have come to collect what the books say is mine."

The burned garden stood still around them. Even the ash hung. At the center, the white-gloved figure had stopped tending, and its gloved hands hung at its sides like tools set down, and Dar understood, in the space of one skipped heartbeat, that the figure had been tending rows that would not take root because the Gardener had been tending rows that would not take root, and neither of them had ever once been able to stop.

Below the floor of the room, the Sister's under-line had gone silent — the mother's line, the harmony she had hummed since the beginning of everything — and the silence was total, a held ledger, an entire account paused mid-column. Then it came back. One beat late. Deliberate, exact, and off her mother's downbeat, the way a debtor pays a day late to remind the creditor who is keeping which books.

The voice did not take offense. Offense was not a line item. It filed the harmony's late return where all such returns were filed, and laid out the terms the way a creditor lays out terms — three entries, cleanly ruled, no interest accruing on any of them until the signature.

The whole city heard. That was the shape of it, though Dar did not understand the shape until later: the frost on every window in Ashkarad, the grey bloom on the Gavel's confiscated pick, the petals gone quiet in a hundred pockets and doorways and walls, all of them mouths for the same few minutes, and every soul in the city that was awake, and most of the souls that were not, received the entries the way a city receives a rain — at once, and where they stood. There was no reading to be served. There was no door to post them on. The city itself was the paper, and it had been fertilized for a hundred years against exactly this delivery.

"Plant the ninth. Your city blooms, the seed takes its Throne, and Ashkarad takes its place in my garden — fed, and free, and eaten last of anything alive."

"Burn the garden. The Root with it. Every seed in the world wakes hungry, and we discover together what hunger builds when nothing is tending it."

"Or rewrite the ledger. I do not know what that entry costs. Neither do you. Open it, and we will both find out — and I will tell you now, as one keeper of books to another: no ledger in the history of keeping has ever closed without a life in it."

"You have until the tide forgets to come back."
