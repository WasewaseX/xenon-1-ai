# Chapter 71 — Every Crack Sings

The writ came at dusk, and Roya read it through Ilay's hearing before the messenger's boots reached the step.

The girl had come to her at the attic window with her chalk already out, the way she came when the walls were saying something they shouldn't. "Grey coats," Ilay said. "Lots. And they're humming. Low, so the stones don't hear them planning." The Index did not march in step when it hunted stock children; it hummed — bars of the claiming-hymn, tossed down the streets like lines into water — and waited for the fish of the Church's long investment to rise toward the bait in their blood. Roya's song-lobe had been severed since the Blacktide; the city's living tissue was silence to her, had been silence every day since, and would be silence every day until she died. But Ilay was not silence. Ilay was the best instrument in Ashkarad, and Roya had spent a year learning to read her, and the girl's hearing painted the night the way her own maps painted terrain.

The writ, when the messenger presented it at the clinic door, was everything Ilay had heard and worse: an order of conscription, under one seal, blank-rectangle tar armbands, requiring the surrender of all sibling-stock minors in the sanctuary — for the city's defense, the paper said, as sonar, to be keyed, trained, and billeted at the annex. Signed, dated, stamped. Count every soul once under one seal. The children were the last uncounted souls in Ashkarad, and the Index had come with a barrel of ink.

Dar read it twice and gave the messenger the answer the clinic gave everyone: sanctuary terms, both seals, no names leave the building. The messenger went away the way weather goes. Suri took the front — couriers on the stair, Milla at the desk with the census under her hands, Mrs. Gable from the next tenement already herding the smaller ones back from the windows with the terrible competence of a woman who had raised children through three purges — and Dar made the decision Roya had already mapped from the girl's silence: the children did not stay in the crossfire. They went down.

The only uncounted direction was under the world.

Roya took them into the deep herself, because her maps did and because Ilay's maps did and because the thing she could not do — hear — mattered least where the walls rang like struck glass. Fourteen children, copper-haired Ilay at the head of the line with her hand on the stone like a helm, Mrs. Gable bringing up the rear with Elias's collar in her fist and a storm-lantern she refused to surrender to anyone. Two hundred steps to the dead First Vessel's cathedral, four hundred more through the cold, and at the junction where the reliquary corridor met the down-shaft, Roya set her listening post: a niche, a slate, a stool, a child.

The plan was simple and Suri had signed it: the children shelter in the antechamber above the salt-glass door, in the one room in Ashkarad no faction will enter, because every faction that has met the deep has learned what it does to accounts. The clinic's line holds the junction until the writ's hour burns out and the conscription dies of its own paperwork. Nobody sings. Nobody fights if fighting can be filed instead.

Then the first boots came down the shaft, and the plan's account began to clear.

"Boots," Ilay whispered, palms flat to the stone. "Eleven. Coming down the tithe-stair, the fast way. And under them — picks. A lot of picks. The other side of the cathedral. They're not being quiet." Her face did something Roya had learned to dread, the small scowl of a child hearing people do arithmetic out of tune. "The Index is humming the finding-song. The miners are just walking."

Three forces, one corridor, one door. Gavel's crews, down the freight ways with their lamps hooded — the odds had improved, with the Index in the field; a mine abandoned is a mine the Index owns. An Index conscription squad, humming their way down the fast stair after the children the writ called sonar. And the clinic's line between them, couriers and freed donors and Suri's flat counted voice moving people like freight. Roya could hear none of it. Roya heard Ilay, and Ilay was hearing everything, and that was the whole of her instrument: a girl's ears pressed to the stone of the world, a blind woman's chalk, and between them a map that updated itself with the sound of boot-heels.

"Fourteen at the cathedral," Roya said to the runners, reading the slate as Ilay wrote it. "Miners, double file, freight way. Index squad of eleven on the tithe-stair, four minutes behind. Line to the second pillar, then hold. Suri holds. Nobody advances." She called positions off the girl's ears like bearings off a star, and the line moved in the dark the way ships move on soundings, and it held, and it held, and then the reliquary corridor began to sing.

The Choirmaster had held the corridor since the burning, and he held it now.

He was a gray stalk of a man with a wrist that had never forgiven Dar and a baritone the nursery choirs had once obeyed like weather, and his ground was the long reliquary gallery where the Church had shelved forty years of harvested voices in glass. The voices were gone — freed or silenced by the First Singer's release, the shelves empty, the glass dreaming — but the corridor remembered them, and the Choirmaster remembered the corridor, and he had spent the year since doing what he had always done, which was keeping the dead's house ready for singing. The freed donors stood with him: thirty of the once-harvested, whose throats the Church had borrowed and whose voices had come home, shopmen and dockers and a needlewoman who had been a soprano and was again. They could not fight. They had never needed to.

When the Index squad's finding-song came up the corridor — the claiming-hymn's bars, hummed low, the fisher's bait, designed to wake the hymn-training in stock blood and call the children out of hiding like rungs waking in a ladder — the Choirmaster stepped into the gallery's mouth with his fire-axe grounded like a parade weapon, and gave his thirty the downbeat.

They sang back.

Not the hymn. Never the hymn. They sang the corridor — the old reliquary acoustics he had re-tuned shelf by shelf for a year, the resonances that made the bait-song scatter into harmonics and die, the dissonances that made hymn-trained blood roll over and take notice of nothing. The Index hummed to find children; the corridor ate the hum and gave back a wall of wrongness; the squad's own blood-sense went as blind as Roya's song-lobe. The bait was in the water, and the water fought back.

"Somebody," the Choirmaster said into the sudden wreckage of their song, in the melodic baritone that had once graded children like fruit, "has come to requisition voices in my corridor. The corridor is closed to requisitions." The axe came up off the stone. "Also, I keep the dead, and you are standing on their shelves."

They rushed him. Of course they rushed him; the Index did not retreat from acoustics. The first two went down at the gallery's mouth to the axe and the axiom of a man who had watched forty years of harvests and had exactly one lesson to teach about doors: that they close. The needlewoman and the dockers pulled the third into the lee of an empty reliquary shelf and sat on him, which was not in anyone's training, and the corridor held — bar by bar, wrong note by wrong note, the once-harvested singing the dead's house at the men who had built it.

They held. It was not enough. Nothing that night was enough; the night had been priced for more than anyone had budgeted.

In the niche by the down-shaft, Ilay's hands never stopped moving. GALLERY HOLDS. WRONG NOTES WINNING. MINERS PAST CATHEDRAL. INDEX BEHIND THEM. Roya read the slate as it was written and called the bearings down the runner-line, position by position, like bearings off a star — the only surveyor in Ashkarad mapping a battle she could not hear, through the ears of the one child the battle was coming for.

The miners came through the cathedral with their lamps hooded and their picks shouldered, and the Index squad came on behind the wreck of its own song, and the clinic's line stood at the second pillar with the antechamber and the children and the door behind it, and the three of them met at the mouth of the vault — Gavel's greed, the Index's seal, and the clinic's counted refusal — in the one place in Ashkarad where every account ever opened came due at once.

The melee was short, and ugly, and administrative to the last: boots and lanterns and the flat crash of picks on stone, Suri's voice spending orders like coin, Dar's voice two galleries over telling someone to breathe the way he told patients to breathe while the world tried to bill them, Elias's lungs pulling the smaller children back along the antechamber wall, Mrs. Gable's storm-lantern swinging like a censer. A courier went down with a pick-butt across the shoulder and got up counting her own fingers, because that was the training now. An Index enforcer reached the second pillar, got one hand on Suri's ruined graft-arm, and left the encounter with a broken grip and a lesson about assuming the maimed are the spent. The line bent. It did not break. Somewhere above it all, the actual night turned on its wheels — the tide, the ration bells, the fleet's lanterns weeks away on the dark seabed — indifferent as interest.

And then a Gavel pickman, swung off his footing in the crush, flailed for balance, and his pick — the twin of the pick that had come back flowering, another head from the same quarry racks, another debt-hungry tool out of the same ration night — kissed the salt-glass door beside the flower-keyhole as he went down.

Hairline. A thread of fracture, off the fifth petal of the flower, four inches of bright nothing in the glass.

Every faction stopped. The pickman on the floor stopped, mid-curse. Suri stopped with an order half-spent. The Index squad stopped with its writ literally in its captain's hand. In the listening post, Ilay's hands flew off the stone and covered her ears, and Roya, who had no song-lobe to be wounded by it, felt it anyway, in the slate, in the floor, in her teeth: the crack had begun to sing.

Every crack sang. That was the horror and the anthem of it — not one voice but a choir of them, the fracture branching as it sang, hairline splitting into hairline, and each new thread of it carrying the lullaby in a different dead language. A tongue like water over slate. A tongue like the sound a bell makes falling. A tongue Roya's bones knew and her mind did not — the language of the First Burning, maybe, the language the Gardener's hands had signed in. Fourteen languages before anyone could count them, seventeen, twenty — the door's own deep glass reciting its one song in every tongue that had ever been lost above it, soft as frost forming.

And every one of the languages was singing the same bar first. The opening bars. The child's thread. The four notes a petal had tapped against the far side of this glass a year ago, the four notes a faceless woman had hummed over a kitchen, the four notes the Church had spent forty years of harvested throats feeding down through the floor of the world. The door was not warning them. The door was not threatening them. The door was letting them hear, in every tongue that had ever been lost above it, exactly what it was — the lid, the lock, the lullaby's instrument — while three armies stood in the dark with their lanterns shaking, and no one — not the miners, not the seal, not the line, not the children — could make themselves move, or speak, or do anything but stand in the uncounted dark and listen to the smallest sound the war had ever made, which was the sound of the lid, very quietly, beginning to come apart into music.
