# Chapter 55 — The Woman from the Mud

On the third morning after the coming-home, the fog off the dry seabed lay against the tide-gates like a creditor waiting to be let in, and the Lily leaned Dar toward the door before breakfast — not down, toward the deep, the way she had leaned since the vaults filled, but out, level, seaward, a new bearing she had never once asked for — and he had learned to price her leanings the way sailors price weather, so he was pulling on his coat when the wardens' runner arrived to say that the machine at the gate had printed something it could not file and that the woman it would not file was still standing on the mud, waiting, as if the gate were a teller's window and she had all day.

The tide-gates had not opened for anyone in a season. There was nothing to open them for: the sea had gone out past the horizon's manners, the harbor was a mile of cracked grey mud scored with the writing everyone had now seen and nobody could unsee, and the great gates stood throat-high in salt crust like a mouth too dry to speak. A warden line held the road across the mud, and behind the line, half-sheltered in the gate's iron shadow, stood the thing the machine had choked on.

She was small. That was the first datum, and Dar's eye took it the way it took all triage data, without permission: small, barefoot on salt-plate that would have opened a boot-sole, her feet split and sealed by salt into their own grey boots of scar. Salt-burned everywhere the salt had reached — lips cracked to the tissue, hands scoured to the whorl, the whites of her eyes gone the dull nacre of a shell left out — and she stood at a kind of attention that was not military but tidal, as if she were keeping herself upright the way a post keeps a wire up, because something on the far end of her was still attached. At her collarbone, spread against the skin like a hand laid flat under cloth, was a petal. Black, lace-veined, wide as a palm, rooted — he could see the rooting from six feet, the way the edges of it feathered into her skin without seam, no graft-line, no suture, no account of how it had been paid for — and it moved when she breathed, very slightly, in a rhythm that was not her breathing.

The warden's reader hung from the line-post where they had cranked it and left it, and its last slip still stood in the clip, and Dar did not need to step closer to read it, because he had read this exact slip before, in his own cot, with his own two rhythms on it, in another life:

ERROR: RHYTHM UNRESOLVED. MACHINE FAULT.

"Two rhythms," the warden sergeant said, not quite to Dar, in the tone of a man reporting a leak in a roof he cannot bill anyone for. "One of them keeps following the other. We cranked it four times. Same print."

"I'll take her," Dar said. "Sanctuary terms. Both seals."

The sergeant looked at the woman, and at the mud behind her, where the fog stood in a wall the height of the storm-walls and did not drift, and at the slip, and did what the city had taught its officers to do with things they could not price: he stamped the escort chit and got out from under it.

She came through the gate at her own pace, and that was when Dar felt it — the petal-token in his breast pocket, Old Tally's parting gift, a black petal of a living seed pressed into his hand a world ago to show to a Sister he had not yet met — and the petal-token warmed. Not sun-warm. Body-warm, blood-warm, a warmth arriving in an object that had no blood, rising against his chest like a compass finding north, and the bearing of the warmth was the woman, and the woman's own petal at her collarbone flexed once, slow, like a thing greeting a thing it knew the smell of.

The Lily leaned him two degrees toward her, and he let his boots take it, and inside his chest the hum he lived with rose by half a step and held there, the way a held chord holds in a room where someone has just come home.

At the clinic they did the intake the way the intake had been designed to go, before the fall, for the kind of patient nobody had ever had: shutters closed, lamps hooded, the front room dimmed to a coal-glow, because the darkness was a kindness here and everyone at the desk knew it without being told. She stood in the dim and her shoulders came down half an inch, the first currency she spent, and Suri entered it in her ledger without a word.

Dar examined her on the bench, surgeon's rules, narrating flat so she could stop him with the slate if any of it was wrong. Pulse first: and there it was, the thing the machine had refused — two rhythms, layered, the strong one hers and the second one following it exactly, one beat behind, patient, a copyist laying its line one rule down. The petal next: he got his lamp low over it and she did not flinch from the light, but the petal drew in its edges, a centimeter, the way a leaf draws in drought, and he took the light away and it eased, and he filed that under the oldest rule he had: darkness feeds them, light taxes them. Under the petal's root-line the tissue was cold, and the cold was not dead the way a numb limb is dead; it was cold the way the vaults were cold, a working cold, an expense being met somewhere. He pressed two fingers below her jaw and she allowed it, and her throat moved — once, twice — the full ordinary machinery of speech loading and firing, and nothing came, no air spent, no sound, and the petal at her collarbone flexed in place of the word, and Dar wrote nothing down because there was no word for the entry.

Vahid had come down at the second footstep and stood at the bench's foot with his spectacles pushed up into his hair, one hand gripping the bench-edge, and Dar watched him do the arithmetic he himself had already done: two rhythms, one following; a root alive and toll-taking; a woman who had paid the toll of her own voice somewhere out on the flats, or before the flats, in the deep, in whatever dark agreement had made her a vessel.

"Chalk," Vahid said hoarsely, to the room, and Roya put the slate into the small salt-split hands.

The woman looked at the slate a long moment, the way a debtor looks at a clean page, and then she wrote. She wrote quickly for the first line, quickly and whole, as if it had been written a thousand times already on the inside of her and this was only the copying-out:

ISRA. I BRING HER HOME.

The room did what rooms in Ashkarad did when the numbers arrived: it went still and counted. Her, Dar thought, and heard Suri's chalk stop on the other side of the room, and heard, through six floors of brick and forty feet of salt and the whole sleeping geology of the city, the deep music hold both its voices, and the new voice — the risen one, the one keeping time one beat behind his own — did not change at all, and that was the answer. Isra. I bring her home. The woman had walked inland out of a drowned port across forty miles of seabed with no shoes and no voice, following — or being followed by; the grammar would not settle — the second heartbeat, the enormous patient one, the one the whole city had felt go under the walls and rise into the vaults, and she had arrived at the only gate there was, and the first thing she had told anyone was a delivery note.

Home, Dar thought. The vaults of a salt city, over a thing that turned over in its sleep. It said something about the places this woman had been that Ashkarad's deep qualified.

They fed her, because intake was intake: broth, heat-berries mashed to their glow, water with the salt already in it that her blood was clearly owed. She ate the way Vell ate, precisely, wasting nothing, a person who had renegotiated her relationship with hunger somewhere far from a table. Suri took the slate up after and, over Isra's nod, entered her in the clinic book under the sanctuary heading, and then stood over the page with the chalk not moving, because the name column was full — ISRA — and the debt column had nothing in it, and every other ledger Suri had ever kept had had something in it. "No page," she said finally, to Dar, in the flat voice she kept for audit findings. "No number. No registered debt anywhere in her. She walks in off the flats and the only thing she owes is — " and she stopped, because the only thing she owed was obvious, standing in the dim with a black flower growing out of her chest-bone: she owed the deep. She was carrying, at cost, the only unregistered liability the city had ever hosted that made the Chorus's books look tidy.

The eleven knew what she was before the telling did. The stock children came to the common-room door at dusk in their silent line and looked at her, and she looked back, and there was no surprise in it on either side — the look of a cohort taking attendance and finding itself one column wider. Elias, whose lungs the Church had graded perfect and whose blood the Index's machines now filed under HYMN RESONANT, put his hand flat on the door frame, and Isra put her hand flat on the bench, and Ilay, watching from the stair, said, to Roya, in her spare way: "They ring the same. Like two bells from one cast."

The petal-token stayed warm all day. By evening it was almost hot, a coin of blood-heat over Dar's heart, and the Lily lay quiet in him in a way he had not felt since the salt-glass door — not sleeping, not sated, but attentive, a customer at a counter waiting for the clerk to finish with someone else.

Night came down, and the shutters stayed closed, and the clinic banked its lamps to coals for her, and Isra sat upright on the pallet they had made by the cold cellar door — closest to the deep, farthest from the light, the room's own logic — and Dar, checking the ward rounds at the late bell, found her not sleeping but writing. The slate was on her knee. Her hand was moving very slowly now, no copied-out fluency in it, each letter cut with effort, the chalk creaking, her breath running shallow with the cost of it, the root spending her somewhere for the ink, and he stood in the coal-dark and watched her finish the second line of her whole spoken record in this city, and she turned the slate to him without being asked, because it was not for the room. It was for the man with two heartbeats, because he was the only other person alive who already knew the third party involved.

SHE IS AFRAID OF HER MOTHER.

Dar read it twice, the way you read a bill twice when the total is impossible and the arithmetic is perfect. He thought of a page kept in a coat lining: nine cities, eight stamped and spent, a signature that was not a name but a mark, five petals, over and over, like a stamp. He thought of a face shown to him for three heartbeats on a road a world away, older in the eyes than any mother, mouthing in a dead language an endearment no one had ever said to him: little root.

Her mother, he thought. Not his. Not Isra's.

Theirs.

The petal at Isra's collarbone had gone very still. Down in the deep, under the vaults, under the salt-glass door, the risen heartbeat kept its copyist's time, one beat behind his own, patient as a debt, and for the first time since the night on the roof in the first storm after the Blacktide, Dar heard what the patience was built on, because fear had a rhythm too, and he could hear it now that someone had finally written the word on a slate and shown it to him: she was not waiting.

She was hiding.
