# Chapter 82 — I Don't Get Kept

The charter came up to the quarry at dusk under the parley glove, in a clerical transport that had been built, like everything else in the fleet, to be counted rather than fought: one herald, two tally-bearers, one document-case chained at the wrist, and a reading-lamp.

Mother Gavel received them in her counting-room above the pit, because she received everyone there, and because a room lined with her own ledgers was the only neutral ground she had ever believed in. She was a woman with a face like a roadmap of old scars and eyes flat as a ledger page, and she sat through the herald's preamble the way stone sits through rain.

The herald was good. Better than good — the fleet had sent someone who understood her, which she noted, and resented, and filed. He did not open with tribute or threat. He opened with a valuation. Ashkarad's order-through-debt, he said, had been appraised by the Reclaim's own auditors as the finest surviving instrument of its kind in any city on the schedule. The Church had built the machine; she had kept it running through the silence when everything else of the Church had stopped. The Gardener's fleet did not want to destroy that. It wanted to scale it.

Then he unlocked the case, and laid the charter on her counting-table, and read it aloud.

The terms were beautiful. That was the first thing she noticed, and it was the tell; she had never once in her life signed a beautiful document. Warden's partner. The city's charter of the Reclaim: her crews as the schedule's enforcers, her water-works as the Reclaim's ration, her counting-room as the branch office of the audit, her title — Mother Gavel, Warden's Partner, Ashkarad — engraved on the first page in letters that had cost someone money. In exchange: the Reclaim's protection, the Reclaim's roads, the Reclaim's ledgers. Maintenance of order in perpetuity, at scale, per G.

It was aimed at her like an arrow she had described to a fletcher herself. Order-through-debt was her creed, and had been since she was fourteen years old in a debt-prison in the seawalls, watching a clerk feed a family into the ledgers one finger at a time and understanding, with the terrible clarity of the young, that the clerk was the only person in the room with any power at all. She had spent her life becoming the clerk. The fleet had read her life the way it read everything, and offered her the clerkship of the world.

And it was honest, which was the insulting part. The pit's protection against the schedule — real. The roads open to her freight — real. The harvest deferred while she enforced order — real, and written plainly in appendix two, DEFERRAL, NOT REMISSION, because the fleet did not lie in its charters. The fleet did not need to. It had her own method to borrow, and it borrowed well.

She read it three times while the herald waited, and the third reading was the one she paid for, because it found the clause the beauty was built to hide. Page nine, sub-section four: upon signature, all outstanding obligations of the partner's crews — wage-debts, ration-debts, pit-debts, flesh-debts, every column of every book she had ever kept on a human being — were assumed by the Reclaim. Assumed. Her crews' debts would stop being hers, and start being the fleet's. Her people would pass, on signature, from her ledgers into the Gardener's, where interest ran by the salt-moon and the term of the loan was the schedule, and the schedule was the harvest, and the harvest was forever.

She had seen what the fleet's interest did to a debtor. Everyone in Ashkarad had seen it by now, in miniature, in one man: pickman Sedge, who had swung a pick at a flower-keyhole and come away owing the deep a count that ran backward through him at every threshold. She had kept Sedge on full wages since, though he could no longer work; she kept him the way you keep a margin note in a book you wrote yourself. And page nine wanted the whole quarry in margins like that, in a book she would never see again, kept by a hand that signed per G.

The herald watched her read it. He had, she granted, honest eyes. He believed the mercy. That was the saddest column in the whole document.

"You've written me a fine title," she said.

"The partner's title is genuine," the herald said. "The Warden of the Reclaim signs personally. Per G. It is —"

"It is a leash with my name engraved on the collar," Mother Gavel said, "and I know engraving when I see it, because I have paid for it." She stood. She took the charter off her table in both hands, went down the stair to the pit floor, where the evening crews were racking their tools and the lamps were coming up along the galleries, and she stood on her own mark in the center of her own sand, and she held the thing up so every crew in the quarry could see the seals.

"Forty years I have kept ledgers on every soul in this city that asked me to," she said, and the brass tube carried it to the last gallery without her raising her voice, the way it always had. "You owe me hours. You owe me water. Some of you owe me flesh. It's all in the books, and the books are mine, and you have always known where you stood, because I show you the page when you ask." She let that sit. "This paper says the fleet will take those books. All of them. Every name. Every debt. It says you'll be their entries and I'll be their clerk, and they'll call it partnership, and they'll call it scale, and the interest will run until the schedule comes due — and you've all seen what comes due."

She turned to the brazier. She fed the charter to the coals page by page, seals first, and stood watching it with her scarred hands folded while it burned, and the crews watched her watch it, and nobody in the quarry made a sound until the last seal curled.

"I keep ledgers," Mother Gavel said. "I don't get kept."

The crews did not celebrate. She had not expected them to, and it said something about how far her people had come that they did not cheer her either. They came to her counting-room in deputations instead, all that night, the way crews come to a boss when the arithmetic has frightened them.

The pump-crew came first, and they were for the charter — water guaranteed at scale, work guaranteed at scale, their ration-debts assumed and their names on the Reclaim's protection rolls. "It's paper we'd never get from anywhere else," their overman said, standing where he had stood for twenty years. "It's the safest column on the seabed."

"It's the safest column in the book because the book eats cities," Mother Gavel said. "Brack sat in a safe column too. Read me Brack."

The pit-crews came, and they were against — they had seen the flowering pick, some of them had friends counting backward in the infirmary — and the freight-crews came, and they were split, and a girl of seventeen from the lamp-crews came alone at the dead hour with her wage-debt book in both hands and put it on the counting-room table, open, and said: "If they take the book, do I stop owing you?" and Mother Gavel looked at the girl's twenty-two entries, every one entered in her own hand with its reason written plain beside it — sick mother, broken arm, the winter the pumps froze — and felt the whole weight of what a ledger is, which is a promise you made to someone weaker than you, kept until it costs you.

"No," she said. "You stop owing me when I say the column's paid, and I haven't said it, and no one signs my people over my signature. Not even me. Especially not me. Go to your bunk."

It was after the girl left, with the lamps burned down and the ash of the charter still in the brazier's draft, that she took the brass tube down from the brackets by the window and set it on the table and looked at it while the night ran out. It had never once been pointed at her own people in forty years. It had pointed at enemies, at the sand, at the sky. There was no keeping it and there was no keeping herself out of what was coming; the only choice the arithmetic left was which books survived it.

When Dar came to the quarry the next morning, because word travels in Ashkarad faster than fire, he found her in the counting-room with the ash swept and the ledgers open and the day's water-rations ruled up in her own hand, as if nothing had happened, because as far as her books were concerned nothing had.

They traded the way they always traded, which was to say like two surgeons consulting over a patient neither could save alone. Her price for joining the third way was the water-works: not the ration — she had read the clinic's censer-lines and Suri's breath-books and knew exactly what the new order intended to do with arithmetic — but the works themselves. The pumps, the mains, the heads, the hours. Paid in function and never in flesh.

"You'll run the water for the whole city," Dar said. "Under the census, not over it. No debts in the pipes. Suri audits the ration and posts it open."

"Fine," Mother Gavel said. "One correction. Not no debts in the pipes. No new debts in the pipes. The old ones are mine to retire and I'll retire them my way, at my pace, and your registrar can watch me do it." She drew a sheet toward her and began, without ceremony, to draft the articles in her own square hand, ruling the columns where their two books would meet. "I don't work for a mob, Mehran. I work for the arithmetic. Your third way doesn't have one yet. I'm giving you mine. Don't waste it."

It was when he stood to go that she stopped him at the door.

"Take this." The brass speaking-tube came off its brackets by the counting-room window — the old pit-horn, brass gone brown at the mouth from forty years of her voice, dented once by a thrown bottle in a night she had never bothered to have repaired. She put it in his hands with no ceremony at all, and its weight surprised him, which she watched him hide, which satisfied her. "The day the city has to be counted in — all of it, one count, everyone at once — you'll want a voice that reaches. Mine reaches. I'm not giving it to you, mind. I'm lending it, and the day it calls that count, it comes home to me, because I'm the one who'll be calling." She turned back to her columns. "Carry it. Don't talk into it. If I hear my voice coming out of it before the day, I'll want the weight back the same hour."

"Why hand it to me now?"

"Because the tide is coming back," Mother Gavel said, flat as a ledger page, "and everything that floats in on it is going to want the city to kneel, and I have decided, after considerable accounting, that Ashkarad is the only debtor in this ledger I have never once been ashamed to carry." She went back to her columns. "One condition. Write it down."

Dar waited.

"When the new order writes its books," Mother Gavel said, not looking up, the nib moving through the figures like a pick through soft seam, "my crews' debts go into the fire first. Before yours. Before the Church's. Before the deep's. First." The nib stopped. She looked at him then with her eyes flat as the page she kept, and there was nothing in them to argue with. "Every debt in this quarry I have counted myself, and every one of them I can justify except that they exist, and if freedom is coming to this city, it arrives at my people's houses before it arrives at mine. That's the price. That's the whole price. Now get out of my counting-room — I have water to move."
