# Chapter 63 — Shirin's Marginalia

Vahid Anousheh's life's work ended at Bone, and he had known it for forty years, and he had only now, with the chalk dust still on his fingers from a ladder he had drawn a hundred times, admitted it in public.

He stood in the clinic cellar under the Nine Veins as he had chalked it — Flesh, Marrow, Blood, Breath, Nerve, Bone, a rung for a year of his disrepute, each one documented in his own discredited hand — and above Bone the ladder was bare wall. Mind he never chalked at all, because Mind had never been climbed by the book. Mind had opened in a boy's head during the Blacktide the way a tooth opens a jaw: as a wound, with the price paid in the coinage of a mother's face. There was no marginalia for that rung. There was a scar.

"Past the sixth rung," he told the room — Dar, Suri, Roya, Isra seated by the stove with her slate on her knee, and the climbing-rig's manifest spread open on the bench like a diagnosis — "I am a man reading someone else's mail. I taught this boy the first six rungs because a better anatomist than me taught me the first six rungs, and she is forty years dead, and everything above Bone lives in fourteen cylinders of wax, and I have been avoiding them because listening to her voice was a debt I did not know how to service sober."

He put on his spectacles — round, salt-scoured, mended with wire — and took them off again, which was as close as he came to a rhetorical flourish.

"So tonight we service it."

The listening took the whole night and most of the gin, and it was not a matter of putting a needle to wax. The fourteen cylinders held the formal archive — the lectures, the graft-tolerances, the tissue-gradients dictated in the lecture voice she used when she knew the machine was her only student — and between the formal passages her voice dropped, and she talked to herself while she worked, the way working anatomists do, and it was in the talk, in the margins of her own recordings, that Shirin Bahar had hidden what she had never dared publish: her thinking on the upper Veins.

They found the theory on the third cylinder, in the gap between a protocol on sinus-repair and a note about the cost of lamp oil.

"*Marginalia,*" she said, in the young-dark voice of a woman talking to her own hands. "*For nobody. For me. Everyone asks me — the funding men, the deacons, Vahid when he is drunk and honest — how high does the ladder go. They all draw it as a height. Flesh to Throne, a climb, a spire. They are wrong, and the wrongness is the whole of the medicine. The first six veins are depth — going down into your own body, cellar by cellar, and every one of them makes you more of what you already were. Mind is not depth. Mind is up — out of the cellar into the architecture, the whole house of you at once. And the eighth —*"

The needle hissed through a pause that Vahid, who had edited recordings in his youth, identified professionally as a woman deciding whether to say a thing and deciding yes.

"*The eighth goes sideways. Soul is not a depth in you and not a height of you. It is a shared organ. It is grown between two people, the way a bridge is grown between two banks, and it grafts along the line where two silences fit. You cannot grow it in one body for the same reason you cannot shake hands with yourself. Every honest text that says otherwise was written by someone who tried it alone, and every such text stops mid-sentence, because the writers stop mid-everything. The door is shared between two or it is not a door. That is the whole of it. That is what I would have told the funding men if any of them had ever asked what a soul was for.*"

Vahid made the room sit with it for a while. Isra wrote one line on her slate and turned it to the company: THE SISTER IS THE OTHER BANK. Roya, mask motionless, said: "She has been holding her side of a bridge for a century with nobody on it. That's not an entity down there. That's a co-signer."

The climbing-rig went up over the salt-glass door over the next six days, and it was the strangest construction job in the city's history, because the site was seven hundred and eleven steps down and the client was a door that must not be touched.

The haul alone was a civic effort: sixty-two loads, ferried down the shaft on a winch-brake Suri commandeered from the Gavel's own dock crews and paid for, in Gavel's hearing, at the Gavel's own posted rates, because if the city was going to build an instrument at the bottom of its deepest hole it might as well do it through the only logistics chain that functioned. Milla Ferun kept the load-manifests — the woman who had spent a career stamping other people's tithes now checked off salt blocks and brass rod in a fair clerk's hand, and reported, only half-joking, that the manifests were the first honest paperwork she had ever processed. Every load was counted going down; every block was counted again going into the wall; Vahid signed off each stage with the ceremony of a man who had been stripped of the right to sign anything and had decided, years ago, to keep doing it anyway.

They built in the antechamber, against the ringing salt, a body's width off the glass. A floor of fitted salt blocks to level the sloping vault floor. A canvas wall to hold the warmth in and the vast draught of the door out — canvas, not timber, because timber sang, faintly, at resonances nobody could hear but everybody's blood could read. A frame of brass. And in the middle of the cold room, facing each other across four feet of salt floor, two chairs — plain, webbed, built in the clinic's workshop by a one-handed professor and a young apprentice with perfect lungs, and Vahid personally oiled every joint so that neither chair would creak when it needed to hold two people perfectly still.

One pulse-reader stood on a brass tripod at the head of the two chairs: the old machine, the one Suri had stolen for a debt-ridden intern to practice on a lifetime ago, the first machine in Ashkarad ever to print RHYTHM UNRESOLVED and mean it. Vahid had asked for it by name. Its crank was oiled; its stylus was new; its paper tray was stocked with three hundred slips. Whatever happened in the room between the two climbers, the machine would write it down, and the machine had never yet lied to this particular chest.

On the canvas wall, in chalk, in his own hand, Vahid wrote the room's law, the way he had once boxed a law on the wall of the cold cellar upstairs: NO SONGS. Under it, smaller: NOT EVEN HUMMING. The reason went unwritten because everyone who would use the room already knew it: songs were the heralds' grammar, the hymns' grammar, the deep's grammar; a song was a key that fit every lock the Gardener had ever grown. The climbing room would be the quietest volume in Ashkarad. The climbers would enter it the way divers enter water: with their mouths shut and their blood loud.

The briefings were Vahid's, and he did not enjoy them, because a good briefing is a forecast and he was about to send two people past the edge of his knowledge. He taught them the entry — Mind first, the architecture of the self as the staging ground; the between-space as Shirin had modeled it, not a place in either mind but the seam where two prepared minds met. He taught them the discipline: pay deliberately, never pay in panic; sit back-to-back in the physical chairs until the shared room held a floor, because the body's habit is the mind's handrail. He taught them the failure modes — the pull to look at each other's payments before the walls were up; the itch to take back what had been given; and the one he would not say aloud, which went into the manifest instead, in the flat register he reserved for things he could not bear to say standing up: *the room does not care which of you it keeps.* He taught them the rule of payment: the room between them would hold what was given, but what was given was *given*, and Shirin's marginalia on that point, found on the ninth cylinder, were copied out flat and posted on the canvas beside the room's law: *WHAT IS POOLLED IS POOLED FOREVER. THE ORGAN GROWS FROM WHAT TWO PEOPLE NO LONGER OWN ALONE. IF YOU ARE NOT PREPARED TO HEAR A STRANGER WALKING IN YOUR HOUSE, DO NOT BUILD A HOUSE WITH THEM.*

"Choose the payments like a surgeon chooses flaps," he said. "Nothing hidden, nothing vital, nothing you will lie about later. The organ grows from what you no longer own alone — so own nothing in that room that you cannot stand to have witnessed." He looked at Dar, and then, because it was owed, at Isra. "And the risks past that are posted past my signature, which means I cannot price them, which means I will not pretend to. The honest text ends where my partner's voice ends. I have sent boys into wounds I understood. I am sending two people into an organ I have only heard described. Bring each other back. That is the entire discipline. Everything else is anatomy."

The last cylinder he played alone, at the end, after the others had gone up to their beds. It was the fourteenth — the final recording, the one the archive's own index had listed as *damaged, truncated* — and he had been saving it the way a man saves the last of anything. It was late dictation; her voice was older here, rougher, rapid, the voice of the last spring, and it was not a lecture and not a marginalia. It was a message, left the way people leave messages in wars: short, addressed to no one, addressed to whoever. She said — the wax crackled, and his one hand lay flat on the bench as if the bench were a pulse —

"*If anyone ever climbs it — if anyone actually builds the room — the door will show them the far side, and they will ask me, if they can still ask anything, what it was. I stripped my own chair rather than say it to the deacons, and I will say it once here, to the wax, where the deacons keep no votes. The far side is not a place. And when the organ takes the whole weight of you both — and then you will hear the sound—*"

The recording stopped.

Not wore out. Not skipped. Stopped — a clean cut in the wax, the groove ending mid-word the way a page ends mid-sentence, and Vahid sat in the lamplight with the stylus ticking the run-out and did not move for a long time.

Because he had heard that truncation before. They all had. The Lily's oldest dream — the loop Roya had once wired a song-lobe to map, the dream of the salt garden and the nine lantern-heartbeats and the woman with bone-white hands — ended, mid-phrase, at the same word. *The sound.* A century between the two recordings. A woman's wax and a seed's sleep. The same knife.

Vahid took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, thoroughly, twice, and said to the empty cellar, in the voice he used for lectures no student would ever pass:

"Someone has edited the woman I loved and the thing in that boy's chest with the same blade. Which means the cut is upstream of both of them. Which means, my dear Shirin, that you got close enough to frighten the *publisher*."

He racked the cylinder with the others. Fourteen wax souls on a brass spindle, and the fifteenth voice they needed was a door, and the door was open, and it was waiting on two chairs and a machine that had never yet lied.
