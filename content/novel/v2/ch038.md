# Chapter 38 — Sibling Stock

Vahid Anousheh had performed dissections in better rooms than this and in worse, and he had learned long ago that the room never mattered; only the specimen's honesty mattered, and specimens lied in every room ever built. He pinned the two documents to the cellar corkboard with the brass pins he had once used on excised hearts, poured himself the evening gin out of ritual, and did not drink it, because tonight he was working on Shirin's half of the ladder and Shirin had hated to see him pour and not think.

The first document was Dar's copy of a registry page, taken out of the Choratorium's reliquary archive in the raiding season and copied in a surgeon's exact hand:

VESSEL: ILAY. AGE: 9. GRADE: PERFECT. RESERVED: THRONE.

And down at the bottom, in its own ruled column, the line that had been staring at them for two months like a debt no one would read aloud:

SIBLING STOCK: ACTIVE.

The second document was the Choirmaster's — one unburned copy of the harvest-registry, salvaged from the census fires entire, the vessel list running back generations, page after page of children entered in the Church's own notation. The man himself sat in the corner of the cellar where the lamplight went to fail, half-starved still, grey as his old robes, keeping his hands folded like a choirboy at a funeral he had conducted. Vahid had not invited him. Vahid could not have kept him out. It was, the Professor conceded, the man's book in a way nothing would ever be Vahid's.

They had both been staring at the notation for a week. Tonight, with Brack's zero still going around the city like a coin no one wanted to touch, the notation opened.

It was the graft-lineage code. That was the shame of it, the pure mechanical shame: he recognized it in four seconds once he stopped reading it as a Church script and read it as a ledger. The conventions were his and Shirin's — the serif conventions, the descent arrows, the percentage columns, the whole honest apparatus they had built at Saint Ferrous to match graft to patient so that no one would ever again be sold a heart that would be rejected on the table. A notation is innocent. A notation only makes saying easier. He sat with his one hand flat on the desk and watched the Church's forty years of saying pour through his own grammar, generation by generation.

Here was the whole of it, in their language, in the Church's hand:

The nursery was not an orphanage. It had never been an orphanage. The vessels were not taken children, graded; they were grown children, entered — and the stock column did not record siblinghood as blood records it. It recorded tissue-lineage. Every line in the vessel list descended, through as many as five recorded generations, from the same root entry, and the root entry was written in the notation reserved for specimens, not patients, and its catalogue number was one Vahid had seen once in his life, in the margin of an older man's forbidden map, with a warning in ledger-script beside it.

The First Vessel. The deep thing beneath the Throne shaft. Forty years of harvested organs grown onto one dreaming host — and before that, before the harvests, a source. The Church had been drawing tissue off the First Vessel the way a landlord draws rent, and growing the draw into children, cohort after cohort, grading each child's acceptance of graft, burning the failures into the great unlabeled columns at the back of the book — Vahid counted the failure columns the way you count a plague — and refining, refining, refining, until this last cohort of twelve, of whom eleven had walked out of the nursery on the raiding night, and one of whom was:

GRADE: PERFECT.

"Ilay," Vahid said aloud, to no one, because the name had to be said in a human voice once the figures had said it in theirs.

"She was never reserved for the Throne," he said, still in the human voice, because it helped. "She was grown toward it. The column isn't a booking, boy — it's a batch. The whole cohort is one batch. Twelve children off one root. Sibling stock, active." He drank nothing. "The Church has been farming the First Vessel's flesh into children for three generations, and we pulled eleven of the harvest out of a building that was still billing their keep."

From the corner, the Choirmaster spoke, and his smooth baritone was a ruin of what it had been on the harvest floor. "Grading nights," he said. "You know them now as what they were. I was told the stock was the city's — that the vessels were Ashkarad's children, tithed up and perfected for a great work. Hymns to make the tissue compliant, we called it. Compliance." His folded hands did not move. "No one ever asked me whose children the children were. It was not a question the hymns had a verse for."

"And you, Anousheh," Vahid said to himself, because the confession would not be honest with only the Church in it. He had built the ruler. They had measured the pantry — no. He took the thought back and wrote it correctly: they had measured children with it, and called the children stock, and the stock active, and active was a word from his own percentage columns, a word that meant the line breeds true.

Shirin had seen where the grammar pointed. That was in her last letter, which he had reread so many times the paper was going translucent at the folds: They have stopped asking what the seed can do for a body. They are asking what bodies can do for the seed. She had gone at the Church over that question like a woman going at a locked door with her shoulder. The Church had kept her voice six years.

He poured the gin down the drain, all of it, undrunk, and unpinned the first document, and put the second in his case, because if the clinic burned tonight the vessel list would not burn with it. Not ever again.

Roya brought Ilay down the cellar stairs an hour after midnight, and Vahid knew from the set of her masked jaw before either of them spoke that the night had decided to continue presenting evidence.

The girl's drawings had changed again. Roya pinned them up beside the registry pages without comment — Roya had begun translating the child's hearing into chalk the way she had once mapped dreams, and her chalkwork had learned a terrifying fluency — and where last month's sheets carried the great slow spiral converging under the Throne shaft, tonight's sheet carried the same spiral with sound written into it: short strokes, overtones, a pitch. Ilay stood in the lamplight with her copper hair pushed back from her river-stone eyes and looked at the corkboard full of her own kind's bookkeeping without one flicker of recognition, and then she began, absently, the way a clock runs, to hum.

Vahid's whole body went still, the way it had gone still in the anatomy theater forty years ago when a heart did something hearts were not supposed to do.

The hum was the music. He knew that at once — the music only Ilay heard, the deep music from under the salt, the thing they had all assumed was a broadcast, a beacon, the second heartbeat's voice reaching up through the vaults. The girl hummed it because she could not not hum it; it was in her the way the hum in the walls had once been in the whole city.

And the cellar answered her.

Not echoed. Answered. The salt in the walls — old, deep, memory-crammed salt — took her phrase and returned it half a step down and a breath behind, the way a companion voice does, and Ilay, who was used to this and had never been taught to notice it, hummed on, changing her pitch slightly, and the deep changed with her. Twice more. Twice more exact.

Vahid laid Roya's spiral-chart over Dar's copied page, and the innermost ring of the spiral sat on the registry like a signature sitting on a line, and the arithmetic assembled itself in his head with the horrid smoothness of a sum that has only ever had one answer.

It is not singing to her. It is tuning to her.

The realization arrived in the lecture-voice he could never fully retire, because lecture-voice was how he kept the front of his skull orderly: The vessel stock is First Vessel tissue. The Second — the one behind the salt-glass door, the patient immense heartbeat, the sister — is kin to that tissue as a sibling is kin. When it sounds, it sounds down the family line. The child is not receiving a message. She is the message's tuning fork. It plays her to hear what else still rings. Somewhere below the city, something is striking the last cohort of the Church's harvest, one child at a time, and counting what comes back — and the child it has struck most often, the child it is always striking, is the one stamped PERFECT.

"Stop," Roya said — to the room, to the deep, to nobody; and her tin-muffled voice cracked on the word like ice under a boot. "She's nine."

"She's the tuning fork," Vahid said, and hated the sentence, and did not withdraw it, because the withdrawal of true sentences was the luxury that had cost Shirin everything. "And whoever struck the first note knows the pitch."

The Choirmaster had put his face into his folded hands. Vahid did not bother to classify the gesture. Some accounts you let a man settle privately.

It was Ilay who ended the night, because Ilay ended everything the deep began, in her small literal voice, without ceremony.

She had stopped humming. She was standing by the corkboard with her head tilted, listening the way she listened, to the music that was not a broadcast and was not a message and was somehow, tonight, at this hour, louder than it had ever been — louder, Vahid thought, since the barge, since the count of zero came into the harbor, as if the whole deep had leaned toward the sound of a city's population being stamped out and stayed leaning.

"The music has taught me a new word," she said.

Vahid did not move. "Taught," he said. "Not gave."

"It leaks," Ilay said. "Like under a door." She held out her hand to Roya without looking, and Roya put the chalk in it, because that was their arrangement now, the map-keeper and the girl who heard what could no longer be mapped, and Ilay knelt at the cellar's chalk wall — the wall where the Nine Veins ladder had been drawn in an older man's hand and a younger man's hope — and beneath the ladder, in her careful child's letters, each stroke ruled first with her thumbnail like a counter ruling a margin, she wrote the word she had been given through the deep by whatever spoke down there in the dark, over and over now, in the voice of the family line:

RECLAIM

She wrote it the way children write words they do not know the price of. Then she sat back on her heels and looked up at Vahid with her river-stone eyes and asked the only question she had ever asked him in two months of living above his lectures.

"It's not a word for a thing," she said. "It's a word for a debt. Isn't it?"

Vahid looked at the registry page on the corkboard, at the Church's own notation in the Church's own hand, at the column that read SIBLING STOCK: ACTIVE, and he heard Shirin's last letter say what it had said all along, in the grammar he had built for honest matching so long ago: They are asking what bodies can do for the seed.

"Yes," he said. "It is."
