# Chapter 68 — The Pick Comes Back Flowering

Mother Gavel kept the pump-works the way she kept everything: on the books, and ahead of them. She met Dar in the ration hall at the eighth bell, under the new pipework, at a table her clerks had cleared to bare wood, and she let him stand a while — not discourtesy, procedure; you priced a man's posture before you priced his terms — and then she sat, and folded her scarred hands, and opened the meeting the way she opened all of them, with the arithmetic.

"Herald movement on the flats," she said. "Caravan flags running the Reclaim route, week on week. Your wain is coming, cutter. When it comes, the vault-mouth is the door the city fights at, and tonight my crews can hold it." She slid a paper across the bare wood. "Here is the price."

Dar did not touch it. He read it from where it lay, the way you read a bill you have no intention of paying, and the terms were exactly the terms her runners had promised: the Gavel's crews under Gavel's discipline to hold the shaft, the reliquary corridor, and the antechamber against all comers — Index, herald, fire, or famine — until the fleet's business was done. In exchange, on the day the city's affairs resumed, the deep became hers to work. The salt vaults. The salt-glass. The cathedral of the dead god and everything its flesh would render. A mine, with the clinic's name on the deed of surrender, and her water-debt against the clinic forgiven forever, in perpetuity, in the manner of perpetuities.

"You're paying me in guards," she said, "for what you can't guard. And you're paying me in water you can't pump. I've read your ledgers, Mehran. You're a rich man in every currency but the two that spend."

She had read more than his ledgers. Four days ago her ledgermen had surrendered a debt-book page under grease-pencil, and the retirement coin had come across her counter in sealed boxes, and the asset the coin retired had walked out of her books and back into the clinic's census without one word of thanks. She did not resent it; resentment was interest paid on a principal you refused to collect. But the arithmetic of it sharpened the terms she was about to lose him with: the clinic could raise ten thousand in coin in four days. What it could not raise was water in a drought, or men in a siege, and both were coming up the Reclaim road with the caravans.

"The deep isn't mine to lease," Dar said. "That's the first refusal and the whole one. There's a woman behind that door who has stood at the threshold of her own rescue for a hundred years, and above her there's a grave, and under her there's a heart, and you're proposing to put a shaft through all three of them and call it yield."

"I'm proposing to put men with lamps on a frontier," Gavel said. "Frontiers pay or they don't. The ones that don't get closed."

"It's not a frontier. It's a patient." Dar's voice stayed in the register he used on wards. "You don't mine a patient, Mother. You triage one."

She looked at him for a long moment with her eyes flat as a ledger page, and Suri, who had come as far as the hall's door and no farther, would say afterward that you could hear the column closing. "Then I'll price it again when you're hungry," Gavel said. "The ration runs on my pumps, cutter. Everything in this city runs on my pumps now. Refusals are entry numbers. Good evening."

He went. She sat a while in the ration hall and did not look at the paper he had not touched, and her clerks entered the refusal in the day-book: CLINIC — TERMS DECLINED, NO COUNTER. Under the column for assets, she drew a line, and under the line, in her own hand, she wrote the word she was to think of, later, at the shaft-head, with a flowering pick standing in the corner of her counting-room like an umbrella nobody claimed.

The word was: ODDS.

They went down four nights later, without her order and without her knowledge, and she reconstructed it afterward from depositions the way she reconstructed everything, in statements, in order, in ink.

Statement one: the overman, Harl. His crews were owed water — three shifts of pump-work under ration law, billed and unpaid since the Index seized the eastern mains, and a crew owed water under a water-for-work ration is a crew with nothing to trade but risk. The deep was full of the one wealth the war had not touched: salt-glass by the ton, walls that rang like struck glass, the cut flower in the door's lock like a gem the size of a hand. The dead god's cathedral was full of tissue the old Church had graded and certified and never finished rendering — biogel stock, Church-grade, enough to buy the whole crew out of the ration books twice over. Harl's arithmetic, in his own words, taken down flat: The church priced that flesh for forty years. The church is gone. Prices don't die with the church. Somebody was going to go down and collect.

Statement two: the pickman, Sedge. He had swung at the flower-keyhole. Not at the door — everyone agreed on that; nobody crossed thresholds down there, not even the thing that lived behind the door crossed its own threshold — but at the lock. The keyhole stood exposed in the salt-glass, a five-petaled flower cut clean through, and a cut flower is a cut gem is a fortune, and Sedge had put his pick to it the way a man puts a chisel to a safe, with the whole crew's hunger standing behind his shoulder.

Statement three, and the shortest: the strike. One blow. The pick came back flowering.

Harl's deposition ended there, and then went on longer than any statement she had ever taken from him, because he kept trying to explain the sound. There was no sound, he wrote first. Then: there was a sound like a struck glass, but struck from inside. Then, crossed out and rewritten twice: it rang back. You swing at a wall, the wall rings. This rang back at the swing. He finally wrote the sentence she kept: It didn't take the blow like stone. It took it like a ledger takes an entry.

The fourth statement was the one she took herself, at the shaft-head, from the two men who had carried Sedge up seven hundred steps at a dead run while the third man ran behind them holding a tool that had stopped being a tool. They were still shaking. She wrote their words down herself, in her own book, in her own hand, because some entries you do not delegate.

The frost had run down the haft from head to grip in the time it took to lift it off the door. Grey petals, fine as breath on glass, open and unfolded and pointing — every petal of them, the whole flowering length of the haft — compass-true, down. Not at the city. At the deep. The pick had gone down to take a lock and come back as a receipt.

And Sedge, who had swung it, had begun to count before they reached the second stair. Backward. From one hundred and nine.

Dar came to the quarry at first light, because the sanctuary terms cut both ways and Gavel crews were treated at the clinic without charge and without names, and what came up the shaft at dawn needed treatment the way a fire needs ignoring.

They had put Sedge in the infirmary shed, on a table, under blankets he had kicked most of the way off. Frost on his knuckles in five-petaled blooms that came and went; pulse two-handed and holding; eyes open, tracking, present — and counting. It came off him the way breathing goes on without permission: one hundred and nine, one hundred and eight, one hundred and seven — a man counting down to something with the calm of a clerk who has seen the schedule.

Dar took his wrist. The Lily went still in his chest — not the stillness of craving, not the stillness of rage; the stillness of a thing listening to its own name spoken in another room — and under his fingers Sedge's pulse paused at the touch and resumed one beat late, like a copyist reproducing a line one line down.

"One hundred and six," Sedge said, to the ceiling. "One hundred and five."

"Does the count move?" Dar asked him, and Sedge's eyes came down out of the middle distance and fastened on him with terrible sobriety, and the answer was the worst thing Dar had heard in a year of bad numbers.

"Only at thresholds," Sedge said. "Doors. Stairs. It's at one hundred and four."

The Cartographer's tally shard had read one hundred and nine for a year, with five fresh strokes cut under it by no hand Dar could name, and the count only ever moved at thresholds — and here was a man with a threshold in his mouth, spending it backward one number at a time. Something behind the door had been counting Dar's heartbeats since before the Blacktide; now it had handed the arithmetic to a pickman, the way you hand a bill to the nearest clerk. The deep did not defend itself. It billed.

"Can you treat him?" Gavel asked, from the shed's doorway, and it was the only time Dar ever heard her ask anything.

"I can keep him warm and hydrated and seen," Dar said. "The count isn't a sickness. It's a lien. Someone's lien on someone, and he's standing where the paper does its business." He looked at the tool rack by the door, where the pick leaned where they had put it, flowering still, the grey petals having neither wilted nor spread, holding their pointing like a compass holds north. "The debt isn't his. He's just the address it was delivered to."

There was a chalked line on the old shaft maps, the Cartographer's warning, copied by hand onto every chart that went below: DO NOT COUNT THE HEARTBEATS. And under it, the reason, in the same copperplate: Because it counts back. Dar had read it in a fog of nerve-scream a year ago and filed it as a warning about listening. It had never been about listening. It had been about accounting. Swing a pick at the deep and the deep does not defend itself; it bills you. One hundred and nine had been an invoice all along — the Cartographer's shard, the five fresh strokes, the count that only moved at thresholds — and now the invoice had a debtor's name on it, and the name was Sedge's, and the delivery address was a quarry infirmary in the rain.

Gavel came into the shed, and crossed the floor, and stood over the pick for a long moment — the longest Dar ever saw her do anything — while her crew waited at the door for the word that would send seven hundred men back down the stair into the one frontier in Ashkarad that had never once, in any ledger she kept, failed to pay.

The petals pointed down. Sedge counted: "One hundred and two. One hundred and one."

Gavel put her hand flat on the flowering haft, and took the measure of it — weight, cold, direction, odds — the way she had taken the measure of the paper Dar had not touched, and whatever she found in the arithmetic of a tool that had come back as a receipt, she entered it, and closed the column.

"Off the deep," she said, in the brass-tube voice she saved for orders that had to survive her. "All crews. The shaft is Gavel ground and Gavel closed, this bell, to everyone, forever, until the odds improve."

Harl, at the door, waited for the rest. There was no rest.

She looked at the pick a moment more — at the grey frost, at the pointing, at the patient arithmetic written all over it in a language older than her books — and then she took her hand back, and Suri, at the shed's edge, watched the oldest survivor in Ashkarad price something and decline to buy.

"One hundred," Sedge said, to the ceiling, comfortable, unhurried. "Ninety-nine."

The count went with him into the dark of the shed, and the pick stood in its corner, and the deep, far below the quarry and the clinic and every book in the city, kept its own books, and waited for the next entry with the patience of a creditor who has just been shown — at last, after a century — that the city above can read.
