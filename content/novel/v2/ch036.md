# Chapter 36 — The Barge from Brack

The tide-gate bells rang thirteen times, and Suri counted every one, because one of them did not belong.

The gates had kept a twelve-bell schedule since before her mother owed her first debt — six bells flood, six bells ebb, and the harbor-wards paid the ringer a wage that outlasted three reforms of the currency. Thirteen bells meant a fault in the mechanism. Thirteen bells meant a clerk somewhere had entered an extra line. Suri stood on the tide-gate catwalk in the grey before dawn with her hands in her coat pockets and her ledger-heart doing what it always did: finding the line that had been added without authorization.

Below her, the harbor of Ashkarad lay like a purse turned out and shaken. Two months of silence had emptied it of traffic. The tithe-barges that once worked the coast were burned, scuttled, or sold for hull-iron; the chandlers' rows stood shuttered; the fog-sea beyond the seawalls kept its own accounts and shared nothing. Only salvage moved now — Suri's crews, couriers turned wreckers with permission, pulling name-pages and copper out of the city's drowned margins the way she had once pulled them out of burning census halls.

The fog opened, and the barge came through.

It came against the current. That was the first entry Suri filed and the one she never afterwards amended. The ebb was running hard out through the gates — she could see it combing the pilings, white-threaded, going the way water goes — and the barge came up that sliding hill of sea at a walker's pace, unhurried, without oars, without sail, without one living hand on any of its lines. A tithe-barge, forty feet, black-hulled, of a pattern the Ashkarad yards had not built in her lifetime. Its wake opened behind it like a ledger ruled in reverse.

Every surface of it was furred with grey frost-petals.

Not black. Suri knew the black ones; the whole city knew them, the frost-blooms that had crawled the syndicate banners and pointed compass-true at the Choratorium, the ones that pointed now, on the clinic windows, at a surveyor's hired room over a chandler's shop. These petals were grey — ash-grey, ledger-grey, the grey of a thing that had finished its working life — and they grew in thick felted banks along the gunwales, the tiller, the booms, the faces of the dead.

Because the barge was crewed by the dead.

They stood at their posts the way poor men stand at posts they have stopped being able to leave: upright, patient, salt-stiffened. Six at the lines. One at the tiller, both hands lashed to it with tarred twine. They wore the grey coats and brass cuff-guards of counting-house watermen, a city's livery that Suri had never seen and knew instantly she was seeing — because the harvest-registry copy the Choirmaster had carried out of the burning listed Brack second from the top, two entries over Ashkarad's circled name, and everything on this deck was grey as that page's margins.

Someone had paid them. That was the detail she filed third and kept longest. Sewn into every cuff, flat and neat as a kept promise, was a coin — harbour silver, worn thin, a dead man's wage delivered in full where no dead hand could spend it. The auditors of Brack, she thought, settle their accounts. They just never say to whom.

"Boards or burn?" asked Dess, the youngest of her crews, a boy who had come to her out of the census-fire carrying three names that were not his.

"We board," Suri said. "Dead men don't file complaints, and I want the manifest."

Dess looked at the grey felt on the gunwales and asked the sensible question. "What if it's the same sickness? What if it comes off on us?"

"It's not a sickness," Suri said. "It's a signature. Signatures don't take. They only say who signed." She said it with more certainty than she carried, which was the oldest trick in a courier's book: you quote the rate before you know if you can pay it.

She counted as she crossed. Eleven planks of the gangway she had her crews lay. Fourteen feet to the main hatch. Six dead, one at the tiller, one — a seventh — folded at the base of the mast like a receipt that had been crumpled and kept anyway. The cold on deck was wrong; it came off the hull itself, off the felted petals, a cold with direction and intent, and when her glove brushed a petal it did not crumble. It turned. All of them turned — she watched it happen in the corner of her eye, slow as a clock hand — every grey flower on that deck swinging, degree by degree, until every petal pointed not back the way the barge had come but inland. At the city. At the gates. At her.

Noted, she thought. Filed.

The hold was empty. That was the manifest: nothing. No organ-jars, no reliquary straw, no tithe-crates, no ballast worth the name. Just cold, and the smell — old rope, tallow, and under it something sweetish and clinical, like a sickroom that had been paid off. The Lily's kind of smell, she thought, and did not say it, because Dar was a mile away behind the clinic door and she did not put his burdens into words where the wind could carry them.

She found the survivor in the stern, in the nest of dead men's coats behind the tiller-post — a woman folded in among them the way a clerk folds herself into a strong room at night, deliberately, by design. Alive. Grey lips, salt-scoured hands, the cuff-guards of a counting-house senior, ink-stains gone brown on her fingers. Her eyes opened when Suri's lamp touched her, and they were not a dying animal's eyes; they went over Suri's face, her coat, her crews, the deck — counting, Suri realized, the way she herself counted, out of the same unpayable habit — and then the woman shook her head, once, at whatever total she reached.

Her mouth was open. Her tongue was gone. Cut at the root, old work, healed wrong.

She was clutching a slate to her chest with both arms, the way other people hold children or money.

"Easy," Suri said, which was not a word she used, and then, because the woman's eyes kept counting her, she gave her what she gave counters: the truth in figures. "You're aboard a dead barge in a live city. Four of mine, one of you, seven of them. Nobody's collecting today."

The woman looked at her for three heartbeats. Then she turned the slate, slowly, and showed it to her.

Rows of tally-marks, five-bar gates, clerk-neat, running down the slate in columns like a debt schedule. The upper rows were steady. The lower rows wavered. The last line held a total — a large one, unfinished, the final stroke of it half-drawn, as if the hand that was drawing it had heard something on the stairs.

Suri did not need the tongue the auditors had taken. It was a count of the dead of Brack, she thought. She kept counting even then. That was the whole of her — they left her the arithmetic and took the voice off it.

The pulse-reader they found lashed under the tiller-post, still turning.

A Syndic Warden's machine, brass and bone, hand-cranked — but the crank was not a hand's. It was geared to a vane aft, to the wake, to the barge's own motion through the water, so that as long as the barge moved the machine turned, and as long as the machine turned it printed. They found the slips beneath it in a drift, hundreds of them, identical, furred at the edges with grey frost like everything else on the boat — page after page after page of the same census, printed and reprinted across however many miles of cold water lay between a silent city and this one.

Suri read the top slip by lamplight, and her face did not change, because Dess was watching, and because her face was the one part of her that had never once been entered into evidence.

BRACK POPULATION — 0.

Below it, in the machine's small stamped capitals, the second line, on every slip, the same:

FINAL COUNT FILED. RECEIPT DELIVERED.

Not a wreck, she thought, standing in the drift of identical pages with the grey petals turning slowly around her boots, all of them pointing inland. Not driftwood. Not an accident of current and grief. A receipt is addressed. Someone printed a city's closing entries, lashed the machine to a dead crew, and sent the account forward along the round — and the round, she knew, she had watched Dar work it out under Vahid's lens with Roya's charts in circles on the chalkboard — the round ends here. Ashkarad is the last name on the page. This was addressed to us.

She took one slip for the clinic and left the drift to keep its total. Then she had her crews carry the woman — slate and all, slate most of all — down the gangway, eleven planks back to the living side of the gate, and she did not look at the grey petals as they passed because she already knew, without looking, that they had all turned to follow.

The walk up from the harbor she spent on figures, because figures were the only furniture that fit the room in her head. Eight cities on Vey's page, eight flower-marks, one circle in red ink. Brack harvested two entries up the list — silent, reclaimed, its seed cut out — and its population machine printing zero in an endless identical column, like a heart that had learned only the one word. Her own page rode where it always rode, inside her coat, over the ruined graft: her name, her graft-number, resold three times, legible still. For two months she had kept that page as evidence, as ammunition, as a joke she told herself in the dark — they wanted this? this is what they paid for? — and now, walking behind a tongueless woman who had survived a city by the thickness of a ledger line, the joke wore through to the bone under it.

A city is a page, she thought. She had known it all her life; Ashkarad said so in its sleep, in every stamped chit and compounded tithe. She had just never believed anyone would come to collect the whole book at once — or that a count could be delivered like a death notice, machine-printed, addressed, and stamped FINAL.

And the woman had been left in the margin. That was the part Suri's ledger kept returning to as she climbed. You do not leave a survivor in a delivered receipt — a receipt closes. Unless the survivor is also an entry. Unless somebody, somewhere, still has a column with the woman's number in it, open and accruing, and sent the barge ahead not to announce a harvest but to deliver a reminder: we know one of you is missing from the count, and we know where the missing walk.

At the clinic door, Milla Ferun was waiting with a lamp, drawn out of her sleep by a courier's knock, and Milla took one look at the cuff-guards and the ink-stains and the slate and said, in the flat voice of a woman identifying her own kind across a ruined system: "Counting-house. Senior grade. They kept one alive to — " and stopped, because the end of that sentence was a thing none of them had a column for.

The woman looked up at Milla. Then at the clinic — the cots, the chalkboards, the walls where, along every sill and frame, the black frost-blooms of Ashkarad's own quiet garden grew in their slow way — and she began, very softly, with no tongue to do it with, to shake.

Suri got her inside, sat her down, wrapped her in sailcloth, and put the slate in her lap where her arms knew how to hold it. Then she laid the one salvaged slip on the table under the lamp, smooth, face-up, and stood looking at the machine-stamped truth of it with her thumb resting, without her permission, on the hidden edge of her own page inside her coat.

BRACK POPULATION — 0.

"Somebody deliver us the bill," she said to the empty room, "when they finish counting ours."
