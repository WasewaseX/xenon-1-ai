# Chapter 6 — The Cartographer

The Undergleam smelled of lamp oil and damp paper, a place where light was rationed like every other resource in Ashkarad. Every tallow stub had its ledger line; every shadow paid rent to somebody. Dar followed Suri through tunnels so narrow their shoulders brushed the slick, salt-crusted walls, and each brush of his coat left a pale smear behind, the tunnels keeping accounts of their own. They passed stalls where shadow merchants traded in rumors and contraband organs preserved in jars of cloudy fluid — a larynx, a heart-valve, a length of grafted nerve — each jar ticketed in cramped script, each price whispered rather than spoken, as if naming a thing too plainly might repossess it. Somewhere behind them, a buyer was haggling over a coil of intestine the way other men haggled over rope.

Suri's carbon-scaled arm scraped against a pipe, making a sound like stone on stone. "Three turns left," she said, counting under her breath. Dar had learned to trust her counting; in the Saltstairs, numbers were the only saints that answered. "Then look for the stall that smells of ink and old blood. The Cartographer doesn't like being late, and she hates being kept waiting more."

The air grew colder as they descended deeper, the salt-storm fog above pressing down like a physical weight, seeping through hairline cracks until the lowest passages drank it like cold soup. Dar felt the Lily in his chest stir, a subtle pulse that matched the rhythm of his own heartbeat but with an alien cadence — his beat, then its beat laid over it, the way a creditor's signature is written across a debtor's name. It had been quiet since they'd left Professor Anousheh's, patient as accruing interest, but now it seemed to wake, curious about the direction they were taking. "What exactly are we paying with?" Dar asked, keeping his voice low. The Lily didn't like loud noises; they made it withdraw, leaving him feeling hollow, a brief emptiness behind his sternum like a tooth drawn from the center of him.

"Information," Suri said. "And trust. The Cartographer deals in both. She'll want to know what the Lily dreams." She paused at a junction where three tunnels converged, each marked with different symbols scratched into the wall — a spiral, a cross, a stylized heart. Beneath the heart, someone had cut tallies, dozens of them, a five-barred gate of counted things that trailed off into the dark. "Left," she said decisively. "The other two are watched."

The stall was nestled in an alcove where the natural seepage of the Undergleam had formed a shallow pool of brackish water. The water moved without wind, small rings crossing and cancelling one another like entries in rival ledgers. A woman in a brass mask that covered her face from forehead to chin sat behind a table covered in parchment. Her gloved hands moved with practiced precision, drawing lines that shimmered and rearranged themselves as she worked, ink settling into place the way blood settles into a pattern a physician can read. The air around her hummed with a faint energy, like static electricity after a storm, and the fine hair on Dar's forearms rose to meet it.

"The Cartographer," Suri said by way of introduction. "We have what you want."

The woman looked up, her eyes visible through the slits in the mask — dark and sharp, like obsidian chips. "And you have what I need," she said, her voice a low contralto that seemed to echo slightly, as if the alcove itself were taking notes. "The dreamer. Bring him closer."

Dar approached cautiously, aware that he was being assessed — weighed, measured, entered into some private column. The Lily in his chest was restless now, a fluttering against his ribs that made him short of breath, a borrower's flutter, the kind that came just before a note was called in. "What exactly will you be doing?" he asked.

The Cartographer gestured to a chair of woven reeds that looked uncomfortable but sturdy. "I will be listening to what your guest has to say. Through you." She held up a small device made of brass and wire, intricate and somehow alive; the wires flexed between her fingers like small breathing things. "It's called a song-lobe. I'll wire it to your temples. You'll sleep, and I'll map what comes through."

Dar hesitated. Sleep meant vulnerability, and vulnerability in a city like Ashkarad was a debt waiting to be called in. He thought of the wardens with their hand-cranked pulse-readers, of ledgermen with their lists, of every enemy he had made simply by continuing to breathe. "What's the price?" he asked.

The Cartographer smiled, a thin line visible behind her mask. "Not money. Not yet. Information. What you know about the Church, about the Lily, about the man who sent you to Professor Anousheh. And what the Lily dreams — that's what I really want."

Dar exchanged a glance with Suri, who gave a slight nod. "It's the fastest way to what we need," she said. "The Cartographer's maps are worth more than any currency in the Undergleam."

So Dar sat in the reed chair, wincing as the metal contacts of the song-lobe touched his temples — cold as coins taken from a dead man's pocket. The Cartographer's gloved fingers were surprisingly gentle as she adjusted the wires, seating each one with a surgeon's care. "Close your eyes," she instructed. "Let go. The Lily will speak through you."

The darkness behind Dar's eyelids wasn't empty; it was filled with the Lily's presence, a pulsing, living darkness that reached out to touch his mind. He felt himself sinking, falling through layers of consciousness — skin, fear, memory, debt — each layer thinner than the last, the Lily's dreams rising to meet him like water rising in a hold.

He was standing in a garden made of salt, under a sky the color of dried blood. The ground was crystalline, and beneath his feet he could feel the slow, rhythmic pulse of something vast and sleeping, a heartbeat so deep it took a full minute to finish a single beat. Rows of people lay buried in the earth like bulbs, their heads protruding, eyes closed in peaceful slumber, their faces dusted with fine salt that no wind ever disturbed. A tower rose in the distance, pulsing with light — each window a heartbeat, each floor a different rhythm, the whole structure shuddering gently out of time with itself, like a ledger whose pages had been bound out of order. And from the tower, a voice hummed, a lullaby in a language Dar didn't know but somehow understood, the way you understand a debt: not the words, only the weight.

The Cartographer's voice, distant and muffled, intruded on the dream. "Draw it," she said to someone — perhaps herself, perhaps an unseen assistant. "The salt gardens, the sleeping rows, the tower of hearts. And the voice — can you map the frequency?"

Dar tried to speak, to ask where he was, who was humming, but the dream held him fast, the way deep water holds a body that has stopped struggling. The Lily was in complete control here, showing him what it wanted him to see — or what it needed him to see; with the Lily, he was learning, the two were rarely the same. A figure approached through the garden — not walking, but gliding, her robes trailing behind her like smoke. Her face was obscured, but her presence was familiar, like a half-remembered melody. She stopped before one of the sleeping figures and placed a hand on their forehead, humming a single, perfect note that made the salt crystals tremble — and somewhere far away, in a room that smelled of ink and old blood, a quill began to scratch faster.

"Who is she?" Dar managed to whisper, though his voice was lost in the dream.

The figure turned, and for a moment, Dar thought he recognized the eyes — Anra Vey's eyes, calm and ancient, patient as a collector at year's end. But then the dream shifted, and the garden dissolved into darkness.

Dar woke with a start, gasping for air. The reed chair felt rough against his skin, and the metal contacts were gone. The Cartographer stood before him, her gloved hands stained with ink, several maps spread across the table. Her mask was still in place, but her shoulders were slumped, as if the weight of what she'd seen had settled there — an entry written in her own body's ledger.

"You saw her," Dar said, his throat dry. "The woman in the garden."

The Cartographer nodded, picking up a quill and dipping it in ink. "She's the one who's been hunting my signal too. The one who wants the Lily to bloom." She began to draw, her movements precise but hurried. "The Chorus facilities — their clinics, reliquaries, tithe-routes. Everything I have."

Dar watched as the maps took shape, each line and symbol representing a piece of the Church's hidden network. The Cartographer worked quickly, her breath visible in the cold air of the Undergleam. "There," she said, pointing to a series of interconnected lines. "The main Choratorium here, the nursery there, and the reliquaries where they keep the harvested voices."

She pushed the maps across the table toward Dar. "Take them. But there's one more thing." She picked up a fresh sheet of parchment and began to draw again, her hand shaking slightly this time. A single shaft, deep beneath the Marrowworks, older than the Church above it. At its base, a single word: THRONE. And around the shaft, lines of old ledger-script: DO NOT COUNT THE HEARTBEATS.

Dar leaned closer, squinting at the script. "What does that mean?"

The Cartographer looked up, her eyes shadowed behind the brass mask. "Because it counts back," she said, her voice barely above a whisper. "The heartbeats in that place aren't just alive — they're aware. And they remember."

Dar and Suri moved quickly through the Undergleam, the maps rolled tight in Dar's coat pocket, tight enough that he could feel their edges against his ribs like a second set of accounts. The Lily in his chest was quiet now, but he could feel it processing what it had shown the Cartographer — the dream of the salt gardens, the sleeping rows, the humming woman — filing something away in whatever ledger it kept inside him. "What do you think she meant by 'it counts back'?" he asked Suri as they navigated a tunnel that sloped upward.

"The Church has something old beneath the Marrowworks," Suri said, counting her steps. "Something that remembers being counted. Something that might be hunting us back."

They emerged from a maintenance shaft into the Saltstairs, the tenement warrens rising around them like teeth. The salt storm had passed, leaving behind a thick fog that blurred the edges of buildings and swallowed distant sounds whole. A warden's whistle cut through the fog, sharp and demanding. Suri grabbed Dar's arm, pulling him into an alley just as a grey-coated figure passed by, hand-cranked pulse-reader clicking rhythmically, the little brass gears gnawing through the quiet like an insect eating through paper.

"They're checking for vital-debt defaulters," Suri whispered, pressing herself against the damp wall. "We need to move before they get to this sector."

Dar nodded, the weight of the maps heavy in his coat. The Lily pulsed once, a reminder of the debt it had placed on him — blood for growth, darkness for strength, and now this: a shared dream with a woman who seemed to be everywhere and nowhere at once. They had a lead, a path to the Church's secrets, but it was tangled with something older and far more dangerous, something the Church itself had seen fit to warn against in a dead hand's script. As they slipped back into the fog, Dar wondered if they were running toward answers or running straight into the thing that counted back.
