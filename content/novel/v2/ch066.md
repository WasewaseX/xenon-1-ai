# Chapter 66 — Paid in Full

The Gavel served its claim at the sixth bell, and Suri counted the service in, because it was hers to count: two ledgermen on the clinic step with the rain still coming off their coats, one courier-detail waiting in the lane with a covered barrow — for her, she understood; the barrow was the tell — and between the ledgermen, held out flat on both palms like a relic, a Chorus debt-book with its spine broken open to the page they had bought the right to show her.

KAVEH, SURI. Graft-number, inked in the old maroon. RESOLD — THREE TIMES. Interest compounded every salt-moon since she was nineteen. And in the margin, in a hand she recognized from the ration lines, the purchase note: acquired at derelict auction, 110 coin. The Gavel had bought her out of the Church's burning for pennies, the way you buy a drowned house, and had waited the polite interval of an audit before coming to collect the property.

"The debt is Gavel property now," the senior ledgerman said. He had the flat courtesy of a man reading terms he had not written. "The debtor is therefore an asset. Mother Gavel elects to take the asset in person. The boss remembers your hands. Counting hands are rare."

"Present yourself at the pump-works by the eighth bell," said the younger one, "or be collected."

Suri did not answer at once. She read the page the way she read everything, top to bottom, twice, and her eyes stopped one line above the total. "Your interest table is wrong," she said. "You've billed me nine thousand, nine hundred and forty. The resales compound from the third resale date, not the first, and the third resale paid down principal — it's on your own page, the entry in faded ink. The true retirement figure is ten thousand, six hundred and eight."

The senior ledgerman looked at the page, and then at her, with the first honest expression she had seen on him. "You're correcting your own bill upward."

"I don't retire on a rounding error," Suri said. "A debt that dies wrong doesn't die. It haunts. Re-price it."

They re-priced it. It took them twenty minutes, and she stood over them while they did it, and the covered barrow waited in the lane like punctuation.

· · ·

Dar heard the terms in the ward with his sleeves still rolled from a delivery, and Suri watched him do the arithmetic she had already finished. There was exactly one thing in Ashkarad the system respected, and it was payment, and the clinic had no coin. What the clinic had was a slip of thermal-grey paper, five years old, flagged RHYTHM UNRESOLVED, the only surviving print of the two-rhythm pulse that had started everything — and an Index bounty on that slip rising since the fires.

"You could refuse," Suri said. "Sanctuary covers me. The terms say no names leave the building."

"Sanctuary covers the sick," Dar said. "They'll argue you're not a patient, you're an asset, and they'll be right by their own books. Arguments lose to ledgers, Suri. We pay."

"And the price of paying is the printout."

"The price of paying is a copy of the printout," Dar said, "at a price that retires your page in full. Nothing more. I'm not selling them the anomaly. I'm selling them the receipt for it — and I'm pricing it at exactly what you cost, so they know the trade was never about them."

Roya made the copy that night. She worked at the back table with the lamp hooded, and she did not trace the slip so much as persuade a second sheet of scrounged thermal stock to remember what the first one remembered — pressure and heat, her old craft, the lines rising like veins under skin. When she finished there were two slips on the table and she covered the older one with her scarred hand before anyone could touch it. "This one stays," she said, through the brass mask. "Copies are for selling. Originals are for keeping."

"Mine's already sold, then," Milla said from the doorway, in a voice with no weather in it at all, and went back to the front desk, and counted something very hard for a long time.

· · ·

Dar went to the annex alone. Suri waited at the clinic and did the only work left to her, which was bookkeeping: she entered the claim in her ledger under GAVEL — CLAIM SERVED, the corrected figure beneath it, and beside that column she opened another, and ruled it, and left it blank, because she did not yet know what went in it.

He was back by the fifth bell of evening with rain on his shoulders and three papers, and he laid them on the ward table one at a time, like a man dealing out a verdict.

The first was the Gavel's surrender: the debt-book page with her name on it, stamped across the maroon in the Gavel's blunt grease-pencil — SETTLED — PAID IN FULL — the chain of resales struck through in one clean stroke. The second was the Index's receipt: ten thousand, six hundred and eight in coin, received against delivery of one print, anomaly class, origin Saltstairs, the figure written in a hand that shook slightly, as if the clerk who wrote it had understood he was buying a confession. The third was blank paper, and Dar turned it over and said nothing, and Suri understood the blank was the point: the Index had offered more, and he had priced the city's only evidence at cost.

"They verified it against their rebuilt machine records for an hour," Dar said. "Then their auditor paid, and thanked me, and said a thing I'm supposed to carry back. He said: the seal counts every rhythm eventually — now it has two of yours on file." He looked at the table. "The city's secret is in their annex. There's no column that undoes that. I checked."

"You paid my debt with the one proof that we exist," Suri said. It was not an accusation. It was the entry, being read aloud for errors.

"I paid your debt with the thing they were already hunting," Dar said. "The hunt was priced in the moment they posted the bounty. I just chose who got paid."

· · ·

Milla was waiting when the ward emptied, and she did not wait politely. She came to the table with her cold fingers flat on the wood and her stamp-callus facing him like a credential, and said, "You sold the Ferun printout."

"I sold a copy of it."

"You sold my page." Her voice stayed level, which was worse. "You sold it to the men who priced me by name before I knew I was for sale. I gave that slip to this clinic. I did not give it to a market. Sanctuary means no names leave the building, Mehran, and that slip is the loudest name in this city."

"It bought ten thousand, six hundred and eight," Dar said, "and every coin of it is stamped on the Gavel's surrender, and Suri Kaveh is owned by no one as of tonight."

"I know what it bought." Milla's hands did not move. "I've known since I heard the barrow in the lane. I'm not here about the price. I'm here about the estate." She unpicked the seam of her coat lining with a clerk's small knife, three fast strokes, and drew out a fold of oilcloth, and opened it on the table.

The slip inside was thermal-grey and five years old and perfect, and it was not the slip Dar had carried since the night she defected. It was the same rhythm — five spikes, a dip, five spikes — but sharper, first-generation, and along the bottom edge ran the machine's fault-strike and, in faded official ink, a destruction-order number. The Chorus original had gone into the furnace; this was the first pull, the true print, the one the copyist had kept.

"I told you, in this ward, that the copy I gave you was the only print left in the world," Milla said. "I'm a clerk. I run originals twice and file the difference — the second pull went into your coat, and I let you believe it was the whole estate, because the bounty was on my name and I had been audited before. Originals stay with their clerk." She folded the oilcloth back over the print, seam by seam. "So the Index has the pattern — a faithful copy of the second pull, made by the best forger in the city. They'll count two rhythms, they'll verify it against their rebuilt machines, and they'll build their readers around it, and that cost is real, and you meant it to be. But the first pull has the machine's own fault-strike and the destruction order in the margin — the only marks in the world that prove where this rhythm was read, and when, and on whose order it was to die. When the fleet comes, and someone has to prove what was done to this city — the proof is mine, and it stays mine, and it stays sewn where it has slept since before the Blacktide." She sewed the lining shut with three strokes of the same small knife turned needle-wise. "You should have asked me, Mehran. I'd have said yes. That's the part I'll hold against you — not the sale. The not asking."

Dar took that the way he took triage, without flinching and without arguing. "Noted," he said. "Filed."

"You're damn right it's filed," Milla said, and went back to her desk, and the clinic was quieter by one old lie.

· · ·

They burned her page that night, in the clinic stove, because Suri said a thing that was paid for should be seen to burn, and Dar said a thing paid for should be buried, and Suri said graves are just ledgers the ground keeps, and won.

She took it out of her own ledger's spine, where it had ridden since the night of the fires: the salvage page, her name and graft-number, RESOLD — THREE TIMES, soot-stained at one corner where she had pulled it out of ash that was still breathing. She laid it on the ward table, and Milla brought the ink without being asked, and a pen with a clerk's nib, and stood by while Suri wrote across the maroon entries in her flat, counted hand:

PAID IN FULL.

She had written those words once before, on a page about Dar, with a parenthetical after them, and her hand hesitated out of pure habit above the space where the parenthetical went. Then she wrote the words only, and capped the ink, and carried the page to the stove.

It did not burn cleanly.

The Chorus inks, Vahid had told them once, in the register he used for the things that shamed him — their census inks carried a living trace, a tissue-ink, so the books could answer to their masters when called. The flame took the paper fine and ordinary, and then the maroon entries caught, and the page began to scream. Faintly. The way a kettle screams from a street away; the way a wet finger drawn on glass would scream, if glass could grieve. The graft-number howled in a register below hearing that everyone in the ward heard anyway. Suri held the stove door and did not step back, because the debt had been hers and the dying of it was going to be hers too, and her face did nothing at all.

Ilay came down the stair before anyone called her — drawn, the way she was drawn to wrongness, with her copper hair tied back and her eyes the color of river stones. She listened to the page for the length of three heartbeats, hers or Suri's nobody counted. Then she sat down on the wood-box, folded her hands, and hummed.

It was not the lullaby. It was the small, tuneless music she made when the deep was quiet and nothing in the walls was counting — the sound of a child alone in a room where nothing needed her. She hummed it at the stove, evenly, the way you hold a note you intend to keep.

The screaming settled. The entries blackened quietly, one line at a time, like a ledger being closed by a careful hand, and the maroon ink let go of its living trace and became only ink, and only ash. The stamped words went last, the way good ink does, holding their line until the fire made them true.

Suri closed the stove. She went back to the ward table and opened her ledger to the blank column she had ruled that morning, and wrote across its head, small: KAVEH, SURI — RETIRED. The column beneath it stayed empty, and she looked at the emptiness the way other people look at the sea.

The column read zero. She kept counting anyway.
