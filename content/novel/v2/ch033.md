# Chapter 33 — Eight Cities, One Route

The question changed shape in the three days after the descent, the way a debt changes shape when the debtor finally reads the contract to the end. It had been *what is below.* It was now *who put her there*, and the answer had been lying folded in a sterile wrap on the attic desk for the better part of a season, waiting for him to stop flinching from it.

They made the cold cellar into an audit room. It was already the only chamber in the city where Vahid felt at home — the Vein-ladder chalked down one wall, his prohibition chalked under it, the girl's spiral map pinned beside both with its innermost ring lettered *soon* — and Roya arrived with her arms full of salvage and turned it into a cartographer's parish. Charts everywhere, unrolled, weighted with sterilizer jars and river stones: coastal charts of the fog-sea lifted from the census fires and dried flat; two Warden tide-tables with the Index's burn-scorch across their corners; and a cloth-bound route-book of the Caravan League — overland trade guild, surveyors and freight-clerks, the people who moved goods between the cities that still had goods — copied out in her own cramped, exact hand years ago and priced, then, at more than a harvest-floor saw in a month.

Dar laid Vey's registry page flat under the lamp.

It was vellum, and it was old, and it had been handled the way sacred things are handled — along the edges, by gloved fingers, never flat — until the night a Custodian had come apart into lace and left it behind for a surgeon to steal. He had carried it from her sanctum folded against his ribs, which even then had struck some unburned part of him as the correct filing: documents of this kind traveled next to the collateral. Eight city names, ranked in a column in a cramped and patient script: *Lowmark. The Vigil. Gullet. Sarn. Weep. Ossuar. Brack. Hallowfen.* And a ninth line, beneath them all, circled in red ink gone the brown of old blood: *Ashkarad* — with its entry not stamped BLOOMED but marked in the ledger-script he had last seen scratched into the salt of a shaft landing: *VESSEL: FLED—RECLAIM.* Below the column, one word: *RECLAIM.* Below the word, a title, unsigned: *GARDENER.*

Beside each name, repeated like a surveyor's mark on a boundary stone, grew the flower. Five petals. Blooming from a crack.

"Read me the margins again," Vahid said. He had his lens out — a brass loupe older than the Church's patent on anything, mended the same way his spectacles were mended, with wire and refusal — and he bent over the page one-handed, turning it a degree at a time, the stump of his left arm braced against the table's edge for ballast. "Not the words. The *material*. I have been reading what the page says for a season. Tonight I want to read what the page is."

"Vellum," Dar said. "Calfskin, by the grain — no, wait." He adjusted the lamp and looked again, and the grain looked back, and the grain was wrong. Calfskin had pores. This had *cells.* Small, closed, regular, like the surface of a leaf seen under a clinic glass. "Not skin. Leaf. It's pressed leaf."

"Leaf," Vahid agreed, without surprise, in the tone of a man crossing an item off a list he wrote years ago. "And the marks?"

Dar moved the lens to the flower beside *Lowmark.* "Ink. Black ink—"

The lens stopped his mouth.

The black was not ink. Under the loupe, at eleven powers, the sigil resolved into granularity — into grain — into thousands of tiny bodies, ovate, dark, matte, with the dull authority of seed-coat, pressed into the vellum's grain the way pollen presses into the varnish of a thing left long in a flowering room. Not one mark of pigment anywhere. The flower was a *deposit.* The names might be ink; the sigil was not. The sigil had been grown there, or shaken there, or dusted there, the way a tailor dusts charcoal through a pricked pattern to carry a design onto cloth.

"Roya," Dar said. "Your loupe. Now. Look at the *Lowmark* flower and tell me what you see."

Roya bent. The lamp slid off her brass mask, forehead to chin, so that her reflection hung in the room doubled and featureless. For a long moment she said nothing, and the mask said nothing, and the silence had the specific pressure of a professional measuring the worth of her own trade.

"Pattern-pricking," she said at last, metallic and flat. "But for a route, not a hem. Look —" her white-scarred hand hovered above the column without touching it, moving name to name, "— every flower sits on a puncture. Needle-marks. Fine ones, through the sheet, at the flower's heart. And between the flowers — follow the grain — there are more, a line of them, faint as pinholes in a spy-hole. The page has been *pricked*, Dar. Someone stitched this list with a needle, city to city, like a pattern come apart from the cloth."

"And the black?"

"Is what came through the needle," Roya said. "A pattern-maker pricks and pounces. Whoever made this pricked and *pollinated.*"

Ilay's voice came from the corner, unasked, unhurried, the way her voice always arrived — laid down flat, like something already true. "It's warm," she said. She had come forward without anyone noticing, the way she did, and she was looking at the page with her river-stone eyes. "The paper. It's warm, like the door under the water place." She considered. "The door was warm too. Where the lady waits."

She went back to her charcoal. Nobody in the cellar found it necessary to ask which lady.

The cellar was quiet except for the lamp's small economy of oil and, from the ward overhead, the muffled arithmetic of Milla's medicine counts.

"Say it plainly," Vahid said.

"It's an itinerary," Roya said. "A route. Needle-run, start to finish, through all nine." Her hand stopped above the red circle. "The names are stops."

Suri, from the doorway, with her sleeves rolled and her ruined carbon scales catching the lamp: "Then run the route."

So they ran the route.

It took the better part of two hours, because the page did not give up its order to the eye — the needle-line ran *Lowmark* to *the Vigil*, the Vigil to *Gullet*, Gullet to Sarn, Sarn to Weep, Weep to Ossuar, Ossuar to *Brack*, Brack to Hallowfen, and from Hallowfen the pin-holes ran on, off the sheet, to the ninth line, to the circled name — and the eye kept wanting the column order to be the truth, and the column order was a lie, the way a bill's subtotals are a lie until you sum them. Roya pinned her charts flat, plotted the eight names by their salt-lane bearings and the League's route-book, and the needle-line came alive on the maps as a curve that wandered inboard and out, hill country to harbor, monastery coast to reef shelf, wandering as a creditor wanders when he is in no hurry and knows the debtor is not going anywhere.

"Brack first," Roya said finally, and set her fingertip on the far western shore of the fog-sea. "Then Hallowfen. Then Ossuar, upland. Weep. Sarn. Then Gullet — the reef shelf, here, on the carcass grounds. Then the Vigil, on the monastery coast. Then Lowmark, last of the eight — the drowned port. And then—" her finger came up the charts, across the fog-sea's narrow middle, to the salt-port drawn in her own younger hand, ringed with its storm-walls, and stopped. "Then here. Ashkarad. Last stop on the round."

"Two entries over," Suri said. She had come to the table's edge without ceremony and was looking at the column the way she looked at any column, for totals. "Brack. Two entries over from ours, on the sheet." Her finger rested near it, not touching. "And the League's route-book has no traffic on the Brack lane since before the storm. Freight don't skip a lane for weather. Freight skips a lane when there's no port left to bill."

"Eight cities," Vahid said slowly, "and the flower bloomed in eight of them. BLOOMED—TRUE THRONE, the stamps said. And in the ninth, the vessel fled." He straightened, and his voice went to the lecture-register that was his way of not shouting. "It is not a map of an empire, boy. Empires list what they hold. This lists what is *owed.* It is a collection round. Sown a century ago by one hand — nine seeds, nine debts — and now the hand has come to call them in, city by city, in an order a clerk chose, and our city is the last entry on the sheet because our entry is the one that says FLED."

"RECLAIM," Suri said. Flat. Reading it off the page without looking at it, because she had long since memorized it.

"Reclaim," Vahid agreed. "Not punish. Not conquer. *Reclaim* — the word a ledger uses when the collateral has wandered off the books and the account-holder wants his security back." He looked at Dar, and there was no gin in his face at all. "You are carrying the collateral, boy. You have been carrying it since a dying thief crawled into a canal with a cracked reliquary. And somewhere out past the seawalls there is an account-holder with a needle, working down the column toward us."

Nobody said the rest of it. The rest of it was Ilay, nine years old, registry-grade PERFECT, the garden's daughter, drawing silence-swirls with a charcoal stub — sibling stock, active — and the rest of it was that the round had already reached the cities two entries over from Ashkarad's line, and the sea lanes between here and there had been empty of League traffic for a month, and empty lanes were either an omen or an opening, depending on which side of the ledger you kept your stool. Vahid began packing his lens into its chamois with exaggerated care, which was what the Professor did instead of weeping or drinking, in the hours when he managed both withholdings at once.

It was Milla, of all people, who ended the argument by accident. She came down with the evening medicine slips, saw the lamp burning on the page, and put out her hand to move the lamp-shade from her eyes — and the light fell full on the sigil beside *Hallowfen*, the last flower on the sheet, warm as a hand for the first time since it had been pressed.

The pollen moved.

Not scattered. Not disturbed. The grain in the flower's black petals *turned* — each tiny ovate body swinging on its own invisible axis, slowly, with the gravity of compass needles finding north or sunflowers finding the sun — until all of them, every seed-body in every flower on the page, lay pointed the same way. Toward the wall. Through the wall. West and down, where the charts hung and beyond the charts, toward the tide-line, the storm-walls, the seawalls, the sea.

Roya looked at the page for a long time. The mask reflected the lamp back as one blank coin of light. Somewhere above them a Warden bell tolled the wrong hour — the bells had been unreliable since the Choir died, and the city had stopped trusting them, which meant the city now told time by hunger and lamplight, which meant, depending on your ledger, that the city was either freer or later than it had ever been.

"Not ink," she said. "Not a signature. *Bait.*" A pause, the length of one flat heartbeat. "No. Not bait. A receipt that knows where its debtor lives."

The pollen held its new bearing, patient as everything else that had ever been planted in this city, and pointed at the sea.
