# Chapter 90 — Both, or Neither

The word was still in the walls at dawn.

HUNGRY. It had come up through every crack in Ashkarad at once — through the singing fracture in the salt-glass door, up the cistern-necks and drain-channels, along the pump-works' iron and the tomb-thin spaces between tenement stones — and when the light came, it did not leave. Dar woke on a pallet in the shaft house with it filed behind his teeth, the way the deep's resonance always filed itself there, and lay listening to a whole city breathe around one syllable.

The Standing Count thinned as the sun rose. It did not end. All night it had held — every soul above the vault giving its heartbeats freely into one rhythm, Gavel's brass tube calling the count, Suri's couriers keeping the lines, the stock children singing Ilay's transcription in voices that no hymn could bait, the freed donors under the streets, the Sister's pedal-tone below it all like a hand under a falling body — and Dar had stood at the center of it holding the Ninth Vein's crack open the way a surgeon holds a retractor, not one hair wider than the wound requires. Now the great mass of it went home to water and bread, and what remained was the discipline: nothing below gets an answer. Answers are coin. The city had stopped spending.

The Cow's scream had burned itself down overnight to a whine, and by dawn to a whine you could only hear if you listened for it, which no one did. The herald-chorus stood in the bloom-fog beyond the seawalls with nobody left to conduct them. Their conductor the bone-masked captain they had found at first light, silent, gloves empty, laid out straight as a filed receipt. Ashkarad counted its dead from the night and got a number small enough to distrust, and posted it on the clinic door anyway, because a count you distrust is still worth keeping.

Then, in the hush the count left behind, the bargaining began.

It came up through the cracks the way the word had. But this time it was not one word. It was a voice — warm, patient, intimate, the voice Dar had spent a year calling his mother's — and it did not shout. It negotiated.

Feed me, it said, from the drains, from the door's fracture, from the salt in the walls. Feed me and I open. You want the door. I want the feeding. Both accounts settle. We are both owed.

The mimicry was very good, and that was the horror of it. It had the exact grain of the voice out of the memory bleeds — the voice the First Singer had used on him at the bottom of the shaft, the voice his original heart had begged in when it sat in its glass box singing the lullaby in a mother's voice that was never his mother's. It was wrong the way the three-heartbeat face had been wrong: not in the timbre but in the arithmetic behind the timbre, a patience with nothing personal left in it. Every ninth beat the voice skipped, a needle jumping its groove, and began the sentence again, and each time it began again it was a little hungrier, the interest compounding in real time. It never sang the ending of the song. It hummed to the edge of the last bars and stopped there, as if the recording itself had been cut, and then it asked again, in the daughter's voice, to be fed and opened like a door is opened, like an account is opened, like a mouth.

Nobody answered it. The rule held at every threshold in the city. Suri had ruled it the first night, flat as a stamped page, and the city kept her rule the way it kept water: Nothing down gets a reply. A reply is credit, and we are not lending.

The Gardener came at the second hour, on foot, up the dry seabed.

The sea that had come back uphill to deliver the wain withdrew again before dawn — out and out past sight, as if the horizon had decided not to watch — and the flats lay bare and grey under the bloom-fog, ribbed with the old tide-marks, littered with the fleet's abandoned survey-cairns. She walked out of that fog the way an audit walks into a ledger room: unhurried, unavoidable, without apology. Alone. Bone-white hands bare at her sides, no gloves, nothing carried, nothing hidden. At each wrist a ring of old burn-scars, pale and regular as tally-marks. The herald ranks stood down to let her pass, and the clerks stood up out of their tents to watch her go, and she did not look at one of them, because you do not look at files.

At the tide-gates the last working gate-reader turned and printed, for the first time in its service, the only rhythm it had ever truly been listening for: the deep's own beat, slow, vast, one heartbeat to a mile. She passed under the arch without breaking stride.

Nobody ordered the way to the vault-mouth opened. Nobody dared order it closed. The crowd moved the way water moves around a thing it cannot afford to touch, and Dar stood on the shaft-house steps and watched her come up through her own fog, and knew her, and wished he did not.

He had bought three heartbeats of that face once, at a decade's price, in a counting-annex at Lowmark — a mother's face that was not quite his mother's, wrong in the mouth, where a smile is filed. She had knelt at an empty grave beyond the Door and looked up through a crack in the Ninth Vein and mouthed an endearment at him across the difference of worlds. Here she was at the mouth of the shaft with her hands bare, and the wrongness was still there, and the likeness was still unbearable, and he understood at last that both of those facts were about the same woman.

"You held your door all night with your hearts," she said from four steps below him, in the voice of a woman closing a very old account. "I came to hear why. I heard." A pause, measured and paid. "I have come to ask, not to command. I have not asked anything of anyone in a hundred years. It is harder than commanding. The muscles go."

"Ask," Dar said.

"Open the Door with me." Her voice did not rise; it descended, into some plain register beneath all her registers, the way a tide-priest drops to the honest numbers when the service is over. "Not for me. With me. And let me bury my daughter in the garden she was stolen from. The grave is cut. It has waited a century, tended, empty. I want to carry what is left of her through, and lay it down, and be—" she considered the word the way she might consider a column that would not balance, "—finished. That is the whole of the request. I will sign it in your presence. I will sign it left-handed, so you know it is mine."

Before anyone could answer, the shaft answered for them.

Feed me, said the voice below, in the daughter's borrowed warmth. Open me. Feed me and I open. Both accounts settle—

and for one appalling moment the vault-mouth held both of them at once: the woman above asking, and the thing beneath asking in a stolen copy of the same family's voice. Same grain. Same patience. One wearing hunger and one wearing grief, and hearing them together, it was not possible to say which was the imitation.

"Enough," the Gardener said — to the shaft, not to Dar — in exactly the tone a keeper uses on a dog that begs at table. The cracks went quiet. Not silent. Quiet. A thing put on notice, with its hunger still accruing behind its eyes.

They convened at the shaft mouth because there was nowhere in the city large enough to hold a convergence like this one, and no time to build one. The ruin's open floor, the shaft dark at its center, the fog thinning against the broken arches. Suri came with the census tallies under her arm and her splinted forearm held like a ledger she was done with. Mother Gavel stood with her crews and her scars and her flat eyes, saying nothing, auditing everything. The Choirmaster, with his fire-axe grounded like a parade weapon. Wen, three paces behind his Warden, a century old and half-bloomed, watching the ninth's carrier the way a stopped clock watches noon. Isra with her slate, the black petal at her collarbone spread half open. Roya with chalk she had no map for yet. And Ilay came down from among the singing children, drawn by whatever the deep said to her hearing, and stood with her palms against the stone of the shaft like a girl at a window.

The Gardener stated her account in the language the city respected, item by item, while the bloom-fog leaned against the doors: the Door opened from inside, by a living Throne — by the one who already held the Ninth's crack, who had held it through the whole of the Standing Count without blooming; the daughter's remains carried through and laid in the grave beyond; the Root fed on schedule, by her hand, while it slept; the fleet stood down; the harvest voided; the terms withdrawn. "That is the whole of the account," she said. "I have waited a hundred years to file it, and I file it now, here, with every witness the city can produce."

Below, patiently, in the daughter's voice: Feed me. Open me. We are both owed. Every ninth beat, the skip. Every skip, the hunger, restated.

And then the deep's harmony stopped.

Not the rhythm — the harmony. The pedal-tone under the city, the one line that had not ceased since the count began, ceased, and every head at the shaft mouth turned to the girl with her hands flat on the stone.

Ilay listened the way she listened, with her whole body, her copper head bent, her fingers spread on the rock. The stone said something. The music said it first, and then the girl said it after, in the flat, literal voice she used for counts and for truth, word for word, like a clerk reading a finding into the record:

"Her daughter is not in the grave."

The Gardener went very still. The fog stopped moving at the doors.

"Her daughter," Ilay said, "is the seed in his chest."

It came into the ruin like a verdict, because it was one: a century of testimony, closed out and read. The Gardener did not deny it. Denial was a filing error, and she had never once filed an error. She turned — slowly, the burn-scarred wrists first, as if they led her — and looked at Dar, and then at his chest, at the place where a stolen reliquary had cracked open a life ago and a ninth seed had chosen him, and Dar watched her recalculate a hundred years of arithmetic in the space of three heartbeats. He counted them. Counting was the only prayer he trusted, and he needed one.

Below, the deep went quiet. Not the held-breath quiet of a thing on notice. A listening quiet. Even the hunger, for one beat, waiting on the finding.

And in that quiet Dar felt the crack of the Ninth Vein shift against its own edges — a hair wider, the way a door shifts in a frame when the house settles — and felt what he held: the census of Ashkarad, every freely given heartbeat of the Standing Count, held in him the way a chord holds a note. The Lily rose through that chord like a pedal through a hymn. The hum climbed his sternum, into his throat, into his jaw, and his jaw ached with the shape of words he was not making, and he could have bitten down on them, and he knew with absolute certainty that she was not taking the mouth. She was asking for it. The way her mother had asked.

He let his own mouth open, and the voice in his chest came up through it — his voice and hers at once, doubled, aloud, through his mouth and her own, before the fleet and the city and her mother and the thing that ate:

"Both. Or neither."
