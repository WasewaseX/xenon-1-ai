# Chapter 73 — For My Grandmother

Ilay heard the ending before she had the beginning, because the deep was like that: it gave you the last page first and made you earn the earlier ones.

She had the ending for the length of one bar, on the third night after the inventory, at the deepest listen she had ever taken. Roya held the listening-post niche at the shaft head with the slate on her knees and the lamp turned low, and Ilay lay flat on the stone with both palms spread and both ears down, the way divers lie on sea-ice, and the true song came up through the world's floor and through her bones and she took it down as fast as her hand could move. Numbers for how many. Circles for how loud. Lines for how long. The notation was hers; no one had taught it to her; it had come up out of the same place the music came from, and Roya, who had once mapped the dreams of a god with a wired song-lobe and now mapped a nine-year-old's hearing with chalk, had pronounced it the best survey instrument left in Ashkarad and did not mean it kindly to the city.

The song was not the lullaby. She had learned that early, and it had cost the grown-ups a bad hour to learn it too. The lullaby was the thing the Church had spent forty years feeding down through the Choratorium's floor, the maintenance-song, the locking-collar's oil. The true song was older and did not go down; it came up. It was the song the garden sang when there was still a garden, and it had verses the way a ledger has pages, and the deep below the cracked door held most of it and hummed it in its sleep, and the crack in the door sang it in every language that had ever died up here, and the Sister below the door held the mother's line — the under-line, the harmony that was not a child's thread — and gave it to Ilay at the room's edge, softly, one visit at a time, like an heirloom being handed down a stairwell that had never once been swept.

The crack was her second teacher. Every language sang the same four opening notes and then went its own way, and each way of going was a lesson: this tongue held a verse against the beat, like a diver holding a stone for weight; that one let the ending fall off unfinished and expected the next singer to catch it. Seventeen languages, then twenty, then a number Ilay stopped announcing because Roya had asked her to stop announcing it — the slate could only hold so many columns. Ilay stitched. That was the work nobody else in Ashkarad could have done, and it was not talent; it was tenure. She had been listening to the deep since before she had words for steps and circles, and the deep had been listening back, and between the two of them a text was being assembled the way coral assembles — one deaf ear at a time.

Stitching it together was the work of two nights and one funeral.

At the graveside, after the earth was done and the columns were ruled, one of the smaller children had said the thing about grandmother — she was like a grandmother to us — and Ilay had taken the word the way she took everything, literally, and filed it. A grandmother is the mother of your mother. She laid the word against the music the way Vahid laid two ledgers side by side. The mother's line ran through the Sister. The Sister and the stock children rang like two bells from one cast; the music said so; it had said so the day she walked in from the flats. Cast implies caster. The caster of the cast keeps the garden. The garden's song was being handed to her whole, every verse, the mother's line included — and things handed whole to a cast, in every ledger she had ever heard described, were not performances. They were inheritances. The one who sang the mother's line was the eldest of the cast. The one the garden belonged to was the mother of the mother's line. The word for that was at the graveside, in a small boy's mouth.

She titled the score on the night she finished it, in her careful child's hand, block letters pressed deep enough to read by touch: FOR MY GRANDMOTHER.

Then she did the only thing a transcriber can do with a finished text: she checked it against the source. She lay down on the shaft-head stone and sang the score back to the deep — silently, all of it, verse by verse through her bones, no air, no sound above a whisper's ghost, the NO SONGS law kept in the letter and spent in the ledger — and the deep below took the verses one at a time the way a teller takes a stack of coin, weighed each one, found every verse correct, and returned them with the mother's line added underneath, entire, the way a bank returns a deposit with the interest attached. Nothing tangled. Nothing came back short. On the last verse the deep held the ending a moment too long — the hinge at the page's edge — and let her go, gently, like a hand opening.

Nobody corrected her. Nobody in the clinic had a correction that would not have been a lie, or a worse truth. Vahid read the title over his spectacles, opened his mouth, closed it, and went down to the still. Dar read it and stood a long time with the pages in his one good hand and his ruined nerves, and what he finally said was nothing, because there was no entry in any of his books that paid for what the girl had worked out by ear.

She asked him for her place on the climb at the shaft head, two nights before the dark of the ration-bell, with the rig's canvas snapping somewhere below and the cold room's law chalked at its mouth — NO SONGS / NOT EVEN HUMMING — which she could feel through the stone like a silence with teeth.

"I can come down," she said. "The music goes down better if I carry it. It doesn't tangle in me. It's never once tangled in me."

Dar did not answer at once. He had a surgeon's silence for diagnosis, and she had learned to wait inside it without counting, because he always paid the silence back with the true figure.

"The door is shared between two," he said at last. "That's the doctrine. Not two plus an instrument. A third voice doesn't make the chord richer, Ilay. It makes it a chorus. And choruses open from above." He crouched to her height, which he always did, which had the odd effect of making the refusal feel like a consultation. "Tally told me the door checks your company, not your fortune. If I bring you down and stand you at the door, the door will check whether I love you more than I fear what's under the salt. It will open on the answer. The answer is yes. I'm not spending you on a door."

"Isra spends herself," Ilay said.

"Isra's already down there half the week, and she knows the shape of the thing she's walking into. You know songs, and songs are what the other side collects." He tapped her chalk, once, gently, with one finger. "Bring nothing you cannot lose. That's the Sister's own instruction, and I'm telling you what it prices. You're the thing on that list."

Ilay considered it. She did not cry; crying was a toll and she had never located the booth. She re-filed. If the music could not go down in her, it would go down in the one who was already half of both houses. The score needed a carrier who climbed.

She went and found Isra in the clinic's warm room, sitting with the smaller children asleep against her sound side, the rooted petal at her collarbone folded closed like a hand. Ilay put the score into her arms with both of her own arms, all of its sewn weight — every verse, the mother's line included, eleven pages of numbers, circles, and lines in a notation no academy had ever certified — and Isra took it the way a diver takes weight, settling it, testing its balance against her own.

Then she wrote, slowly, the root-toll visible in the whiteness of her fingertips: THIS IS THE MUSIC. I WILL BRING IT.

And beneath it, after a pause long enough that Ilay understood the second line had been priced before it was written: CARRIED. NOT COUNTED.

Vahid distilled the last of it on the final night, down in the cellar where the fourteen cylinders sat spooled on their brass spindle like a century of held breath.

The protocol had been six vials once. One had gone into Elias's blood in the reliquary corridor and saved a boy's verse. The other five stood in their fleece sleeves, and Vahid looked at them the way a man looks at five arguments he intends to combine into one. One-handed, spectacles mended with wire, he ran the still hot and then hotter, rendered the five vials down through glass and chilled coil and charcoal, and decanted at the end a single dose, no larger than a thumbnail of night, in a vial he wrapped in wool and tied with wire. Twice in the work he stopped, because the last cylinder was on the spindle and Shirin's voice was mid-sentence in the machine's memory, and he stood with his hand flat on the boiler until the sentence finished, the way a man stands at a graveside he has dug himself.

"A single injectable silence," he said, holding it up to the lamp so the dark showed through it, which was the only way it showed anything. "The rest, from Shirin's own recordings — the silences between her words. I have spent five voices' worth of rest to make one rest, which is either wasteful or the only honest arithmetic left in this city." He gave it into Dar's hands and closed Dar's fingers over it with his own remaining hand. "It is not medicine for a hymn you can hear. By the time you hear it, the blood is already singing. It is medicine for the verse before the verse — the one the body learns and doesn't tell you. One dose. One bloodstream. It will not make a child deaf to music, boy; it will make her blood deaf to orders. Choose the bloodstream like you'd choose the last stitch on a wound you can't redo."

Dar tied it into the kit that would go down with Isra, next to the score, and weighted the two of them together in his hand — the song and the silence, carried by the same pair of arms.

Isra carried the sewn score from the night she took it on, but it went down into the world still lacking its last page — Ilay had held the ending out of the sewing on purpose — and the last thing it needed was not music.

She finished the last page at the shaft head, two nights before the climb, with Roya reading her hands, and the page held the closing bars — the true song's ending, which she had heard whole, once, for the length of one bar, and had never been able to shake since, because the ending was wrong the way a door is wrong in a wall. It did not close. It did not resolve. Every verse stacked on the verse before it, and at the end, where a song should sign off and go quiet, the notation ran to the page's edge and stopped against something that was not silence but passage — a hinge drawn in numbers, a door drawn in circles. The ending was not a close. The ending was where the song stopped pretending to be a song.

She thought about the ones who collected things. She thought about the slips on the clinic wall, priced and priced again, and the one that had gone away stamped RECLAIMED, and the complete lullaby riding the road in the wain. Things that were counted could be collected. She pressed the chalk down until it squeaked.

On the last page, beneath the final bars, in her careful child's hand, she wrote the only instruction the score would ever carry:

the ending is a door. don't let them count it.
