# Chapter 86 — Testimony at the Door

The council went down to the deep on the morning of the fourth day, and it went down as witnesses, which in Ashkarad was the highest office the city had ever invented.

Dar had convened it the night before in the dining hall of the Choratorium ruins, under Gavel's provisional roof, and the summons had been written in four hands on one sheet: *The first seed will testify at the door. The city is invited to attend. Come as witnesses or don't come; there is no third column on this page.* Suri signed first, because it was her census that had promised the Sister an answer eventually. Milla signed under her, registrar's hand, and asked only one question — "Will I need the certified forms?" — and packed them anyway. The Choirmaster signed and said nothing, which from the Choirmaster was a paragraph. Mother Gavel's answer came back by runner on the boss's slate: *I'll attend. I owe the deep a look at its creditor.*

Roya held the shaft head with Vell, whose chalk was faster than any transcript when the words came slow, and Ilay went down because the words came *through* Ilay — that had been the arrangement since the first deposition, what the stone said the girl said, and no one had found a better instrument in a year of trying. Isra went down as the door's answer, the Vessel, the one the first seed would speak in front of without the stone costing her more than she could pay. Fourteen of the stock children were kept above with the Choirmaster's freed donors, over Elias's flat-voiced objection. This was not a hearing they needed to attend. They had already given their blood's testimony to the claiming-hymn. The less said back to it, the better.

The Open Counts sent no witness — Clerk Elara's splintered remnant had asked, in writing, only for the certified copy, *to be posted door by door within a day of the close,* which was its own kind of attendance. The Index sent no one at all. Whatever the Index had been since its clerks crossed the quarry floor with their stamps held up like surrendered weapons, it was no longer in the business of hearings. Seven hundred and eleven steps, then, for the people who would actually stand in the room: a courier, a clerk, a choirmaster, a boss, a surgeon, a slate, and a girl.

Seven hundred and eleven steps. Milla counted them against her transcript margin and noted, in the clerk's hand that had survived two purges, that the cold came up out of the ground like a bill.

The antechamber above the door had been swept and bench-lined since the night three armies had filled it, and the witnesses were set along the wall with lamps hooded to embers, because light taxed the deep and everyone present had learned the tax code. The salt-glass door stood wide open. The hairline crack sang its four notes in its dead languages, patient as ever, and behind the door the dark kept its own counsel, one beat behind Dar's heart, three chambers to the count.

Then the deep began to speak, and the girl gave it her voice.

It came up through Ilay the way water comes up through a well-chain, hand over hand, the girl's flat unchildlike cadence carrying words she could not have owned in a lifetime of owning words, and Milla took them down in the certified hand, and Vell's chalk talked up the shaft to Roya above, and this is the shape of what the century said:

That there had been a world before this one, planted by keepers who did not know what they were planting, and that it had burned in the catastrophe the far cities called the First Burning; that the world's root was buried below the salt to keep it from the fire, and the Gardener's own daughter above it, locked where the lid could be kept; and that she herself — the first seed, the eldest — had been set where a witness is set, which is close enough to a lid that over a century she had stopped being able to tell the difference; and that there had been no malice in any of the locking. Only arithmetic. The Gardener had counted what was left, buried the half that frightened her, posted watchers over it, and gone away to sow.

That the nine seeds were sown a century ago with the words *Go. Find your hearts. And bloom,* and that she had counted them go, one to a city, like coins leaving a dead woman's purse; and that BLOOMED—TRUE THRONE, the stamp the far cities celebrated, meant *ripe*; and that the harvest was the harvest — Brack first, then the others on the round, pruned city by city and poured down the deep roads to keep the buried root dreaming. She had watched each one die. She had kept the list. The list had been sent up once already, through her Vessel, every city named and dated, prepared for a trial she had always assumed was coming, because in her accounting she had never once been on the side of the court.

Here Ilay's voice slowed, and Milla's pen slowed with it, because the witness had come to the entry the city actually needed.

*NOT EVIL,* the slate said. *I KNOW WHAT THE WORD DOES. I HAVE HAD A CENTURY TO STUDY IT FROM THE INSIDE. LONELY. THE ENTRY IS LONELY.*

Gavel asked the first question. She asked it the way she asked everything, as if reading terms across a table that did not exist.

"The feeding," she said. "Name it. What does the deep eat, and in what coin?"

The answer came back without pause, in the girl's cadence, flat as poured wax. *CITIES. POURED DOWN THE DEEP ROADS, RENDERED AND CARRIED, ONE PER ACCOUNT, ON A SCHEDULE. THE COIN IS THE SCHEDULE ITSELF. THE GARDENER FEEDS THE ROOT TO KEEP IT DREAMING, AND DREAMING, IT DOES NOT RISE.* A pause, the length of a page turning. *BRACK WAS EATEN IN AN AFTERNOON. LOWMARK MARRIED ITSELF TO THE FEEDING AND CALLED IT WORSHIP. GULLET BUILT A MACHINE TO MILK ITS OWN SEED AND CALLED THE MILK AN ENGINE. I HAVE COUNTED EVERY ONE. I WILL READ THE COUNT TO ANYONE WHO ASKS. IT IS THE ONLY BOOK I HAVE EVER KEPT THAT NO ONE HAS EVER WANTED TO SEE.*

The Choirmaster asked the second question, and his voice, the melodic baritone that had graded children like fruit, came out of the vault's cold with frost on it.

"The collar," he said. "The Ordinal says *maintain tension, sing to load.* Forty years, the machine I kept. Was the song ever — " and for the first time in his professional life he chose a word carefully — "*received?*"

*THE COLLAR SANG TO THE ROOT,* the deep said. *IT WAS MEANT TO. IT WAS THE GARDENER'S OWN LULLABY, RANK BY RANK, VOICE BY VOICE, FORTY YEARS OF HARVESTED THROATS FEEDING THE ONE SONG DOWN THE SHAFT. THE ROOT SLEPT. THAT PART OF THE BOOK BALANCED.* Another pause, and this one had weight in it, the weight of an entry held back for a hundred years. *AND THE GARDENER LISTENED DOWN THE COLLAR EVERY NIGHT OF THOSE FORTY YEARS FOR A VOICE. NOT THE ROOT'S. SHE LISTENED FOR A VOICE THAT USED TO SING UP AT HER FROM THE OTHER SIDE OF THE BURNING. THE SONG WENT DOWN. THE VOICE SHE HEARD COMING UP WAS SOMETHING ELSE. I HAVE TRIED TO FILE THIS ENTRY GENTLY FOR A YEAR. I CANNOT. IT DOES NOT FILE GENTLY.*

And then the afraid part, which was the part that rearranged the room.

*I AM AFRAID OF HER,* the deep said. *YOU HAVE HEARD ME SAY IT THROUGH MY VESSEL'S HAND AND YOU HAVE FILED IT AS THE FEAR OF THE POWERFUL. FILE IT AGAIN. THE GARDENER IS AFRAID TOO. SHE IS THE MOST FRIGHTENED THING I HAVE EVER COUNTED.*

Isra sat with her slate in her lap and her hands folded over it, and the cold of the antechamber kept taking her warmth the way it always did, letter by letter paid out of her own body, and she did not stop the deposition to rest, because the witness below had waited a century to be heard and the Vessel's arithmetic was simple: whatever the transcript cost, it was being bought at a discount.

The witnesses did not move. Gavel stood with her arms folded like a woman auditing a rival's books and finding them, line by line, honest — the worst discovery her profession allowed. The Choirmaster had gone the color of his own reliquary glass. And the Sister testified on, in the girl's flat cadence, to the thing beneath the thing beneath the salt:

The Root had stopped answering her decades ago.

Not dead — her count of its heartbeats was exact, two million and more, kept since the Burning, and last week, for the first time, it had skipped one. Not dead. But the answering had stopped: the voice under the world, the one the Gardener had buried her world's heart to keep, no longer spoke back to the hand that fed it. Decades of silence, and the Gardener — who had built her whole order on the premise that the deep could be kept dreaming, audited, fed on schedule — had gone on feeding it *blind.* The heralds' tallies were her ears. The flower-stamped chits were her hearing-aid. Every pruned city, every tally-book, every stamped line of the Reclaim was the sound of a woman listening at a keyhole for a voice that had stopped caring whether she stood there, and hearing, always, something that answered in its place.

Milla's salt-whitened fingers kept the transcript even through that. It was Gavel who broke the silence first, flat as a ledger page, the brass tube still slung at her hip where she had carried it down without explaining why.

"The harvest," she said, to the door, in the voice she used for reading terms. "Forty years of the Chorus. My mother's generation in the tithe-houses. The nursery. All of it — sedation. One long lullaby fed down the shaft to keep the beast under the floor asleep, because its keeper couldn't hear it anymore and was afraid to stop the song and find out why." She looked at the Choirmaster. "Your Church wasn't a religion. It was her night-light."

The Choirmaster took that the way he had taken the breaking of his wrist — as a professional reviewing another professional's technique. "Forty years of harvested voices," he said, "one long lullaby, fed to the dark." He turned his fire-axe once in his hands, the way he did when the numbers frightened him. "I kept the dead's house ready for singing. I was keeping *her* house ready. I'd like the record to show which of those I can still stand."

The last of it came up quiet.

The Sister had one more entry, and she filed it the way the oldest filers file — without preface, without mercy for the listener, in the space of two sentences, and afterward Ilay stood with her hands pressed to the stone and her eyes shut, repeating it exactly, because the stone had said it twice and the stone did not repeat anything twice for emphasis.

"She thinks she still hears my mother," Ilay said. "She hears the Root imitating her."

For a moment the antechamber held nothing but the four notes of the crack, going around their dead languages, over and over.

And then, for the first time in the whole year of testimony, the deep spoke without the girl. The words came up out of the stone themselves, through the floor, through the walls, through every crack in the door's singing glass, soft as frost forming, in the voice they had all heard once before at a pane of salt-glass — the mother's under-line, the harmony below the child's thread — and every witness present would swear, in the affidavits Milla took down that evening, that the voice was not speaking to the room at all.

It was correcting the record. The way a clerk corrects a file. The way the eldest corrects the youngest. The way, below every ledger in Ashkarad, the deep kept its books.

*She thinks she still hears my mother,* the stone said, to no one, in the dark. *She hears the Root imitating her.*

Milla wrote it twice — once in the certified transcript, once on her own copy, with the salt under her nails and her hand perfectly steady — and above them, up seven hundred and eleven steps, Vell's chalk came down the record like a stamp coming down on a page, and the testimony of the first seed was complete.
