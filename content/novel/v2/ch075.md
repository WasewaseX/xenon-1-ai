# Chapter 75 — Terms on Every Door

The fleet came the way a debt comes: first the quiet, then the notice, then the men with the books.

Three weeks after the Gardener named the Root, the seawall watch saw the lights first, an hour past midnight — not the scattered watch-fires of an army but a grid, lanterns hung in ruled rows, mile on mile, laid out on the dry seabed where the fog-sea used to stand. Ilay heard it before the bells could be rung, from her window over the clinic, with her palms flat on the sill: not marching, she signed to Roya, not even walking. Adding. A sound like a city's worth of clerks wetting pens. Roya chalked the map of what could not be marched on, because nothing out there was moving except arithmetic, and by the time the garrison bells had finished arguing about which of them was authorized to ring, the thing had a shape.

The bells argued for a quarter of the night. That was the system's epitaph in miniature: three garrison towers, three jurisdictions, three chits of standing authority, and not one of them stamped for an event that had never been pre-filed — a fleet arriving on the seabed like an invoice laid against the horizon. The Index wanted the ringing held pending confirmation. The garrison wanted it held pending the Index. The ward bells wanted a payment account opened for the rope-wear. In the end a saltstairs child rang the sinner's bell at the top of the tenements, three strokes, unauthorized, unpriced, and the city woke to look, which was all the alarm Ashkarad needed and the only kind it had left.

Not an army. An audit.

The camp covered the seabed from the old tide-line to the horizon's rule: tents of clerks in ranks as straight as ledger lines, chimneys of sheet brass breathing thin smoke into the salt-dark, long open-sided sheds where bone tally-frames stood in racks like muskets. White-gloved auditors moved down the avenues at filing pace, pairs of them, stopping at each tent-flap to read, initial, and move on, and behind them in slow patrol walked ranks of the herald-chorus — robed, barefoot on the salt, spaced with the precision of pipes in an organ — humming, very low, in unison, a note that Dar felt from the seawall in his teeth and in the gravel-heart's scar and in the black lace under his skin: the pitch of a collection being scheduled.

And at the center, where an army would have kept its general, stood the wain.

It was the size of a chapel that had decided to travel. Brass ribs, lead-glass panels, a hull of banded iron, wheels taller than a tithe-barge's mast, and even at three miles, even in the dark, the light through its panels was wrong — a patient glow, like the interior of a reliquary, like something kept alive in glass and tended by the book. Around it, at a distance nobody had ordered and everybody observed, the camp's avenues bent slightly, as if the ground had agreed to circumnavigate the thing rather than cross it. Frost furred the tent-ropes of the whole camp grey, and every petal of it pointed at Ashkarad. Twice in the night, the wain's engine turned over — a sound like a hymn being swallowed — and the whole grid of lanterns brightened one step, all together, the way a ledger breathes when the ink goes down.

Nobody in the city attacked it. Dar understood the refusal without having to broker it, because he shared it: you do not attack an audit. An audit re-files whatever is aimed at it. It would have taken the militia's charge and entered it under EXPENSES, DONOR: ASHKARAD, and the charge would have been real, and the entry permanent.

The out-riders reached the tide-gates before dawn — six of them on the salt-hard mud where water had once argued with the walls, and five were clerks with canvas cases strapped to their backs. The sixth was a captain.

Bone-plate, and the mask smooth as a skull, and he stood apart from the clerks the way a signature stands apart from the body of a letter. Dar saw him from the wall through a garrison glass and felt the year of the prologue arrive in his stomach like bad interest: the same mask, the same stillness, the man who had knelt in a burning organ dock with Dar's blood on his gauntlet, tasted it, and pronounced his verdict — it chose; as it always does; the choir must be complete. He had not aged. That was the detail Dar's hands understood before his mind agreed to it: not one line different, not one plate re-set, a man filed under a permanence the city had no category for. The skull-face turned slowly along the seawall, reading the city the way a tuning fork is presented to its note, and did not look up, and did not need to.

"He's here for the choir," Suri said at his shoulder, flat, counting the out-riders' positions off the wall's shadow. "Not for us. We're the venue."

The out-riders did not attempt the gates. They posted one page on the tide-gate's customs post, weighted its corners with frost that grew through the paper like a biopsy, and withdrew to the camp at filing pace, and the garrison, to its credit and its shame, let them go, because the writ of every man on that wall said do not engage in the same handwriting. The bone-masked captain withdrew last, walking backward, the way sentries walk at a tomb, and he took up a station on the flats midway between the gate and the camp and stayed there, apart, facing the city — and he would stand there, Dar would learn, all that day and the days after, posting nothing, reading nothing, collecting nothing. He was not a signature and he was not a clerk. He was the seal. And seals, Dar knew from a year of this city's papers, were what got pressed into you when the terms ran out.

By dawn, every door in Ashkarad bore posted terms.

Every door — the clinic's, the ward offices', the sealed wards', the Gavel's quota-house, the pump-works, the cistern-gates, the tenements of the Saltstairs from top stair to bottom, doors that hadn't opened in years, doors behind doors; the Open Counts counted eleven thousand four hundred and some before they gave up counting, and the count did not include the doors of the deep, where Roya reported frost on the salt-glass itself arranged in ruled blocks, like text, patiently waiting for someone to read it to her. The papers were grey stock, good stock, durable as tithe-contracts, and no one had seen them posted, and no nail in the city had been touched; the pages were pinned by their corners in frost-petals, grown through, pointing inward.

Dar stood in the clinic doorway and read Ashkarad's account, and the terms were everything the night had promised and the syntax made worse, because the syntax was kind.

The tribute was named in the first line: the stock children, entire, delivered to the wain; and the ninth seed, delivered in its carrier. The consideration followed in the Church's oldest generous grammar: in exchange, and for the term of the arrangement, a place for the city of Ashkarad at the tail of the pruning schedule, with provisions, with quotas, with the light of the bloom shared according to need. There were sub-clauses. There were always sub-clauses. The provisions clause ran to nine lines and provided, among other mercies, grain against work, water against work, and the continuance of the city's ration in the event of compliance — the Gavel ration, adopted whole into the Gardener's grammar, order through debt at a scale that made Mother Gavel's own books read like a child's abacus. And then the schedule itself, printed city by city, in an order that was not an order until you understood it was a queue:

LOWMARK. THE VIGIL. GULLET. SARN. WEEP. OSSUAR. HALLOWFEN. ASHKARAD — LAST.

Brack was not on it. Brack had been first, once, and Brack was now an entry in a tally-book's first line — PRUNED, PAID — and the absence of the name was the loudest thing on the page. The tail of the schedule was not mercy. It was the queue for the knife, and Ashkarad had been placed last because its seed had fled its carrier, and the Gardener's books had never once lost an asset, and the last city on a harvest schedule is the city that watches seven harvests before its own, with provisions.

Suri read the due line aloud, because someone had to and her voice was the steadiest instrument left: "Payment due: the next tide." She looked at the sea's absence beyond the seawalls, the dry mile, the horizon standing where water should have been, and she did the city's arithmetic in one flat breath. "The tide has been going out since the day you walked out those walls. It hasn't come back. They're not naming a date, Mehran. They're naming a condition we already meet."

Dar walked the city all morning and read the terms eleven times, in eleven doorways, because he kept hoping one of the postings would differ, and none of them did — that was the point of them. In the Choratorium ruins, Mother Gavel read her copy once, with her thumb on the tribute line, and issued the only commentary her books contained: "Tribute lines are where they tell you what they're afraid to take." She ordered her crews to hold the water-works and the quarry and nothing else, and put her clerks to counting what a city eats in a season, because the terms said provisions, and provisions meant the audit was prepared to wait, and the Gavel had been waiting out sieges since before the Church learned to sing. At the annex, the Index re-keyed its pulse-readers against a hymn that had not yet been sung, and filed the terms as EVIDENCE, FOREIGN, PENDING — the machine in Seft's office printing the schedule over and over, as if repetition were jurisdiction. The Open Counts chalked the whole text onto the Choratorium's outer wall in Clerk Elara's hand, big enough to read from the funiculars, because their doctrine had never changed: a city that has read its own bill cannot be surprised by the collector. And in the reliquary corridor the Choirmaster took down his copy with his fire-axe's edge, carefully, the way you take a splinter from a hand you intend to keep, and read the tribute line twice, and said nothing all day, which from him was a paragraph.

The terms were countersigned at the foot of every page, and the signature was the last item audited, and it was wrong in the way a stamped form is wrong — perfectly, deliberately wrong.

Not THE GARDENER. Not the flower sigil that bloomed from a crack in the earth, not the left-handed flourish whose curls matched the mark that had signed nine cities a century ago — none of it. The signature block read, in a clerk's practiced round hand:

WARDEN OF THE RECLAIM, PER G.

Per G. The initial of something the size of the sky, initialed. Dar stood on the clinic step with eleven thousand pages of terms posted on eleven thousand doors around him, and understood that the Gardener had not signed at all — she had been signed for. Somewhere under the sea that wasn't there, someone kept her mark the way the Church had once kept its stamps, in a box, in a drawer, issued to authorized hands; and the hand that posted Ashkarad's terms was authorized, and the authority ran down from the flower sigil through the office of the Warden of the Reclaim and stopped, like everything in this world's books, one initials-line short of a name.

"Bureaucracy," Vahid said, over his spectacles, reading the signature block with the expression of a man confirming a diagnosis, "all the way down."
