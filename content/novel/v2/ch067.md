# Chapter 67 — Common Property

The descent took seven hundred and eleven steps, and Dar counted them, because counting was the load-bearing wall and everything else had been sold off it: two hundred to the dead First Vessel's cold cathedral, four hundred more through the dark where the walls rang like struck glass, and the last stretch down the new-cut stair to the rig the clinic had built over the salt-glass door.

The rig was a cold room — canvas walls, brass frame, two chairs — with the room's law chalked on the canvas by Vahid's own hand: NO SONGS. NOT EVEN HUMMING. The law had been there since the first session and had never yet been obeyed by the building itself. The door breathed at the far wall, ajar, warm, patient, and the dark behind it was not empty the way a room is empty; it was empty the way a held breath is empty.

The cold was not weather. It came off the door in a slow flood, and it had purposes: it kept the climbers' blood thick and their hearing sharp, and it kept the Lily settled in Dar's chest the way a cat settles in a shadowed window. Darkness fed her; the deep had darkness to spare. That was the other reason Vahid had built the rig down here instead of up in the clinic, though he had phrased it as geology. The ninth seed climbed better at the bottom of the world, where her eldest sister could hear her through the wall.

Isra was already in her chair. The watchers had gone up at the eighth bell — Suri's rule now, fewer witnesses to a tithe, fewer debts owed on one — and the salt-burned woman waited alone with a black petal spread at her collarbone like a brooch pinned through her, still as a diver at the edge of a drop. Her slate lay across her knees. On it, in her slow effortful strokes, she had written the evening's only business: I KNOW WHAT IT TAKES TONIGHT. DO YOU?

Dar sat, and put his hands on the armrests, and answered out loud, because the rig's rule was no songs, not no words. "I know."

The pulse-reader clicked itself on. Nobody touched it anymore. Since the fourth session it had run when they ran, and its stylus printed in the small administrative grammar of everything in this city that had ever taken an account, and tonight, before either of them had closed their eyes, it printed two lines:

RHYTHM PAIRED.
TITHE: ONE EACH.

The room between them had been built out of two childhoods and was now furnished by three debts.

It had not started as a room. It had started as a floor — Isra's drowned flats, laid down the first night she paid, a tide-line of kelp-smell and wet sand across nothing — and then his Blacktide refusal had gone in beside it: an altar, a cracked reliquary, a single word standing in the air like a man refusing to sit. NO, said in a graft-theater, to a god's own invitation. The room had kept both payments on open display, the way good rooms keep what they are built from, and every session since had added its tithe to the shelf, and the shelf had grown, and now the room was a room — walls, a lamp that cast no shadows, the two payments at its heart like hearth-stones.

That was the doctrine Shirin Bahar had left in wax, and Vahid taught it at the clinic ladder with his one hand and his wire-mended spectacles: the Soul was not a rung but the shared organ, grafted between people rather than grown in one — and a graft between two minds made a space, and the space had customs, and the customs had a price schedule. Every session the room took its tithe. Not spent, the way the Mind Vein spent — spent was a withdrawal, a room stripped to the nail-holes — but paid over, entire, into common property: held thereafter by everyone in the graft, irretrievable by the giver alone, forever. You could not buy it back. There was no registry house for it.

They had learned the rule the way you learn any ledger, by receiving a bill. On the fifth night the room had taken, unchosen, a memory of Dar's — a doorway, a color of paint — and Isra had lost the taste of her mother's cooking for a whole session, gone from her like a tide, back on waking but diminished, a page re-copied by a stranger. After that, Vahid's law: you choose the tithe before the room chooses for you. You pay what you can afford to have, not what you can afford to lose. There was no third category.

Tonight Dar walked his own corridors to choose.

He went down the halls of his mind with the discipline Vahid had drilled into him, past the swept rooms he visited like graves — mother's face, father's laugh, first kiss, home's color, all stripped to pay the war — and past the furnished door he had never built, which showed its thread of lamplight as always and was not for him. In the deep residential quarter of himself there was a kitchen. Steam on a window. The grain of a table worn pale by two generations of elbows. A voice singing.

The face was gone; it had gone to the Blacktide, spent like a coin, and no part of it had ever come back. But the voice had survived the spend, the way a nail-hole survives the picture — the lullaby, sung low, in a kitchen, by a woman whose face he had paid away and whose voice he had somehow been allowed to keep, and for years he had not known whose voice it was. The lullaby remained. The ownership had not.

He stood in the kitchen the way you stand at a counter, and counted the costs of paying it over. If he gave the memory, the room would hold it — Isra would know a mother singing; the dark below would know it. And the lullaby itself would stay in him, but stripped of its one attestation: it would never again be the song his mother sang. It would be only what the world had already made it — the Lily's song, the Gardener's song, everyone's song and no one's — and the last small ledger that said a woman with no face had sung it in a kitchen with steam on the glass would close.

He could afford to have that. He could not afford to lose anything load-bearing, and nothing had ever been more load-bearing than the proof that the song had a first singer.

He stood a while longer, doing the final audit, because the last part of the price was the part you paid afterward, every day, forever. Above, in the city, there were people who hummed the lullaby without knowing what they carried — Hesh with one note wrong, the tide-priests selling it by the reading, the Church feeding it to the deep for forty years through forty years of harvested throats. After tonight he would hear it in strangers' mouths and feel nothing he could name, because the name would be gone — not the tune, never the tune, but the fact that it had belonged to anyone before it belonged to the dark. He paid anyway, because the alternative was letting the room choose, and the room did not love anyone.

He carried the kitchen to the room's center and set it down.

The room took it the way the press at Lowmark took payment — no violence, a settling, a click of weight accepted — and where it stood there was now a shelf he had not built, and on the shelf, held in common, the memory sat: steam, table, voice. His to visit. Never again only his.

Isra's slate tapped the chair-arm twice. In the between-space her slate was slate and her writing was writing; that was the shape her side of the graft had taken, and no one had chosen it, and it was hers. She wrote for a long time, slower than speech, and turned it to him.

MY BROTHER'S NAME.

Then, beneath, because she never wrote one line when the truth needed three:

HE MARRIED THE BRIDE TO CLEAR OUR HOUSE'S DEBT. THE MARRIED LEAVE THEIR NAMES AT THE TEMPLE DOOR. THE BOOKS DROWNED ANYWAY. I AM THE ONLY FILE LEFT.

Dar understood what she was pricing, and understood it was the exact twin of his. Her brother's name existed nowhere now — struck from the temple register by doctrine, drowned with the port's records — except in one salt-burned diver's memory, and now she was going to make it common property: held by the room, by him, by the dark below. Not destroyed. Mutualized. She would no longer be able to reach it alone, ever, for the rest of her life — and it would survive her, because nothing held in common dies with one holder.

She wrote the name once, in careful letters, and let him read it — a short name, coastal, worn smooth like a stone from a shingle — and then she paid it, and the shelf took it, and the slate in her hands was blank, and she looked at the blankness with her head tilted like someone listening to whether the sea was still where she had left it.

The name sat on the shelf beside the kitchen. Dar held it now, in the way the room held things — not remembered, resident. He knew the boy without ever having met him: older by four years, patient with rope, the one who had taught a small girl that fear was a number you counted down, and that the count always landed. He knew it the way Isra now knew the inside of a kitchen she had never stood in. That was the graft: two climbers, one shelf, and nothing of either one of them entirely alone again.

Then she wrote, in strokes that took her a long time:

NOW IT IS OURS. OURS IS BETTER THAN MINE. MINE WAS SOMETHING THE WATER COULD TAKE.

The tithe paid, the room settled, and the work went on — the slow, wordless diligence of two nervous systems learning to be one organ, the pulse-reader printing its paired rhythms in ever-tightening registration, one beat behind, then half, then finding the interval that held. The lullaby's shelf stayed warm. Common property always did; that was in Shirin's cylinders too, though the cylinder had ended mid-word — and then you will hear the sound — and no one had ever heard the sound.

The pulse-reader printed twice more before the hour turned. TITHE: RECEIVED. TITHE: RECEIVED. It did not print received from whom. Machines in Ashkarad had stopped distinguishing their debtors long ago; a tithe was a tithe.

And then, at the room's edge, where the floor became not-floor and the wall became the standing dark of the one who never crossed, the Sister began to hum.

Softly. The softest thing Dar had ever felt with his teeth. The rig's law was NO SONGS, not even humming, and the law had been written for the climbers, because songs were keys and the door was locked with them — and the first song the rig ever heard came from below its own floor, from the dark that had stood a century at the threshold of her own rescue, and it did not come to open anything.

It came to sit with what had just been paid.

She hummed the lullaby — and not the line of it, not the child's thread of it that Dar had carried all his life without knowing whose it was. She hummed the under-line. The harmony. The line a mother lays beneath a child's singing so the child cannot get lost — low, steady, going where the going is dark, coming back, coming back. The mother's line, not the child's.

She had heard the kitchen through the shelf. Everything paid into the room, she heard; she was the third heartbeat at the wall, the listener, the eldest, and the memory of a faceless woman singing in a steam-fogged kitchen had settled where she could reach it, and she was holding the under-line of it the way you hold something someone has just trusted you with.

The Lily stirred in Dar's chest and answered through his ribs, taking the child's thread against the dark's mother-line, two songs fitting, and Isra sat with her blank slate and her hand at her collarbone petal, and Dar stood in a room built from two childhoods and listened to his mother's line sung back to him from below the world.

He had paid the memory to keep the song. The song had gone into the common dark. And the common dark, which had waited longer than the Church, longer than the ledgers, longer than the salt, was paying it back out — one held breath at a time, the under-line, the mother's line — like a refund from a house that had never once, in a century, been asked what it was owed.
