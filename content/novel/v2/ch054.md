# Chapter 54 — Two Circles Under

The horns at the seawall gate woke Ilay before the light did — three short and one long, the new cadence, the one the Index used when it was counting something in — and she was on the stair with her palm flat against the wall before she was properly awake, because the wall under her hand was singing in two voices, and for as long as she had been alive it had sung in one.

She kept her ear there and counted the city waking, because the hearing was hers and nobody in Ashkarad could repossess it. The Index first: boots in double file down the Saltstairs cut, a new step they had learned since the spring, shorter at the front than the back so that a whole patrol sounded like one animal with forty feet. The crank-and-clack of the new readers riding on their shoulders, winding themselves as the bearers walked, little brass throats clearing themselves every nine steps. Then the Gavel: slower boots, heavier, and under them the dry click of ledger-counters being thumbed open and shut, open and shut, the sound of Mother Gavel's crews doing their morning arithmetic around the Choratorium ruins they held. Then, under that, a sound she had to reach for: nothing. The ward offices of the Open Counts had been chalked shut for eleven days now, one line on each door, and even shut doors made a small sound if you knew how to listen, a held-breath sound, and this morning eleven doorways' worth of held breath was the loudest quiet in the district.

And under all of it, the deep.

The deep music had come home. She had no better way to put it, and she had tried, on slate, on the wall, in her mouth without sound: the great slow beat that had walked its circles under the flats for a month and gone under the city while the two-hearted man was away had risen, and it was in the vaults now, beneath the Throne shaft, filling the salt the way a tide fills a channel it has known for a thousand years. The first voice had never left — the older one, the patient one under everything, the one she had drawn as the center of the spiral before she knew spirals were the wrong shape. Now there were two, and the wall carried both into her teeth, and one of them had changed its rhythm in the night.

She heard the gate open the way she heard everything, from the inside of the sound. The bar coming out of its cradle. The wardens' boots going to their marks. And then, past the horns and the mud-smell and the wind that came off the dry seabed tasting of iron and old salt, the two-hearted man — his two heartbeats, the count she had memorized before she had words for counting — coming up the seawall road with the woman who counted aloud under her breath and a third set of steps that kept stopping to chalk things, and behind all of it, faint, the wooden knock of wax cylinders in a porter's frame.

"They're in," she said, to the stair, to the clinic below her, to nobody, and went down to the door before the knock came.

They came in like a ledger being balanced after a long audit: the two-hearted man first, salt-white to the knees, then the counting woman with her ruined arm carried careful against her ribs, then the chalk woman, Vell, who looked at the clinic doorway the way Brack's dead must have looked at theirs. Behind them, out on the mud, the fog was closing again over the scored flats, where for half a mile the writing stood in the mud that had been the sea — GO HOME, NINTH, in letters tall as houses, cut clean, and Ilay had heard the wardens argue for two days about whether writing could be wet when there had been no rain.

Inside, the two-hearted man stood still in the middle of the front room with his eyes shut, and the Lily leaned him, the way she did — a degree off true, toward the floor, toward the deep — and Ilay watched the woman who counted put out her good hand and steady his elbow without being asked, the way you hold a scale steady while the needle finds its number.

"It's doubled," he said. "I can hear it from here. Roya?"

"Upstairs," Roya said, from the stair, brass mask catching the lamp. "Wall's ready."

The rest of the news had come up the stair with the counting woman, flat, at her working volume, the way she read numbers that meant people: the wain had passed the reef-city eleven days ago, bound for Ashkarad, on the road, on schedule, and no one on the seawalls could say from which quarter it would come up, because the sea was gone and the roads were mud and mud keeps no heralds out. Behind that, the flats themselves: scored, a mile of them, in the dead language the deep had used before there were ledgers, and the score of it said, in the only line anyone could render: go home, ninth. And behind even that, the thing the party carried in a porter's frame without yet showing anyone: fourteen wax cylinders, a doctor's voice, and the two-hearted man's face when he set them down on the cellar shelf, which was the face of a man adding a bill he already knew he could not pay.

The Index came for the water-line at midmorning, because the Index did everything on schedule now. Ilay was at the cistern with the others, Elias and the two youngest of the eleven and a dozen Saltstairs children whose names were on nobody's page, and the patrol came around the corner in its one-animal stride, and the people in the line did the thing the people of Ashkarad had learned to do this season, which was to become very still and very countable. Auditor Seft walked at the front with the new reader on a strap against his chest, and the new reader was not the old reader; the old one had listened for debts and found two rhythms it could not file, and this one had a wider throat and a different appetite. This one listened for the hymn. It had been built, she heard one grey coat tell another, keyed to the training — the nursery's forty bars, the compliance-song, the thing the Church had sewn into stock blood before the fall — because a hymn, once planted, never fully leaves; it waits in the veins like a note waits in a string, and a machine with a wide enough throat can hear the string humming.

They washed the children's hands first, because it looked like hygiene. Then the needle-cuff, then the crank, nine turns, and the machine printed its small hot slip, and Elias went last because he always put himself last, and Elias's blood sang. She heard it leave him — forty bars, rising out of his skin like heat off a stove, the claiming-hymn answering the machine the way a named thing answers its name — and she heard the machine file it.

Elias did not look at the slip. He looked at his own hands, as if the singing had come out of them and he was checking them for damage. The grey coats wrote his name — they had his name; the Index always had the name — and Auditor Seft looked at Ilay, one beat longer than he looked at anyone else, the look of a man reading a column total and finding it does not sum.

"Sanctuary," Roya said, arriving with the seal-board under her arm like a woman carrying a tray. "Both seals. You counted them on the door yourself in the spring, Auditor. Shall I read you the terms, or will your machine do it?"

Ilay had kept still through all of it, hands in the water, eyes down, the way Roya had taught her — be countable, be boring, be a child — though her own blood would have answered the machine louder than any of them, graded perfect, reserved for a throne that no longer had a church to sit in. She counted the crank turns instead: nine, every time, nine turns to a reading, and the machine's little hot slips stacked on the auditor's board like a month of receipts.

Seft wound the crank down. "The machine hears what's there," he said. "I only record that it heard." And he took his patrol up the lane in its one-animal stride, and the line breathed again, and Ilay put her hand flat on the cistern lip and felt the deep music carry both its voices up through the water, and the water shivered in rings, two sizes of ring, like a thing said twice in two languages.

The city spent that day and the next arming itself against an enemy that had not yet named its price. Braziers went up along the storm-walls, and the wardens' bells called the watches in a pattern frayed from overuse. Tithe-carts were dragged across the lane-mouths and chalked with the owners' names, which in Ashkarad was the oldest kind of barricade there was. Mother Gavel's counters stood their post across from the clinic with slates and tallied the foot-traffic hour by hour, and the tally had stopped being about protection and started being about appetite, and everyone on both sides of the lane knew it and priced it in. At the front desk, Milla Ferun counted the day's patients in her clerk's hand, square and even, and twice during the afternoons Ilay saw her stop, look at her own fingernails, and go on, and the salt was back under them, though Milla Ferun had not been underground in weeks.

"We're home," the two-hearted man said, in the cellar, with the chalk ladder over his shoulder and Vahid already arguing the Veins at him like a man reclaiming a stolen coat. "That's what's wrong with it."

On the second night the clinic's map wall got its new sheet.

Roya worked the way she always worked now, one-handed on the chalk, the brass mask tilted to catch Ilay's hearing the way a dish catches oil, and Ilay stood at the wall and gave her the deep the way she gave her everything, in counts and circles, line by line, the two voices laid on the salt like strata. The new voice first — the one that had come home. It sat high, as deep-things went: in the vaults, under the Throne shaft, behind the salt-glass door where a petal had once tapped out a lullaby to a man with two heartbeats. It kept time. That was the first thing she told Roya, and Roya made her say it three times, because it had kept time before it left and it kept time now that it was back: one beat behind the two-hearted man's own pulse, patient, exactly, the way a copyist lays a line one rule down from the original.

"It's holding still," Ilay said. "It came home and it lay down. It's listening."

"Listening to what?"

"To him." She touched the second circle. "And to that."

The older voice had no floor she could find. It had been under the spiral's center all along, so deep the shaft's warning had been nailed to rock above it — DO NOT COUNT THE HEARTBEATS — and it had never once, in her whole hearing, changed its pace: vast, slow, regular as interest, the metronome everything else in the city had been unknowingly set to. Tonight it had turned over. That was the phrase that came and would not leave: it had turned over, the way a sleeper turns over, the way a body in a bed shifts when the house above it goes quiet, and the turning had taken three of the two-hearted man's heartbeats to complete, and when it finished, the deep music was two voices where it had been one, and the new voice had come home to lie down above it like a hand laid flat on a lid.

Roya chalked the two circles one inside the other, dated the sheet in her small surveyor's hand, and stood back, and the mask held its metal calm, and her voice came out level as a wire.

"Two circles under the city."

"Two," Ilay said. "The new one came home. The old one is waking up."

Below them, faint through the clinic's floor, through the Saltstairs' brick, through whatever the city was actually made of down there where nobody had ever built, the deep music held both its voices at once — the hand and the thing under the hand — and the walls of Ashkarad rang like struck glass, very softly, all night long, in a chord nobody but the girl at the wall could hear was waiting to resolve.
