# Chapter 35 — Harvest Marks

The Choirmaster had buried no one, because the Choratorium dead did not need burying; they needed dusting, and labels, and someone to keep the thieves out, and of those three duties he was equal to two.

He lived in the reliquary corridors now — had lived there since the night the city's forty-year chord died mid-note and the Church's cathedral-clinic became a carcass with a floor plan. He was half-starved by choice and schedule rather than necessity: there was food in the annexed halls below, Mother Gavel's people left leavings with the regularity of interest payments, and pride was a currency he had audited and found himself overdrawn in. He ate what the dead had eaten, which was nothing, and he kept the hours of the dead, which were all of them, and he tended the corridors the way a man tends the ledger of a house he burned down himself — not because the entries can be corrected, but because someone who can still read them has a duty to stand at the shelf.

The scavengers came at the lamp-less hours, mostly. Boys from the Hammers' side after the truce frayed, glass-hunters with pry-bars, one memorable pair of Sisters who had repented of the Chorus and looted it. He turned them all with the same instruments: a baritone voice kept in tune by forty years of harvest-floor command, a Chorus enforcer's bill-hook he had never once swung, and the corridors themselves, which did most of the work. People who enter a hall of dead glass carrying the expectation of treasure leave it carrying something else. He had watched the arithmetic happen on their faces, the revaluation, the entry made and then reversed. *Empty,* they thought. *No. Not empty,* the glass said. *Filed.*

Mother Gavel's people held the annexed halls below him and left him to his upper dark, having audited him once, early, through a delegation of two ledgermen who had climbed as far as the first reliquary rank and decided, on some creditor's instinct he respected, that the man in the ruins was already collectible enough. The Gavel wanted the city's fear on its books and not its guilt. He was a liability in the first column and an asset in no column, which in her arithmetic made him furniture. Furniture was left where it stood.

His right wrist had healed crooked. The cutter's grip had done it, on the night a boy named Elias had left the table alive; the joint clicked now on damp days like a lock testing itself, and the Choirmaster had come to use the click as a metronome for the slow work — dusting reliquary glass, righting the little brass name-plates, re-inking, with a stolen pencil, the labels that no one would ever read: *Kaelen. Elara. Elias.* Not the Church's labels. His. The Church's had said yield.

He was re-inking Elias's when he heard the boots, and knew them for careful boots, and put down the pencil.

· · ·

She came up the reliquary corridor with the lamp carried high and a courier two steps behind her, and the lamp-light ran along the brass of her mask, forehead to chin, and made of her face one smooth coin of metal. The Cartographer. Dead, the trade had said; unmasked, the trade had said; severed, the trade had said, her song-lobe cut out of the city's listening the way a tumor is cut. The trade said many things. The Cartographer walked his corridors at midnight with rubbings under her arm, which was a living person's errand.

"Surveyor's marks," she said, without greeting, laying them on the reliquary shelf one by one as if laying cards: chalk rubbings on cheap paper, lifted from beam-ends and door-frames, each a small clean glyph of circles and stems. "Drawn by a guest of my city. Free-handed, exact, and everywhere. I have forty-one of them from one district alone. I don't deal in what things say. I deal in what things are. Tell me what these are."

He looked. He was careful to look the way he had been trained to look, in order, the way he had taught initiates to audit a harvest district — routes first, yields second, gates third — and he got as far as the third rubbing before his hands went cold, and by the seventh he had sat down on a dead organ's plinth like a man whose margin call had arrived, and when he raised his eyes to the masked woman his face had gone grey as the dust.

"Where," he said, and his baritone came out a size smaller, "did your guest draw these."

"On our beams. Our stairs. Our doors. He asked permission for every one."

"He would." The Choirmaster heard his own voice do the thing it had done for forty years — go smooth, go level, go sweet as a graded hymn — and hated it, and let it, because the information deserved a steady instrument. "These are harvest-routing marks. Church-grade. The real grammar, the inner hand — not the street catechism, the *working* set." His crooked wrist clicked as he turned a rubbing toward the lamp. "This one is a route-mark: this lane carries wagons outbound, loaded. This is a yield-mark — see the stem with the two barbs? A count of souls by ward, expected yield, graded in the barbs. This is a gate-mark: here the wagons enter, here they are blooded, here they leave. And this—" the eighth rubbing, the one with the small clean flower inside two circles, "—is a claim-mark. Circled flower. Not a survey of what is. A plan for what will be."

"And in a working set," the Cartographer said, metallic, flat, "what does a full district of them make?"

"A master sheet." He said it the way a man names his own gallstone. "Every tithe I ever planned had a master sheet. The marks you can see are the field-copies — read them together, in their grammar, and they draw the master: one map, one hand, one round. Routes, yields, gates, claims." He looked at the rubbings the way he had once looked at chalk-boards of children's grades. "A city-wide tithe, Cartographer. Ward by ward, lane by lane, with the yields already graded. I drew plans like this for the Marrowworks district eleven times in forty years. I know my own hand's grandchildren when I see them. Your guest is doing a harvest."

The courier — the famous one, the counter, the woman with the ruined carbon arm and the splint gone hard as old debt — said nothing through all of it. She had gone to the shelf where the labels sat, and she was reading them. Kaelen. Elara. Elias. Her lips moved on the names without sound, pricing each one, and the Choirmaster watched her read and understood that of the three people in this corridor, she was the one most likely to kill him, and that she would be right.

"There's a second thing," he said. "Since you've come for inventory."

The reliquary archive lay two corridors down, and he walked them to it with the bill-hook as a walking staff and his heart going like a boy's at a grading. The archive was rank on rank of dead glass, reliquaries dark, the choir organs gone to silence and to dust-dull shine — the greatest choir Ashkarad had ever silenced, and he had tuned it — and behind the fourth rank, behind a false shelf he had built himself with a joiner's patience in another life, stood a lead-lined tithe-box with the Church's Debt-Stamp rusted on its hasp.

"When the order came down to burn the harvest-registry," he said, "I was the one holding the lamp-oil. The Index wanted ash; the Open Counts wanted ash with witnesses. It went into the braziers in the tithe-yard, every volume, and the fire was green and greedy, and I stood over it with a rake the way I stood over everything the Church ever made me tend." His wrist clicked. He opened the box. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth dry as wafers, lay a ledger the size of a paving stone. "I pulled one out. One. I have told myself for two years that I saved it for the courts, for the reckoning, for the day someone would come down here asking after names. That is a lie with good posture. I saved it because I could not be the last man who knew what we were *for*, and now the man who knows is—"

He stopped. Unwrapped it. The harvest-registry, unburned, whole: the vessel list entire, every name the Church had ever entered, grades and graft-numbers and resales, in the cramped patient script of clerks who had been him.

The courier stepped forward and looked down into the box, and her face did not change, and her face changed completely.

"Is mine in it," she said. Not a question, but he answered it as one, because a ledger-keeper pays debts of grammar too.

"Kaveh, Suri," he said. "Graft-number and page. Resold three times, per the transfer columns. I filed it twice — once to the debt, once to the stock." He closed the box, softly, the way one closes a coffin on someone still breathing. "It stays with me. I am not bargaining with it and I am not selling it and I will not hand it to a cutter, a warden, or a queen. When the reckoner comes to read it — and one is coming, Cartographer, your guest's marks say so — he reads it with me standing beside the shelf. I made the entries. I will render the account. That is the only penance Ashkarad has ever priced that I can actually pay."

The masked woman took out her charts then, and chalk, and began to work on the archive floor itself, right there among the dead glass — laying the rubbings out, writing in the field-marks, linking route to route the way she had once linked a dream's towers — and he watched a master sheet assemble itself on the stones of his own cathedral, drawn by a severed oracle in a brass mask from the grandchildren of his own grammar. It took her less than an hour. It had taken the Church's planning office, in his day, a season and six clerks, and the result had never once been so clean. The wards took shape. The lanes took shape. The yield-barbs clustered where the living lived thickest, and the route-marks ran inward from every quarter like veins running toward a heart.

And in the district where the marks ran cleanest and newest, where a free clinic stood over a cold cellar with a chalk ladder on its wall, the Cartographer chalked the claim-mark whole: a small five-petaled flower inside two circles, circled again for the master.

"Ashkarad's page," she said, metallic, flat, final, "being redrawn by hand."

The Choirmaster looked at it for a long time. Forty years ago a cutter's grip had not yet broken his wrist and a boy named Elias had not yet been welcome to the choir, and he had stood in a harvest amphitheater and asked a bleeding stranger his one true question, the question the Church had trained him never to ask and then trained him to ask only of others: *ask yourself what the city is for.* He had spent his life answering it in the only grammar he was given. The answer had filled these corridors. The answer was in the box.

"Ask yourself what the city is for," he said aloud, to the masked woman, to the counting courier, to the claim-mark drying on the stones. "I asked it of a cutter once, as a threat. Now I ask it of every wall in this district, as a warning. Your guest is surveying for the answer." He set his hand, crooked wrist clicking, flat on the closed box. "And I no longer believe he intends to like it."
