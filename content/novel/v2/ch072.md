# Chapter 72 — Inventory of the Dead

Triage is done by inventory. Ashkarad had always counted its worst nights that way — item by item, in the order the items could still be saved — and Dar worked the vault-mouth in the old grammar, sleeves rolled to the elbow, voice pitched flat and itemized, because a counted voice was the only lantern in the place that didn't gutter.

One courier, pick-butt across the shoulder blade, breathing clean; she counted her own fingers twice and made the total agree both times, which was the training now, and he passed her fit on her own figures. One Gavel miner with his palm opened to the bone by his own pick at the moment the singing started — splinted, billed for the thread, told to invoice his foreman and to lose the invoice. Two Index enforcers down at the second pillar: one with a grip that would never again close on anything softer than iron, one with a corridor's worth of re-tuned wrongness rattling in his hymn-trained blood, and to the second one Dar gave a clerk's diagnosis — not a fault of yours, rhythm interrupted — and watched the man decide to believe it, because a person will hold any ledger steady if you put something in his hands. The pickman whose pick had kissed the door sat where he had fallen with his hands flat on the floor, showing the stone they were empty, and when Dar knelt to look at his eyes the man said, in a whisper that had given up on being private, "I heard my own name in it. In one of the languages. It's a dead language and it knew my name." Dar noted the pupil response, noted the pulse, and wrote in his head, beside the man's trade and crew: exposed, once, to the interior — and left the line open, because there was nowhere yet to file it. Thirty freed donors, unwounded, hoarse, already moving through the gallery setting the emptied reliquary shelves to rights, because the Choirmaster's people kept the dead's house ready for singing even now — the Choirmaster himself among them, axe grounded, offering his ruined wrist for examination with the dignity of a man surrendering a monument, and declining treatment with the same breath. Fourteen stock children, accounted for. One storm-lantern, still lit.

The count was good. The count was clean. It was the kind of count a man could sign.

Then the crack exhaled.

It had been singing for the better part of an hour — the fracture branching hairline by hairline off the fifth petal of the flower, every new thread reciting the lullaby in a different dead language, soft as frost forming — and the cold came out of it the way owed money comes out of a hiding place: all at once, and exactly the amount. Not wind. A billow of vault-cold rolled up through the door's bright thread of nothing and into the antechamber, and the storm-lanterns went out one by one down the line of the wall, flinched dark, like candle-counters reaching the end of a column. The stone frosted. The air itself took on a grain, a texture of absolute collection — the cold of a place where nothing was ever spent, because nothing there had ever belonged to anyone.

The children were asleep against the antechamber wall when it came.

Mrs. Gable had raised children through three purges, and she knew the arithmetic of a bad night better than any clerk in the city. She did not scream and she did not run. She spread her coat over the smallest three, dragged the storm-lantern in under her body where its oil would keep, and folded herself over the pile of them — a wall of one old woman going up between the children and the deep with the speed of a thing that had been built many times before and torn down twice as often, elbows out, skirt spread to the last inch of its cloth, everything she owned arranged into a roof.

Dar came around the pillar in time to watch the frost take her breath. It went through her the way the Church's interest went through a debt — quietly, by the terms, on the compounding date. He inventoried her the way he had inventoried everything that night, pulse, pupils, the white climbing the veins of her wrists like a bill filling in its copies, and there was nothing in the inventory to operate on. The deep's cold was not a wound. It was a repossession. By the time he had her shoulders off the stone the billow had passed on and down, receded through the crack exactly as it had come — the deep bills, it does not gloat — and under her body the lantern was still burning.

"Count them," she said. Her voice was already mostly ledger, flat, itemized, going out of stock. "The children. Count them out for me."

He knelt and counted. Fourteen, all present, all breathing, the smallest three under her coat with the frost of her heartbeat still on the cloth. "Fourteen," he said. "All present."

"Good," said Mrs. Gable, who had brought every tenant of her stair through three purges and a war of ledgers without losing one to the cold, and filed herself.

Mother Gavel came down the freight way an hour before dawn with two lantern-men and the flowering pick carried upright between them like a legal exhibit, and she surveyed the cracked door's leaking cold the way she surveyed a defaulted district: at arm's length, in silence, without once touching the asset.

She held her bare hand up in the leaking air and let the frost write on it — grey petals forming across her scarred knuckles, all pointing down — and she read them like a document, turning the hand slowly as a clerk turns a page. Two hundred steps above her, in her counting-room, the pick she had confiscated stood in its corner with its grey bloom aimed at the floor, and the pickman who had swung its twin lay in her quarry infirmary counting backward from one hundred and nine, and his count had crossed below forty at the last threshold he was carried over, which meant the deep was billing steadily and the account was approaching delivery. Gavel recalculated. It took a long moment, and it cost her something visible: the flat ledger-page eyes narrowing by degrees, the way a rate narrows.

"A mine," she said at last, to no one, in the voice she used to close accounts, "is a hole with an exit." She looked at the crack, at the frost on her own hand, at the door that had been a door and was becoming a throat. "This is a hole with an entrance."

Her lantern-men turned their hoods toward Dar, waiting to see what the clinic would spend to change her figures. He had nothing to spend. The door was cracked, the cold was leaking, the singing was a lien with no maturity date, and somewhere on the dry seabed the wain was keeping its schedule; every number she held was already better than any number he held, and she knew it, and she let him know she knew it, by not once looking at him.

She ordered the withdrawal to the quarry without apology, apology not being a line item her books carried, and she filed the withdrawal correctly, too — not as retreat. "Order through debt," she said, turning to the stair, brass tube under her arm, "postponed." Her crews went up the freight ways with their picks grounded, orderly as a re-routed funeral, and the vault-mouth belonged to the clinic, the donors, and the dead.

The Index squad was already gone. The writ's hour had burned out at midnight, and conscription dies of its own paperwork on schedule; somewhere upstairs an auditor was already dating the re-file. They left the second pillar standing and the corridor humming with wrongness, and the only trace of them was one dropped glove, grey with frost, that nobody picked up.

Suri audited the cold itself before the shift changed, because unpriced weather was how the worst accounts opened. She held the clinic's one working pulse-reader in the leaking air at a body's width from the crack, and the machine, hand-cranked and honest, tried three times to file the reading and printed, on the third attempt, in its small stacked type: AMBIENT: COLLECTION IN PROGRESS. She chalked it into the night's column under EXPENDITURES — ENVIRONMENT: THE COLD, ONE OLD WOMAN, TOTAL — and ruled the line off with a straightedge, so that the figure could never quietly grow.

They carried Mrs. Gable up the 711 steps in relays of six, and the freed donors took the first shift, and the needlewoman who had been a soprano and was again carried the storm-lantern the whole way without once letting it swing, so that the flame would not be seen to bow in any direction at all.

The mourning was done the Ashkarad way, which is to say it was done in a book. There was no priest; the Church was a silence with a building on it. There was no hymn; the city had learned at last what hymns were for. There was a table, a lamp, and Suri opening the clinic's ledger to a fresh page with the flats of her thumbs, and the whole of the mourners' arithmetic going down in columns in her flat counted voice, because grief that is not filed is grief that can be repossessed:

GABLE — TENEMENT, SALTSTAIRS — DIED SHIELDING. ASSETS: FOURTEEN CHILDREN, ALL PRESENT. DEBTS: NONE.

Then her pen stopped, because the Ashkarad way was a line-item, a name kept, a debt recorded, and no form in the city had ever shown her the direction of that debt. She solved it the way she solved everything, by ruling a new column, and the rule of the pen was steady as a stair rail. OWED BY THE CLINIC: FOURTEEN CHILDHOODS. INTEREST: COMPOUNDING. TERMS: FOREVER. "We keep the name," she said, flat as a stamp. "Names are the one asset this city has never once debased."

Milla Ferun filed the death notice in her registrar's hand, ruled a line through the empty stamp-box at the bottom — no Heart-Stamp, no Debt-Stamp, no Voluntary-Stamp, just the clean strike of a clerk refusing a blank — and considered the space where the Church's forms had always wanted a cause of death in one word. Her pen hovered. She wrote THE COLD, and beneath it, smaller, where only another auditor would ever look: THE DEEP'S.

They buried her at the head of the Saltstairs, in the strip of cistern-garden she had kept like a boundary marker for thirty years, and the stock children stood in a row at the graveside in the order Mrs. Gable would have counted them, smallest first. The Choirmaster stood at the back with his hat over his axe. When the earth was done, one of the smaller children — a boy with perfect lungs and no idea yet what his lungs had been graded — said the only eulogy anyone dared: "She was like a grandmother to us."

Ilay, at the edge of the row with her chalk in her fist, went very still, in the way she went still when the walls said something worth writing down.

That night Dar went back down the shaft, because there was one more line in the inventory and he had not let himself count it.

The crack was still singing, though the languages were thinning now, a tongue at a time, like a choir going home. He stood at the door and put his eye to the four inches of bright nothing, and the deep breathed on the other side of the glass, slow, tidal, one beat behind his own heart and patient about it. DO NOT COUNT THE HEARTBEATS, the old margin said in his memory, because it counts back — and he counted anyway. He was owed one dishonesty, and he chose this one.

Through the crack, he counted the deep heartbeats, and got three.

Not three hearts. One heart, three chambers — each beat coming back in threes, beat, beat, beat, the way an invoice comes back in copies — vast, unhurried, and keeping its own books. He stood with his hand flat on the singing glass while the threes rolled under the city like patient freight, and beneath his counting hand, precise as a clerk correcting an underling, the deep counted back.

In threes.
