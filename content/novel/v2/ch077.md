# Chapter 77 — The Consensual Draft

The vote failed, and the city organized anyway, which was how Ashkarad had always done its accounting: the form denied, the substance filed anyway, in whatever book would hold it.

Suri saw the shape of it at dawn in the quarry's mouth, while the pit's chits were still being burned for warmth by the fog-sick. Elias had hummed four notes to a silent quarry and no one had stopped him, and the reason no one had stopped him was the reason the whole failed vote had already come apart: four notes of the true song, hummed by unclaimed blood, had done nothing. No hymn had woken, no reader had printed, no child had risen to the bait. The deep's own song, sung by consent, was the one music left in the world that did not bill the singer — and every soul in that pit had heard the nothing it cost.

Dar came down the tier while the torches were being doused, and he did not argue, and he did not articulate. Two days of articulating in doorways had taught him what the failure in the pit had taught the city: the third way could not be sold, because a thing that is sold is priced, and a thing that is priced is owned, and everything the Gardener's books touched became an entry. It could only be staffed. He struck the only three bargains the city had left on its books, and he struck them on the only terms Ashkarad had ever respected — work, price, and skill — and the striking took him the morning: Mother Gavel, for her part, got the water-works held and her crews' debts honored in any order that came after; Seft, for his, got the writ of conscription withdrawn by his own clerk's pen and the Index's readers pointed outward at the seabed instead of inward at the nursery; the Open Counts got the wall, and kept it. And the three mandates went out by noon, chalked under Elara's published terms where the whole city could audit them, and the city, which had failed nine votes short of saving itself, read the mandates and understood that the vote had been a form all along: Ashkarad had been assembling the third way for a year, one unpriced kindness at a time, and the only thing missing had been a book to hold it.

Suri took the counted.

She set the census up on the clinic stoop, because sanctuary had made the stoop the one ground in Ashkarad where names had never once been surrendered, and because Milla Ferun was already there with the registrar's table she had not stopped setting since the sister walked out of the sea-fog. Columns ruled in Vell's straightest chalk: the second copy going up on the counting-house wall as fast as the first went into the book, twin ledgers, no seal on either. Three columns, headed in the registrar's hand: SING. FIGHT. HIDE.

A census of the willing. That was the whole mechanism, and it was without precedent, and it worked by the one force the city's creditors had never once learned to collect against: people stood in line to be counted on purpose. The couriers signed SING first — forty of them by the second morning, Lena Vance's voice already carrying the word down the waterways — and the dockers signed FIGHT in blocks, whole crews with their foreman's mark first, and the tenement grandmothers signed HIDE with the competence of a trade, because hiding children through purges was a craft and they were its guild and they did not intend to be modest about it now. The lines crossed. The needlewoman signed SING and FIGHT. A Gavel ledgerman signed all three columns and Suri made him initial the overlap, because a mark in two columns was a promise twice and she collected promises twice. The freed vessels came with the guardians who had kept them through the purges, and signed SING in a cluster with their lungs' grades unread, because the census did not carry a stock column and nobody proposed one. Vell of Brack chalked her own consent on the wall-copy where a signature would go — a small tally-mark, one stroke, the first mark she had made for herself since her city stopped existing — and Suri entered it in the book as a name with no sound in it, spelled V-E-L-L, and moved down the line. Nobody was drafted. Nobody was asked twice. The Gavel crews signed as a block behind Mother Gavel's own mark, which she set down first, at the head of her crews' page, where a signature belongs.

By the third day the book had a body count of the living: two thousand one hundred and some counted, and the count not slowing, and Suri ruling the totals at each dusk with the straightedge, and Milla ruling beneath them the line the census had existed to make true: COUNTED — BY CONSENT — FOR ASHKARAD.

Vahid took the makers.

He took them the way he took everything, by lecture, assembling them in the cold cellar under the clinic — glaziers and chandlers, tallow-boilers and a lamp-oil refiner, four apothecaries who had kept their trade through the Chorus years by selling it quietly — and giving them the only inheritance he had left: Shirin Bahar's protocol, spoken aloud over her own cylinders, the chorus-free medicine rendered from the silences in a harvested woman's voice, scaled at last past six vials and one saved boy.

"City scale," he told them, and his wire-mended spectacles caught the still-light, and his one hand drew the runs of the apparatus in the air like a man conducting a foundry. "Which does not mean more. It means ordinary. The dose stops being a jewel and becomes a utility, like water, like lamp-oil; it is drawn, it is stored, it is rationed by the census. We are not brewing medicine any longer. We are bottling an absence, and the absence is the point."

They built it in three days. Still on still, coil on coil, the Choratorium's salvaged glassware traded back from the Gavel's salvage-crews at rates the Gavel's books called generous and the Gavel's face called nothing at all, because Mother Gavel had read the fleet's provisions clause and knew exactly what unrationed water was worth in six weeks. The cylinders played in rotation on the brass spindle, Shirin's voice running the numbers of her own silences into the batch, and the vials came off the line in ranks — hundreds the first week, plaited into straw baskets like lamp-oil, staged at the census's SING tables so that every willing throat would know where its rest was drawn from.

And the smoke came after the medicine, because the smoke was older. The Vigil's recipe — carried home in the party's packs from the walking city, the Silent Order's one gift that could be given without silence being broken — for a grey ash that ate resonance. Vahid's people built the censers in the Chandler's Row shops: squat iron pots on chains, smudge-fire packed with the ash of burned hymn-pages and lead-dust and salt, the Vigil's proportions, the Vigil's patience. One censer gave a room a rest. Ten gave a street one. The crews drilled the hanging of them on the clinic's roof until the smoke stood over the Saltstairs in a low grey lid of its own, and Ilay, hearing it from the shaft head, signed to Roya's slate the closest thing to approval the deep had ever offered the city: it sounds like snow.

Roya took what cannot be mapped.

Not the city; the city was mapped, was over-mapped, was chalked on walls in three hands. She took the deep vaults — the region no surveyor's instrument had ever honestly seen, the salt vaults beneath the Throne shaft where the lock-seals sat in their century of dark and the cracked door sang its dead languages into the cold — and she mapped them the only way left to her, which was the way she had mapped a battle in the dark: through Ilay's hearing, live, chalk in one hand, the girl's voice in her ear like a cable.

They worked from the listening-post niche and then below it, where DO NOT COUNT THE HEARTBEATS was scored into the old stone in its ledger-script that no one obeyed anymore because everyone was counting everything now. Ilay lay on the rock with her palms down and called the vaults the way soundings are called off a hull: seam, seam, pool of cold, seam. Roya chalked. Beneath the girl's hearing, all the while, the Sister kept her post — the pedal-tone, one beat behind the city's heart, holding her harmony under the numbers like a hand under a falling ledger — and Roya had learned to chart her too: the calm rooms where the pedal-tone rose, the rooms where it pressed down, the one corridor where it thinned to almost nothing, which Roya circled and labeled in her blunt capitals, DO NOT POST THE FLEET HERE, without knowing why she was sure, and trusting the surety, because for a year now her surest instrument had been a child's ears and her second-surest was an exile's instinct that had never once been wrong about where the danger stood. What came up under her hand was not a map as the city understood maps — it was a score. The vaults' architecture was acoustic to its bones: every gallery a resonator, every corridor a pipe, every lock-seal a node where the deep's rhythm came up through the salt like water through a wall. She charted the leak — the cracked door's cold, its tidal range, the rooms it reached at slack and at flow. She charted the crack's languages by gallery, thinning east, thickening west, and marked where they overlapped into unison, because unison was load. And on the master sheet, in her blunt surveyor's capitals, she chalked the one finding the whole draft had been for: STANDING ROOM — WHERE A CHORD CAN BE HELD — and under it, circled, the antechamber above the door, and the reliquary gallery, and the long stair, and the pit.

The fleet wanted the children as tribute and the lid as collateral, and it had posted its terms in the city's own generous grammar. Roya chalked the city a different grammar — a survey of its own basement, hung on the counting-house wall beside Elara's published terms where anyone could read it — because her doctrine had never changed either: the unmappable is only unmapped, and the bill for leaving it so was already in the mail.

On the fourth evening the census's first page came up out of the book for certification — the signature page, the page that made the willing binding on the willing — and Milla laid it flat on the registrar's table, and the room of counters went quiet, because the first line of the first page of the count had been held blank all week.

Suri knew whose it was. She had been saving the line like the last unspent coin of a dead currency.

Milla ruled the heading first, in her registrar's hand, the only heading the signature would ever need: ASHKARAD — COUNTED. Then Ilay stepped up, last, in front of everyone, and she did not sign her name, because she had never once needed one to be exact. She took the chalk and drew, in the careful pressure she used for maps, a flower: nine petals — nine, and everyone in the room could count, and everyone did — and every petal connected to every other, point to point, a lattice that was also a bloom that was also, Roya saw from the back of the room, exactly the pattern the frost had made in the rig where two pulses had crossed.

Milla certified the mark with the registrar's hand, no stamp, and Suri ruled the page into the book, and the book took the weight of two thousand signatures and one flower without a sound.

The chord. Drawn by a child, on the first page of the first census in the world's history taken by consent.
