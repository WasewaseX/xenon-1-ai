# Chapter 60 — Flag of Truce, Black Petal

An open door is never free. Dar had learned that in a cellar full of storm-dark with a god's seed unspooling in his chest, and the deep confirmed it now, on the third night of the watch at the shaft mouth, where the door of salt-glass stood wide open at the bottom of seven hundred and eleven steps and nothing had come through it. Nothing was not the same as nothing owed. Above, the city went about its arithmetic — bells, queues, tally-boards, the annexed Choratorium's new flags — and every column in every ledger in Ashkarad ended, one way or another, at the line none of them had priced: something enormous stood at their threshold, politely, the way a creditor stands, waiting to be answered.

The watch was Roya's rule — someone holds the top — and she kept it from a camp stool with Ilay cross-legged at her knee, chalk in hand, translating. Dar took the hours between the fourth bell and the first, because he slept badly in the deep places and saw no profit in pretending otherwise. Suri counted the watches the way she counted everything, out loud, flatly, as if the numbers were a wall she was building one brick at a time.

Three days, and the city had done what Ashkarad always did with an unprecedented event: it had filed it in triplicate. Mother Gavel's crews held the shaft head now by arrangement, four enforcers and a ledgerman, their protection dues suspended by verbal treaty and their eyes very busy; the Choirmaster had gone into the Church's own doctrine with a lamp and come out grey, reporting that forty years of lid-maintenance theology contained no column at all for a lid asking its jailers for protection; the Index had opened a file, which was how the Index hugged. Only the clinic treated the open door as what it was — a patient in the hallway, breathing warm loam-scented air up seven hundred and eleven steps, asking for something no one in the building knew how to chart.

The petal came up on the third night, at the hour between bells when the shaft's breath runs warmest.

He felt the air change before he saw anything — a slow displacement that smelled, absurdly, of loam, of turned garden soil, of a summer no one in Ashkarad had ever been invoiced — and then the lantern-light caught it: a single black petal rising out of the dark the way a bubble rises through oil. Unhurried. Deliberate. It climbed the whole seven hundred and eleven steps of the shaft the way a flag goes up a pole, cleared the lip of the mouth, and did not stop until it hung in the air before Dar's chest, level with the two hearts beating there, and waited.

"Flag of truce," Roya said, metallic and quiet. "That's a flag of truce, cutter. Someone taught her court manners down there."

The petal was warm. It settled onto his open palm with the weight of a coin and tapped — once, twice — the opening bars of the lullaby, then a pattern he did not know: three soft beats and a rest, repeated, patient, a question shaped in touch. In his pocket Tally's petal-token went warm against his thigh and leaned, compass-true, toward the open door below.

Isra came up the stair when sent for. She read the rhythm on his palm the way Roya read charts, her whole body bent over it, salt-burned fingers hovering a hair's width off his skin; the black petal spread at her collarbone opened a little wider, the way a flower opens at dawn it cannot see. Then she took her slate and wrote, slowly, the chalk pressed whiter with effort:

I ASK ASYLUM.

Under it, after a minute, the chalk moving like a woman wading upstream:

FROM MY MOTHER.

And under that, in strokes that had cost her something to make:

I DO NOT INVADE. I DEFECT.

Asylum. Dar held the word up against his ledger and found no shelf for it. Ashkarad priced shelter by the week, sanctuary by the favor, and citizenship by the blood you kept on deposit; but asylum — protection granted to a defection — was a category the city had never once needed, because nothing had ever tried to defect to them before. Things fled Ashkarad. Nothing, in the city's recorded memory, had ever fled anything else, to Ashkarad.

"She's a century old," he said, mostly to himself. "And she's doing the one thing no one down there has ever done. Changing sides."

The runners went out before the chalk dust settled — to the quarry, to the annexed Choratorium, to the Index's counting-floor by the seawalls — and Dar stood at the shaft mouth holding a flag of truce in his hand and listened to the city prepare its answers, none of which were his. Mother Gavel would toll the door. The Index would file the Sister under a page — vessel registry, page nine, the page their machines kept asking for — and call asylum an unaudited presence and bill it accordingly. The Open Counts would declare her welcome from the rooftops and then splinter over the wording, because a declaration is a document and every document in Ashkarad is a contract, and contracts are where the feeding starts.

"Nobody answers a door for free," Suri said beside him, watching the runners' lanterns braid away down the Marrowworks hill. "We'd be the first city in recorded history to return a refugee's deposit."

"She isn't asking us to open it," Dar said. "She's asking us not to sell her."

"Same ledger," Suri said. "Different column."

The Choirmaster's answer came back first, by a boy runner still out of breath, in the baritone the man had for writing as well as for speaking: the doctrine is silent; the doctrine was built by people who assumed the lid could never want anything; convene the council before the factions convene without it. Gavel's answer came back second, in the form of four more enforcers and a second ledgerman, which in the grammar of the Gavel was a complete sentence. The Index sent nothing at all, which Suri, watching the seawalls, liked least of all. "When they've got nothing to say," she said, "it's because they've already said it to each other."

He did not answer, because the answer had to be one word — yes, no, or the Ashkarad special, a price — and every faction in the city owned a different word, and he was still counting the cost of each when the waterways began to scream.

It came up through the floor first, through the cistern-ropes and drain-grates under the Saltstairs, a sound with a human shape stretched over it like canvas over a frame: Lena Vance's voice, the voice the Church had kept six years in glass and the First Singer's death-song had given back to her, pitched now past singing into something older than singing. The freed voice ran down every waterway at once — the drainage canals, the Undergleam tunnels, the flooded seams of the canal-works — and everywhere it ran it carried the same three words, over and over, in a register that made the lantern-glass ring and Ilay cover one ear without waking:

"GLOVES IN THE CANALS. GLOVES IN THE CANALS. THE OLD WORKS — THEY CARRY THE BOOK —"

The couriers' relay confirmed it inside a quarter hour, arriving in pieces the way bad news always does, cheapest first. A team had come up the old canal-works from the dry side of the tide-gates — six of them, walking the drained canal bed in single file through the fog the vanished tide had left standing in the harbor like a held breath. No lanterns. No bearers. No barge. White gloves, dark coats, correct as a filed return; and they had come ashore at the water-stair where the Gavel kept its customs post, because six weeks after the sea went away the customs post was still there, still manned, still charging, the end of the ocean not being an acceptable reason in Ashkarad to close a revenue window.

The Gavel crews barred the way with pikes and a fee schedule, and the sergeant of the watch — Mother Gavel's own sister's boy, a man who had personally collected protection dues from every stall on the landing since he was fourteen — stepped into their path and named his number, the way he named it forty times a day, a number with a pike behind it and a pit behind the pike.

The lead herald had stopped. Had reached into its coat, unhurried, correct, and brought out a book — a tall ledger chained at the wrist, salt-scuffed, travel-stained, its clasp worn bright by handling — and opened it to the first page and held it up at arm's length so the whole water-stair could read it by lantern-light.

Then it had closed the book, stepped around the sergeant the way you step around furniture, and walked on. All six. Toward the Marrowworks underbelly. Toward the vault approaches. Nobody had touched them. Nobody had priced the fee. The sergeant was still standing where they had left him, the runner said, doing the arithmetic of a man who has just watched his own ledger fail to total.

"They didn't jump the toll," Roya said, when the report reached the shaft mouth. "They audited it. And they let him see the book on purpose. That page was addressed to him — to the whole pit of them. Show the gate what happened to the last gate."

Dar heard the report at the shaft mouth with the petal still in his hand. Warm. Patient. Tapping its three-soft-beats-and-a-rest against his palm like a clerk waiting for a stamp. He thought of Brack's counting-house, of a tongueless clerk chalking herself into the death column to survive it, of the line scored across a wall in a dead city, and he understood the book the way the sergeant had understood it: the first page was not history. It was a precedent. Everything after it was pricing.

"It showed the book," Suri said, when the runner finished. "At a toll gate. It doesn't need to fight us, Mehran. It showed us the receipt for the city before ours."

The wain's tally-book. The round's account, carried ahead of the wain itself the way a bill of lading travels ahead of the cargo. And its first line — its opening entry, stamped before ever the team reached Ashkarad, already old, already paid — was a city two entries up the route, a city of counting-houses and water-marks that had kept its books to the end:

BRACK — PRUNED, PAID.

Dar folded the petal into his coat, into the inner pocket where Vey's registry page lay folded next to Milla's printout, the archive of everything the city had not yet answered, and felt it go still against the paper like a bird settling. The council would convene on the asylum question by morning; the factions were already drafting their terms; and it no longer mattered, because the answer had been overtaken in transit, the way answers in Ashkarad always were. The city could grant the Sister asylum, or refuse it, or bill it — and the tally-book would go on up the route regardless, page by page, stamp by stamp, an account no vote had ever been able to close.

Below his boots, at the bottom of seven hundred and eleven steps, the door stood open and the Sister stood at the threshold of it, neither in nor out, waiting on the verdict of a city that billed everything it touched — and had not yet found a column for mercy.

Above the lanes, thin as wire, Lena Vance's voice was still running the waterways, following six white gloves down through the dark of the old works, repeating the only warning Ashkarad had left that nobody owned:

"GLOVES IN THE CANALS. THEY CARRY THE BOOK. THEY CARRY THE BOOK."
