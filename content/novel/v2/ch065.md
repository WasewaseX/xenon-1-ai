# Chapter 65 — The Gavel Ration

The sea had been gone six weeks, and Ashkarad learned what it had never once priced: that the ocean had been doing the city's water-work for free.

The fog-sea had not come back with the tide. It stood out past the seawalls where the horizon should have been, a grey absence with a curve to it, and the dry harbor breathed at night like a thing with a collapsed lung, and every salt-storm since had come inland off mud instead of water, tasting of iodine and old debt. The wells were the only water left in the world now, and they were dying at a measurable rate.

Mother Gavel seized the pump-works on a Tuesday, in the manner of all her acquisitions, which was to say the gear-houses changed their flags between one shift and the next and the crews who came off shift found their wages already renegotiated. The canal-works pumps, the deep cistern-wells, the chain of reservoirs under the Saltstairs — all of it had been straining against a sea that was not there, hauling from drop-wells the city had never needed to drill, and the Gavel's ledgermen had done the only honest arithmetic left in Ashkarad and posted it over the pump-houses in letters a hand high: WATER FOR WORK.

The ration worked like this. A day's pumping, hauling, bracing, or well-cutting bought a household's measure — a counted bucket-line at dusk, stamped chits, no exceptions, no credit. The Gavel's brass speaking-tube read the schedule from the pit-head at each dawn: which districts pumped, which cisterns were open, what the day's water-price was in hours. It was extortion with a flow-rate. It was also — and Suri hated this the way you hate a rival's good bookkeeping — correct. She went out to see it run on the fourth morning, because numbers you have not stood in front of are rumors, and she stood in a queue of sixty households at the Tanner's Walk cistern while a Gavel lance-corporal counted buckets with a chalked tally-board and two enforcers held the line with their arms folded and their faces empty, and the queue was silent, and orderly, and fed. Nobody pushed. Nobody begged. The ration had done to the crisis what the crisis had done to the sea: taken the uncertainty out of it. A queue you can count is a queue that holds, and the Gavel had understood, before any hall of government in this city, that fear is mostly an accounting problem.

Suri audited the ration's third week out of professional grief and found the numbers cruel and true. The well-yields balanced against the bucket-lines to the bucket. There was no skim. There was not, in the whole apparatus, a single unpriced kindness to exploit, and she wrote in her ledger, under GAVEL — PUMP-WORKS: ADMIRED (PROFESSIONALLY) / DESPISED (PERSONALLY), the note that the most dangerous government was the one whose arithmetic you could not fault.

The Index's demand came the same week, by writ, under seal, in Auditor Seft's rebuilt bureaucratic hand.

The Index wanted the stock children. Conscripted sonar, the writ called them — a listening-post of living instruments, *for the city's safety, for the duration of the emergency* — because the hymn-training the Church had poured into their blood was, the writ observed with its horrible frankness, the only sensor array in Ashkarad that could hear the heralds coming and the deep beneath them, and it was wasteful, in an emergency, to leave an asset sleeping in a clinic. The writ requested transfer of eleven vessels and one Ilay, GRADE: PERFECT, to the Index counting-floor by the seawalls, where pulse-reader cradles were already being built to spec around listening-children's beds. Page nine again. The Index had been hunting that page since before the fire, and the fire had taught it that when paper failed, you went to the flesh.

Dar's answer to the writ was two words and a signature, and the two words were *They're patients*, and the signature was the clinic's, and Suri watched Seft's clerk receive the refusal, stamp it RECEIVED, NOT COMPLIED, and file it — and understood that in the Index's grammar that was not a defeat but a docket. The writ's runner, a boy with the Index's blank rectangle tarred on his sleeve, asked, in the flat courtesy of his masters, whether the clinic would care to file an objection in triplicate, and Suri told him the clinic filed everything in one copy and kept the original, and the boy wrote that down too.

The Open Counts splintered the same fortnight, publicly, over the only question three kinds of clerk can splinter over: whose count was true. The original Open Counts — the reform census that had counted the city openly after the halls burned — broke into three named factions over the deep rhythm the pulse-readers had printed. One faction held the printouts were contamination and every count since the sixth bell was void. One held the rhythm was a new variable that had to be counted *into* every census going forward, door by door, breath by breath. The third simply walked out of the counting-house with the master tallies under their coats and became, by nightfall, a rumor, and by the following week a bounty, and the city's last public arithmetic thus acquired a black market. Three censuses now circulated where one had stood, each sworn, each sealed, each true from inside its own premises, and the only question the street could not answer was the one that mattered: when a count was a weapon, who got to hold the pen. The lane outside the registry saw two counts' worth of clerks fighting over a tally-board like gulls over a dropped fish, and the city watched its last neutral instrument — arithmetic itself — descend into the same factional ledger-war as everything else, and the clinic, watching the water rise around it, did what islands do.

It declared itself neutral ground, and posted the terms on the door.

The sanctuary census was Suri's construction, and she built it the way she built everything, out of the materials the city had left her. Every soul under the clinic's roof and in its yard-cots — patients, couriers, the stock children home from the deep counting-rooms, Isra, Vell, the staff — counted once, openly, by name, in a census posted where anyone could read it. Milla Ferun as registrar: the woman who had spent years stamping other people's debts into other people's ledgers now kept the count of the living, in her fair clerk's hand, and signed each page like a woman signing a confession she was proud of. Vell of Brack chalked the second copy on the yard wall, because in Brack you never trusted a count to one surface, and a clerk who had survived the end of her city by chalking herself into a death column had opinions about redundancy that Suri honored like scripture.

Counted once. Counted openly. Counted by name. The census was a shield, and both factions' seals went on it — the Gavel's hammer and the Index's blank rectangle — because the terms of neutrality required the books to be open, and Suri, who had spent her life in closed books, was betting her entire remaining stock that open books, in Ashkarad, could be armor.

The dispersal was Dar's addition, and it was the part the census-takers argued about. The clinic's sensitive holdings — Vey's registry page, the tally-book from the reliquary corridor, Milla's printout, the evidence of everything they had survived — could not sit in one building that both factions now had the right to audit. So the archive was distributed, deliberately, into the keeping of the people whose business it had always been. Dar gave Milla her printout back at the registrar's desk, in front of witnesses, and the room went quiet, because everyone in it knew what the slip was and what it had cost her. "Evidence keeps best with its witness," Dar said, in his triage voice, folding the slip into her coat lining where it had lived for years before any of them. "You're the registrar. It's provenance." Milla took it with both hands and did not say anything, and the salt under her fingernails, Suri noticed, had never quite gone away.

The audit was the price, and the price came due on the sixth day.

Two of the Gavel's ledgermen arrived with the morning bucket-line, polite as invoices, and Suri sat them at the long table and gave them the census and the water-accounts and the treatment-tallies, everything, because the terms were the terms. They were good. She marked them good the way she marked everything — the senior one turned a page with a wetted thumb and never lost his place, and his junior recited sums back unasked — and she was beginning to believe the week might total clean when the senior ledgerman opened the last item in his audit-bag, and the room's arithmetic changed.

It was a book. A Chorus vital-debt ledger — one of the master volumes, salt-stained, fire-licked at one corner, salvage-stamped on the spine in the Gavel's receiving mark. The wrecking crews clearing the burned census halls had sold the surviving Chorus paper off by weight, Suri knew; the Gavel had bought a lot of it for pennies, the way the Gavel bought everything the city stopped guarding. The ledgerman laid it on the table and said, without ceremony, "Exemption audit. Property schedule. We check the sanctuary against the debt-books, miss. Nobody neutral by accident."

He turned the pages with his wetted thumb. Suri sat still and counted her own pulse, because there was nothing else in the room to do with her hands, and the pages came around like weather, and the ledgerman stopped, and read, and did not look up.

"Kaveh, Suri," he read aloud, flat as his master's tube. "Carbon-scaled, registry number attached. Debt resold — three times. Original holder, the Chorus. Final assignment of record..." He turned the book a quarter-circle so she could see, and there it was in the assignment column, in forty-year-old ink, the whole history of a skin she had sold at nineteen arriving like a tide at the top of a wall: the chain of resales running down the margin, transfer, transfer, transfer — and the last holder of record, stamped in the receiving mark she had watched go up over the pump-houses three weeks ago.

The Gavel.

For a moment the room's accounting held its breath. The junior ledgerman looked at the page, then at her arm — at the carbon scales sheathing it, the receipt she had carried since nineteen, ruined and regrown and always, always somebody's paperwork — and looked away again with the practiced blankness of a man who has learned where the eyes go when the books stop being abstract.

The ledgerman drew a chalk circle around her name on his audit-slate, unhurried, correct, and said, "Your exemption's fine, miss. Your person, though — that's an asset on our schedule. We'll want the paperwork reconciled." And he passed her the book, page open, the way you pass a man his own death notice, because in Ashkarad even mercy was documented.

Mother Gavel came to the door herself at dusk — the pit must have been told by runner within the hour — and stood in it a moment with her scarred roadmap of a face and her eyes flat as ledger pages, and looked at Suri sitting at her own long table with a Chorus debt-book open in front of her and her whole ledger-discipline arriving, beat by beat, at the sum.

"Sanctuary keeps names in," Mother Gavel said, through the brass tube, conversational, almost kind. "It's never once kept a debt out. I'll send terms."

Suri looked down at the page, at the registry number at the top of it — the number the Church had filed on her skin before she was old enough to read — and then at her own ledger, her true one, the one the system had never been able to repossess. She opened a new column, and her pencil did not shake, because the counting was hers, and it was the last thing on earth that was.

She ruled the line and wrote the heading, and the heading sat on the fresh page like a claim being staked, like a lien filing itself:

THE GAVEL — OWNS — ME?
