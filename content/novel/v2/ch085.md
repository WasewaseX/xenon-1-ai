# Chapter 85 — Burial as Gift

The war-room split along the only seam the city had ever split along: what to do with a thing that sang.

"Destroy it," Vahid said, and he had the chalk, so the word went up on the board in his crabbed lecture-hand, underlined twice. "It is an antenna wearing a heart's clothing. The Vigil taught us the recipe — ash, lime, a fire that is fed nothing it can sing to. I built this strain's grandfather in a room like this one. I am telling you as its maker's ghost: some instruments are only ever retired by fire."

"Cage it," said Mother Gavel's runner, reading from a slate in the boss's own flat hand, because Gavel did not climb stairs for anyone, even now, even for this. "*An asset that sings is an asset. Lead box, quarry vault, my ground, my guards. The clinic holds the key or I do. Terms negotiable.*" The runner set the slate on the table like a deposit.

"It sings in threes," Roya said, from the corner, her brass mask catching what little light the hooded lamps allowed. "The deep's meter. Put it in a lead box and it will teach the box the deep's meter, and the box will teach the floor, and the floor will teach the quarry. Everything the deep touches learns to count. You held the pick, Mother. You know what comes back from the mine."

"There's a third column," Suri said. She had been ruling it while the others talked, because ruling columns was what she did while other people had opinions. "The clinic's old trick. It was Hesh's antenna once, and we fed it a rehearsed rhythm for weeks and the lie traveled up the road and sprang him. Cage it *singing.* Feed it a false count until the trigger closes on nothing."

"The trigger is feeding," Vahid said. "It's set to the deep's beat, and the deep keeps time one beat behind the boy. You'd be filing false entries in a book the *Root* audits. The last man who taught the deep a lie was a pickman who cannot stop counting backward from one hundred and nine." He took his spectacles off, which he only did when a finding had frightened him. "And there is a worse column nobody has priced. The composite voice in it. The lullaby. Whatever taught that heart to sing has all the music it bought off this clinic's wall, and if we cage the heart, we leave the Gardener's instrument on the board where she can reach it. Fire is the only ledger that closes."

Into the middle of it, uninvited, came the sound of chalk on the window sill.

Ilay was at the wall — she had been at the wall for most of the argument, palms flat to the stone the way she had pressed her hands to the world since she was nine years old, listening down through the brick to the two circles under the city — and the music she was hearing had changed its mind about something. Roya saw it first, the way she saw everything first now, by watching the girl instead of the stone. Ilay's chalk was moving. Her face was the small scowl she wore when the walls said something they shouldn't, and her hand was writing at dictation speed.

Roya read it aloud, because Roya had spent a year learning to be the girl's voice for rooms the girl could not see.

"LET HER EAT IT," she read, and the war-room went quiet the way the vault had gone quiet when the door first sang. "THE FIRST SEED REMEMBERS EVERY HEART. SHE WILL KEEP YOURS SAFE BELOW."

"The counsel of the defecting party," Vahid said at last, very dry, very carefully. "Received from a locked room under the world, through the girl. Forgive me if I price this slowly. The first seed is the Gardener's eldest. She was made by the same hand, seeded from the same flesh, kept by the same books. We are asked to hand the enemy's eldest sister the enemy's bomb and call it a burial."

"We're asked to give her the collateral," Dar said. "Her word for it is *keeping.*"

"Her word is *eat,*" Vahid said.

"Both things are true down there," Isra said.

She had not written on the slate in an hour — writing cost her warmth, letter by letter, and she hoarded her heat like a diver hoards air — and when she did write, she wrote slowly, and Suri read it out flat: *THE FIRST SEED KEEPS WHAT IS GIVEN. IT IS THE ONLY LAW SHE WAS LEFT. ASK HER WHAT SHE DID WITH MINE.*

Nobody in the room could answer that. Isra's arrangement with the deep was her own; what she had given below, she had given below. But the slate sat there on the table with its question ruling the silence, and the silence, when the vote finally came around, ruled the vote.

"Sanctuary terms," Dar said. "That's the whole of the law we hold. She petitioned for asylum and the city has never answered her, and she has kept the door shut from her side for a year, and asked for nothing, and the one time she asks for anything it is for the safekeeping of a piece of me. I've been a debtor in this city my whole life. I know a keeper's terms when they're offered. We give her the heart."

Vahid looked at him for a long moment over the rim of his spectacles. "Then I'm carrying it down," he said. "I made its grandfather. I'll not have it go to the grave in a courier's satchel like a parcel. If this is a burial, it will be a burial with a next of kin present."

Seven hundred and eleven steps, and Suri counted every one of them, because counting was how she held a hand in the dark.

Roya and Ilay took the shaft head — Roya because her maps owned the vertical, Ilay because someone had to hear what came back up. The party below was four: Dar, Isra, Suri, Vahid with the glass case against his chest, carried slung like a sick man's child, both latches wired shut. They went past the dead First Vessel's cold cathedral of flesh, and Vahid — who had a lifetime of reasons not to look at it, and looked anyway — said nothing the whole length of it, and the walls rang like struck glass, and the temperature fell the way it always fell, as if the descent were being paid for out of the air's own account.

The salt-glass door stood wide open, as it had stood for a year. The hairline crack off the flower-keyhole still threaded the pane like a fault in ice, and it still sang, faintly, the four notes over and over in their different dead languages, a sound none of them would ever call music again without flinching. And behind the glass, in the dark the lamps had never once been allowed to enter, the first seed waited. She had never crossed. Not at the asylum petition, not through the faction war, not when the city cracked itself open at the vault-mouth three armies deep. She stood at her own threshold the way the wain stood at the tide-gates: patient, punctual, and outside.

Dar knelt at the door.

The case was heavy in his hands — heavier than a heart, lighter than what it had been primed to do — and he set it on the threshold stone with the latches facing her, the way you set a settled account facing the teller.

"It sings in threes," he said. "It's keyed to the deep's rhythm, and it's primed to sing the claiming-song through the water the next time the ninth feeds. She built it out of everything I sold her, and she sent it back as a courtesy." He turned the case so the frost-furred glass caught the last of the lamplight from above. "You said you'd keep it. So keep it. Buried, burned, or broken — it stops being a weapon tonight, and it becomes whatever you decide to file it under."

The dark reached out.

Not a hand — the city had learned its lesson about hands — but something in the grammar of one: a limb of root and petal, black vascular lace drawn out of the deep's own darkness the way a signature is drawn out of ink, and it took the case the way the auditors had taken it, with both prongs, carefully, the way you take something you have read the receipt for. The begging voice inside the glass — the composite, the forged mother, four notes of it always rising before anyone could stop their ears — changed. Dar felt the change land in his own ribs before he heard it leave the case: the three-count unwinding, beat by beat, the backward fall easing, the primed verse going slack in the muscle like a fuse pin drawn. One beat behind his heart. Then with his heart. Then kept — held in a rhythm that was not counting at all, only holding, the way a hand holds what it has been given.

"The first seed remembers every heart," Isra wrote, and held the slate where the lamplight found it, "AND SHE HAS NOT HAD ONE TO KEEP IN A HUNDRED YEARS."

They carried it down. That was the part Dar had not known how to imagine until he heard it — the sound of the vault floor taking the weight, a slow grinding of salt on salt, patient as grave-digging because it was grave-digging, down and away from the door, along a passage no one had ever mapped, toward the one chamber in the deep the Sister had asked Ilay's maps to leave unmarked: the cold place where the daughter lay. The Gardener's daughter. The first vessel. Dead since the Blacktide's death-song and cold since before that, a cathedral of flesh with every eye closed, and beside her — grave-goods, an offering, a key — the original heart of Darius Mehran, planted in the dark like a bulb.

The Lily stood witness. Dar felt her do it — felt her come to the front of his chest and lean, hard, toward the deep, her hum dropping to the lowest register she owned, and then — he would spend years deciding whether he had felt this or invented it — settling, key by key, into the Sister's own line. Two songs fitting together, the way they had fitted at a pane of salt-glass a year ago, four notes apiece. Sisters dividing an estate: one got the grave, one got the carrier, and neither of them, as far as he could tell, had been consulted about the will.

"Grave-goods," Vahid said quietly, watching the dark where the case had gone. "And a pass-key, if I know anything about locks and grief. She hasn't defused it, boy. She's *re-keyed* it. From trigger to talisman. The next time anything opens that door for true, it will find a heart in the ground that was told *keep this one safe* — by the one creature in creation the Gardener's books never held." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, one-handed, badly. "The bomb is defused. I want it minuted that I recommended fire, and that I have rarely been so pleased to be voted down."

They came up the shaft in silence, four of them plus the absence of a case, and the silence held all the way to the listening-post at the head, where Roya waited with her slate and Ilay waited with her hands pressed to the stone and her chalk already out.

Because the deep was talking.

Not the four notes. Not the meter. Words — Ilay's scowl said words, her hand said words, and Roya read them up the shaft to the climbers as they arrived, her voice flat and careful in the ringing dark:

"NOW I OWE YOU," Roya read, "I HATE OWING."

The first unprompted sentence the Sister had ever sent the surface. A year of asylum petitions and testimony by transcript, and this — not thanks, not grief, not the century's ledger of the dead — this was the entry she chose to file, through the girl, to a man still catching his breath at the top of seven hundred and eleven steps.

Ilay looked up from the chalk. She had one more line to add, her own, small letters under the transcription, and she wrote it and turned the slate around without being asked, because some columns a child audits better than a surgeon.

"owing is a debt," the slate said. "she pays her debts. it just takes her a hundred years to open the account."
