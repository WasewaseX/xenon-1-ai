# Chapter 91 — The True Books

The Lily's sentence had no column. Suri had tried three.

BOTH, OR NEITHER — she wrote it at the top of a fresh page in her own ledger, because somebody had to keep it somewhere, and the clinic's books were where the city kept what it could not afford to lose. Under it she ruled three columns and sat looking at them for the better part of an hour with the pen dry in her hand. Assets. Liabilities. Terms. Every sentence in Ashkarad was one of the three, and the sentence spoken through Dar Mehran's mouth in front of the fleet and the city was none of them, and that frightened her more than HUNGRY had, because HUNGRY she could have filed.

The city, meanwhile, kept the sentence the way it kept the fog: breathed around it, rationed against it, unwilling to name it indoors. Dar had come up from the shaft house gray as his coat and slept eleven hours, and waked, and asked her — the only question he asked, once, over the clinic stove — what she would file it under, and she had shown him the empty three columns, and he had looked at them the way he looked at an X-ray of something that had no business being alive, and said, "Good. It shouldn't fit." Then he had gone down to the wards to work, because a man holds two heartbeats and a crack in the Ninth Vein open by holding still and working, and Suri had closed the ledger on the empty columns and gone back to the door count.

Two days of silence from the fleet. The fog held, the censer-ration held, the Count held at its reduced strength, and the deep kept its listening quiet — not gone, not sleeping; attending — and on the third morning the tide-gates' watch hammered on the clinic door and said the Warden of the Reclaim was at the gate, alone, with a hand-cart.

Suri went down herself. She counted the steps on the way, one hundred and forty from the clinic to the gates, and made herself stop counting at sixty, because she knew what the rest of the count was for.

Wen stood at the customs post with the cart, and the cart was books. It outweighed him; you could see that it outweighed him, the way a coffin outweighs its next tenant — the handles dipped where his hands held, the axle already learning his lean. Stacks of ledgers under oilcloth, corner-clamped in brass, each spine lettered in the fleet's clerk-hand: ROUND-ACCOUNT, with years on them. A century of years. He had the white flag folded over his forearm like a towel over a barman's arm, and when she reached him he laid the flag on the customs post, folded it once more, and left it there.

"The Reclaim has no warden now," he said. His voice was a hundred years of patience worn down to the grain. Half his face was bloom-lace to the jaw; the lace did not move when he spoke, which made the moving half worse. "It has books. Books should live with someone who reads them." He set both his hands flat on the cart's rail, a man steadying himself against a swell that had already passed. "You asked nobody. I am telling you anyway: it sang. Through his mouth. That was the fee I named at the tide-gate — to hear whether the ninth seed could really sing before I ended. The fee is paid. The books are the balance, and I am the balance's courier, and I am done."

Suri looked at the cart, then at him, and did the addition out loud, because he was owed it. "You're defecting."

"I'm filing," Wen said. "I've been filing for the enemy for a hundred years. The paper doesn't care which way the cart points. I do, now, at the end. Take the books, counter of the door. Read them better than we did."

She took the cart. Nobody stopped him leaving, and he did not leave; he sat down on the customs post beside his folded flag to wait for whatever the city decided a defected warden was, and Suri hauled a century of harvest-accounts up to the clinic counter one clamped stack at a time, and began.

The fleet's ledgers were beautiful, and that was the worst thing about them. Double-columned, cross-ruled, every entry initialed twice; a bookkeeping so clean you could eat off it, and what had been served off it for a century was cities. Suri read with Vell chalking the second copy behind her — the mute clerk's chalk moving steady as a pump-handle — and Milla Ferun certifying the pages they burned through, registrar's stamps, the city's own seals going down in the margins like a fence going up.

The round-account opened with Brack. BRACK — PRUNED, PAID, the stamp she had seen on the tally-book's first line, closed out to the last audited unit: yields, render-schedules, the pour-routes down the deep roads, the tallow-convoys, the feed. Lowmark's page was open by a carcass — its seed cut, its rendering begun, its account not yet closed — and behind it, entered in the same fair hand, in the same patient columnar rhythm, seven bloomed cities still on the schedule. The Vigil. Gullet. Sarn. Weep. Ossuar. Hallowfen. Lowmark, to be finished. Every one with its yield-assessment, its season, its route. Every one a place where people kept water and bread and children, priced to the unit, signed per the Reclaim.

Suri read the yields twice. She read them twice because the first time her eyes had done a clerk's trick she despised: they had slid off the numbers the way eyes slide off a wound. The Vigil's whole whispering population, entered as a feed-mass. Gullet's four mortgaged generations, entered as they had mortgaged themselves, to the unit, four generations of scrip and interest reconciled into tallow. She had audited cruel books before. She had never audited books this calm. Halfway down the Vigil's page the schedule computed a per-heart yield, a figure so tidy she copied it out, struck it through, and copied it again, because the strike-through was the only protest the arithmetic allowed — and then she made Vell chalk the true number on the slate instead, because chalk could be washed, and the washing was a rite the city had invented for exactly this kind of arithmetic.

"He's right," she said to Vell, without turning her head. "We're reading them better. Better isn't the word I want."

Vell chalked: THE BOOKS ARE CORRECT. THAT IS THE DISEASE.

The brother came at dusk, with the second set.

She knew the cough before she knew the footsteps — a fog-sick cough keeping time, four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest, the cadence he had counted in since he was six years old and she was nine and the two of them had kept a pavement game of it outside the tithe-clinics, counting to make the waiting shorter. He came up the clinic steps gray-faced with it, a counting-clerk's case under one arm, clerk's calluses and ink-stains and the fleet's blank-rectangle tar still ghosted on his collar where he had scraped at it, and Suri stood behind her counter and counted his coughing for him so he wouldn't have to, four and a rest, four and a rest, and hated that it steadied him.

"Dathan," she said.

"You still count before you speak," he said.

"You still speak before you count." She came around the counter. She did not touch him; the toll for touching, with the fog in his lungs and the fog's hunger in the fog, was more than she could price tonight. "You countersigned for them."

"Eleven years." He set the case on the counter and opened it, and the books inside were not beautiful. Thinner paper, wrong-ruled, the margins full of a hand that had begun trying to write small enough to disappear. "Schedules, sister. The true ones. I countersigned the harvest's true schedule for eleven years, and last week I stood in that fog and coughed in your cadence and heard, through a wall, your census being read aloud in the quarry — names, and names, and no debts attached to any of them — and I understood that I had been an honest clerk for the wrong ledger my whole life." He took his hands off the case the way a man takes his hands off a railing over deep water. "It shamed me. Shame is a kind of arithmetic. I'm bringing you the difference."

She thought of one thing first, before the books, before the war, before the nine days: a nineteen-year-old with her skin sold and six weeks to live on the open market, and a boy of fifteen who went out into the fog with no trade but counting and came back with the coin that bought her graft home. He had never said where the coin came from. She had never asked. Some accounts you keep balanced by leaving the interest unexamined. He buys things back, she thought. It was the family sickness. He had bought her back once, and now he had walked out of the enemy's books to buy the difference on a city.

Inside: the harvest's true schedule, in the fleet's second hand, the one that matched nothing they had been shown — real dates, real yields, the pour-routes and the seasons and the seven bloomed cities still entered and owed — and behind the schedule, sealed in a sleeve of lead-foil that had cost someone a career to open, one page headed with a stamp Suri had never seen and understood immediately:

PROVISION — FINAL. RECLAIM OF ASHKARAD.

Below the stamp, in clerk-hand, countersigned by her brother's own initials in the corner like a knife:

The Door will be opened from within, by a living Throne, within nine days of service of this provision. On failure: prune, and uncount.

"Uncounted," Suri said. She made herself read the word in a normal voice. She was the city's counter; she knew what every threat in the ledger weighed, and this one she had to weigh aloud to believe. "Not pruned. Uncounted. Struck before the harvest, not after. No yield entered. No line-item. No name kept. No receipt." She looked up at her brother, and her voice did what it never did; it went somewhere young. "There's no column for that. There's no column for a whole city that was never allowed to have existed."

"I know," Dathan said. "I countersigned it four times. Service copies."

She ruled a new column on her own page, under the Lily's sentence, and wrote: NINE DAYS. Then, because she was who she was, she wrote the date of the ninth day, and looked at it. The clinic stove ticked. Vell's chalk had stopped.

The true books' last page was not clerk-hand.

It was bound at the back of the second set the way a flyleaf is bound, as if it had always been there, as if the binder had been told to grow the book around it. The paper was not paper. Suri knew memory-velum now; the whole city knew it since Lowmark; the leaf had that pressed, warm grain, like skin that had agreed to be something else. It was written left-handed, the letters slanting against every other hand in the books, and where a signature belonged there was no name — only the flower, drawn in a single stroke and drawn again over itself, a stamp of a sigil, blooming from a crack in the earth. The page carried the Root's account. The feeding, the schedule, the standing balance of the thing beneath them, entered and reconciled to date.

Beside the account, in fresh ink, cramped into the margin the way people write things they do not mean to send, one private line:

it no longer knows my song.

Suri read it once as a clerk and once as a sister, and then she did what she had done her whole life with pages that were too heavy to hold and too true to burn. She filed it, under seal, in the drawer that held Milla's printout and Vey's registry page and the Sister's black truce-petal — the city's unpayables, its drawer of things counted and kept — and she ruled the column beneath her brother's provision with a straightedge, and wrote the only entry her whole hand believed:

WE OPEN NOTHING IN NINE DAYS WE DO NOT CHOOSE IN NINE DAYS. — K.
