# Chapter 62 — The Nine Locks

The deep stirred at the hour of the sixth bell, and for the first time in the history of instruments, every pulse-reader in Ashkarad agreed.

They were counting machines, all of them — hand-cranked, brass-souled, built to file a debtor's heartbeat against a ledger's expectation — and at the sixth bell, in the quarry office, in the annexed Choratorium, on the Index's counting-floor by the seawalls, at the customs post on the dry water-stair, and on the clinic's own scarred bench, they printed the same page. Not a rhythm resolved from a patient. A rhythm with no patient near it at all: vast, slow, patient as interest, filling the paper edge to edge. Runners carried the slips in from every quarter through the grey of that morning, and Suri laid them out on the clinic's long table in a row, twelve machines' worth, and the row was one line.

"It's not listening to us anymore," she said. "It's talking. And every machine in the city just got drafted to take dictation."

The implications arrived on their own schedules, the way costs do. The Warden apparatus's whole rebuilt tracking layer — the new readers, the audited beats, the page-nine registry — was now a choir of loudspeakers pointed the wrong way; the machines could no more file the city's debtors than a bell can file the wind. Every census in Ashkarad was back to chalk and fingers, which meant, as Suri said, that for the first time in forty years the only honest count in the city was going to be the ones people did out loud, in front of each other, with their own pulse in their own throat. And beneath all of it, the rhythm on the slips was not the arrhythmia of something waking in pain. It was the cadence of something waking *deliberately*. Something that had decided to be felt.

Upstairs, Ilay heard the same thing and said it smaller. She stood at the wall-map with her chalk, where for weeks two circles had been plotted under the city — the great slow spiral of the deep, and beneath it, inside it, the small circle that kept time with Dar — and now she drew the two circles touching, then overlapping, then one circle, and wrote beneath it, in her careful child's hand: *together now.*

The council went down. All the way down — seven hundred and eleven steps, past the cold cathedral of the dead First Vessel, into the salt vaults where the walls rang like struck glass — to the antechamber before the open door. Dar walked at the front with Isra; behind him came Suri counting steps for the whole column, Vahid with spectacles and satchel, Roya with Ilay at her knee, the Choirmaster with his fire-axe carried like a crook, and Mother Gavel's overseer with a slate and a mandate, and at the very rear an Index observer who had declined, in writing, to descend below the salt line, and had filed instead a form requesting the minutes.

The door stood open, as it had stood open for six days. The warm air came up out of it, loam-scented, garden-scented, and nothing had ever come through. The Sister stood at the threshold on her side of the glass, a vast slow presence the eye could not fix and the ribs could not ignore, and she had been standing there, so far as any instrument could tell, since the night her petal rose the shaft asking for a word the city's factions were still drafting.

Isra knelt at the door with her slate. Ilay knelt beside her. And the Sister's testimony came up out of the deep in the two channels she had: the girl who heard, and the woman who wrote.

The geometry completed itself the way a column completes, line by line, each figure falling into the one above it.

The vaults beneath the door — the deep salt galleries the city had never mapped because they did not want mapping — held nine lock-seals. The Sister had lived against them for a century; she had counted them by touch the way a prisoner counts bars, and she counted them now for the living: nine great rings of salt and bone, older than the Church above them, older than the burning behind her, arranged in the dark like the chambers of a heart that had never beaten. The city above them was not built on a secret. It was built on a lid. Ashkarad itself — the storm-walls, the tithe-warehouses, the whole vertical ledger of the place — was weight, ballast, a paperweight of masonry set on a door to keep it shut, and the locking-collar of the Choratorium and the forty years of harvested lullaby had been maintenance, service, the upkeep of a seal.

She gave them the count, too, because she gave them everything she had — the long testimony she had kept for a hundred years with no one to file it to. The number of the deep thing's heartbeats since the burning, kept in her head without paper, one of the two longest columns in the world; and the note, still terrible, that a week ago, for the first time, it had skipped one. A missed beat, down there in the dark, in a rhythm that had not missed since before the Church had a name. Either it was dying, she wrote — and Isra's chalk hesitated over the word — or it was waking. She did not know which entry to post it under.

And the seals took keys. Nine seeds, sown a century ago by the same hand, one key to a seal: nine lilies sent down into the world to grow, and bloom, and be harvested — and each harvest, each pruning, each city emptied and stamped was a key turning. Lowmark's bride, humming in her flooded harbor. The Vigil, silent on its walking legs, quarantining its seed in lead and whisper. Gullet's Cow, moaning at shift-change, the third seed's Vessel caged in the engine's heart. Sarn, Weep, Ossuar, Hallowfen — names from a page, weights on a schedule, each one a seal turning over in the dark like a sleeper disturbed. Brack, first of the round, PRUNED, PAID. Eight keys already turning in eight far cities, one by one, on a schedule the tally-book carried in its chained and salt-scuffed pages. When the ninth turned — and the ninth key was not in a far city at all, but asleep in a surgeon's chest two paces from the door — the last seal would open, and the lid's term would end, and whatever the weight of Ashkarad had been holding down would be free to stand up.

The silence after the testimony had a texture, like felt. Then Mother Gavel's overseer spoke for her principal from the back of the antechamber, brass-voiced even without the tube: "The pit asks the obvious. What's the rate? What's the city get for holding the lid? Because somebody's been paying forty years of maintenance on this thing, and I don't see the receipts."

"The receipts are the city," the Choirmaster said, quietly, to no one, and Dar heard in it the man's own old question — what is the city for — finally answered, and heard how it landed on him like a diagnosis.

Then the Sister made her offer, and the offer was the worst arithmetic of the day.

She knew her mother's protocols the way a hostage knows a house. The Eighth Vein — the door past Mind, the one no honest text described — was not climbed; it was opened, and it could only be opened from both sides at once. Two climbers, or none: a door shared between two, each holding the other's silence against the far side. Tally of Gullet had spent a hundred years knowing it; the Sister had spent a century being the far side of it. And the Gardener, who had sown the keys and scheduled the round and sent the wain, was ready — eager, patient, terms prepared — to climb it herself, together, with the ninth. With Ashkarad. With whoever, in whatever council chamber or quarry pit or ledger line, said yes. Her hand on one side of the door, the city's hand on the other. Consent as the toll. The gentlest possible foreclosure. And the Sister, who had watched her mother's courtesy work for a century on eight other cities, appended the only annotation her testimony had ever carried, the closest her slate would come to an opinion: SHE HAS NEVER ONCE SAID NO TO A DOOR. SHE ONLY SAYS YES TO IT FIRST.

The factions' answers came the way weather comes. The Index observer above, consulted by runner, filed a written opinion recommending acceptance on the grounds that a signed agreement with a superior power was the only stability on offer. The Gavel's overseer asked, in one word, what the collateral status of a city that said no would be. The Open Counts, splintering even now over whose census was true, split three ways on the wording of the question itself.

Dar stopped listening to them.

He looked at the open door, and at the threshold where a century of patience stood waiting on a verdict, and he did the only arithmetic that had never once been done in this city, which was triage without a price. The Gardener's hand on one side of the door meant the Gardener's terms on both sides of it, forever; a door opened with her was a door she owned half of, and doors, in Ashkarad, were the only things worth owning. The Eighth Vein wanted two climbers. The city could not be one of them — the city was a lid, and lids hold doors shut. But Isra knelt at the threshold with the Sister's petal open at her collarbone, half-rooted, silence-trained, a diver who had already given her voice to the deep and gotten back a self; a woman who owed the Gardener nothing, feared her openly, and was known — known personally, chamber by chamber — to the one intelligence on the far side. The door between Dar and Isra already existed. It had been building itself since a salt-burned stranger walked out of the fog with a slate that said I BRING HER HOME.

"The climb happens," he said, and the antechamber's ringing salt carried his voice to every ear in it, "and it happens without her." He looked at Isra. "You and me. That's my vote. Nobody's buying the other side of this door."

Behind him, flat as a ruled line, Suri said, "Then I count the door. Every session, both pulses, one column. Nobody climbs anything in this city without a counter," and that was seconded by nobody and needed seconding by nobody, and was, therefore, law.

Isra looked at him for a long moment. Then she took the slate and wrote — no effort in it at all, the fastest he had ever seen her chalk move — I CLIMB. And at her collarbone the black petal turned on its stem, slow as a minute hand, until its point faced him: the Sister's own signature on the arrangement.

Above them, on Dar's chest, under his coat, the Lily went utterly still — the stillness he had learned to read as her version of leaning — and then she pressed once, briefly, against the wall of him nearest to Isra, and was quiet.

The column filed up the seven hundred and eleven steps into the grey morning, and the factions took their answers with them to be misfiled, and Isra caught Dar's sleeve at the first landing, out of the others' sight, and wrote on her slate slowly, with effort, two lines she did not show to anyone else. He read them alone, by the light of the shaft, and then folded the memory of them into the same inner pocket where the black petal rode against Vey's page:

She says: bring the ninth's music. Bring nothing you cannot lose.
