# Chapter 44 — Memory-Vellum

The readings-room sat above the drowned customhouse, which amused a part of Dar's mind that would not stay quiet in any city: the tide-priests had built their mercy-trade over the rooms where Lowmark had once taxed itself honestly. You reached it by a spar-stair from the walkways, past wax lamps burning with that low cold bloom-light, and at the top a man kept the door and the books and the box.

He gave his name as Orlo, registrar of this house of the Bride, and he was a narrow, unhurried person with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of a man who had priced so many things that his own face had become one of them. He looked at Dar the way counting-house men look at strangers — once for the clothes, once for the hands, once, briefly and with professional distaste, for the obvious. Dar had seen that triple entry before, on auditors and on Mother Gavel's ledgermen. It meant: this one will be expensive to cheat.

"You want a reading," Orlo said. It was not a question. The queue's gossip had already filed them. "Everyone wants a reading, after a wedding. The dead's mornings are very comforting. Two coppers buys you the priest's version." He folded his hands. "For the leaf itself, the house has other rates."

"I want the leaf," Dar said. "The real look. Not the sermon."

"Then you pay the house rate." Orlo drew a shallow tray across the counter and set on it a slip of grey stuff that was not paper — Dar had seen organs kept alive in glass and pages kept alive in wax, and nothing had prepared him for a document that was simply skin grown to a purpose. The slip had the weight of wet vellum and the give of skin that has been dried and pressed and dried again. "One true memory," Orlo said. "Small, and whole, and yours. The house presses it to a blank slip — that pays for the reading — and then you may hold the leaf for the space of eleven breaths. You will be shown what the leaf keeps. You will not be shown what the house does with what you paid. That is the rate. It is the rate for everyone. It was the rate before your city had a name."

"And if the memory isn't mine to give?"

Orlo looked at him a moment longer than the question needed. "Then the house will know," he said, "and the house does not return reading fees."

The press was a machine of black bone and brass, knee-high, older than any tool in the room around it — or it looked older; it had the terrible timelessness of the Gardener's make, the flower's own aesthetics grown into hardware. A bed of polished black bone. A screw of brass. A slot for the blank slip and, above the bed, a shallow cradle the size of two cupped hands, rimmed in the same grey velum.

"Hands in the cradle," Orlo said. "Think of what you pay. Hold it whole. Hold it at the front of you. The press does the rest. Most people find it like a tooth coming out. Clean, if you don't fight it."

Dar thought about lying. He had one good lie left in the maneuver budget and he was saving it. He put his hands in the cradle.

He paid the smallest true thing he owned that would still weigh something. It was the smell of carbolic soap in the intern washroom of Saint Ferrous Hospital — his first year, the long counter, the row of basins, the soap's white lather and its medicinal bite under the permanent salt of the city, the smell that had meant: you are inside the machine now, but you are clean, and the work is real. A nothing memory. A clerk's memory. He had carried it twenty years without once taking it out to look at, the way you keep a coin you never spend because it is the smallest one you have.

The press took it. There was no pain; that was the horror of it. There was a pressure, a turning, a sensation exactly like a tooth being drawn whole — and then it was done, and the brass screw ran back, and a slip lay in the slot, grey and heavy, one corner embossed with a five-petaled flower that had not been stamped but had simply come up through the slip's own grain, the way frost comes up through a sill.

Dar reached into himself for the washroom and found the room with the smell subtracted. Everything else was there — the counter, the basins, the cracked mirror, his own young hands — and the room was silent the way a room is silent when it has been robbed of one specific thing. He would never smell that soap again. Every memory of Saint Ferrous he owned was now captioned in absence.

The receipt printed itself while he stood there, from a slot in the counter's flank, in ledger-perfect script: RECEIVED — ONE (1) MEMORY, TRUE, SMALL. CONDITION: WHOLE. FILED. He took it, because a man takes a receipt; that reflex outlives everything.

"Eleven breaths," Orlo said, and opened the lead box.

The leaf came out on its grey cloth, and the room's lamps leaned toward it, all that bloom-light bending like grass in wind, and Dar understood that the lamps were not lamps. Nothing that had been alive was pretending very hard to be a lamp in this room.

The leaf was a page. It was heavier than paper, lighter than lead, grey as the inside of a shell, and its edges were finished with the flower — embossed along all four borders, the five petals repeating like a wallpaper pattern, like a watermark, like a stamp run along a document's length to prove it was money and not paper. A leaf of the World Registry itself. The priests were selling readings off a loose page of the Gardener's books, and calling it mercy, and the terrifying thing, Dar thought, taking it in both hands, was that to this town it probably was.

He breathed once, and touched it, and the last morning came up into him whole.

It was a woman's morning, sixty years old and kept in the page as if it had been pressed that week. Lowmark in flood, but flood the way it had been meant — water where water belonged, boats in streets, light on the harbor. She was young. She was breaking bread on a second-story sill with flour to her wrists, and her sister was braiding her hair with the brisk tenderness of someone who has practiced this exact grief in advance, and her father was keeping his boat, out on the gray water, and working the knots over and over because his hands needed a job that was not shaking. She was dressed in white by the sister's hands. She walked down through the city — through the real Lowmark, the living one, streets loud with people who would not meet her eyes because they had paid her family's debts in silence — and down the harbor stair, and onto the petal-road, and the petals of the Bride stood open for her like doors.

She was not afraid. That was the detail the page kept most cruelly, because it was the detail no sermon would ever use: she was glad. She had counted what her family owed and what she was, and she had decided she was the larger number, and she went down the road toward the dark heart of the flower the way a debtor walks into a counting-house to sign — with the terrible relief of a balance about to be settled.

And Dar understood, holding the eleven breaths of her morning, what the World Registry was actually made of. Not paper. Not vellum. People — the spent parts of people, pressed and filed, the surplus skimmed off every carrier and every vessel the route had ever opened, the way a mill skims the cream and sells the milk twice. The Registry's pages were an industry's waste product that happened to be somebody's whole morning: pressed carrier-memory, dried, cut, ruled, and stamped, and somewhere at the top of that supply chain a white hand signed each leaf like a foreman signing off a batch.

And then, the end of the morning. The end of the page.

The pressing. He was inside it — the memory's final act was its own filing, the last thing the dead woman's morning ever did was become this leaf — and he felt the press of the great bed, the bone under him, the grey of the velum rising through his own last light like frost rising through a floor. And over him, into his sight's last inch, a hand came down to sign.

Left-handed. Bone-white. A wrist with a ring of old burn-scars around it, old scar on old scar, healed wrong and healed long ago, the mark of a person who had once put her hand somewhere fire lived and taken it back out again, changed. The hand held a pen of black bone, and it did not sign a name.

It signed the flower.

Five petals, blooming from a rendered crack, drawn in three strokes, fluid as breathing, identical — Dar knew it was identical because he had a page folded in sterile wrap in the bottom of his pack, Vey's page from the sanctum under the Choratorium, eight cities, one circled in red — identical to the sigil he had been carrying for a season like a debt he could not read. The hand drew the mark once, unhurried, and set the pen down, and pressed the leaf, and that was the end of the morning, and the room came back.

He was standing. His hands were empty; Orlo had the leaf folded in its cloth already, and the box shut, and the receipt drawer closed, and Orlo was looking at him with the first honest expression Dar had seen on him — the expression of a clerk who has watched a customer understand the currency.

"Her mark," Orlo said. "The Bride's own, so they say here. She signs every leaf of the Registry. No name. The house has ten thousand of her signatures in the vaults and not one of them is a word." He said it as doctrine, bored, a man reciting a page he had inherited. "A name is a thing you are given. The mark is the thing that is given you."

Over and over, Dar thought. Like a stamp. She had signed the World Registry the way the Church stamped hearts, the way the census chalked doors — not because she could not write her name, but because the name was not the point; the mark was the account, and the account was the flower, and the flower was everywhere, blooming from every crack in every stone the route owned.

He walked down the spar-stair with his receipt in his pocket and the smell of carbolic soap subtracted from his past, and behind him, through the temple wall, he heard the drowned choir shift to a new harmonic, patient and vast.

That night, in the dive-house dark, he gave the Lily his palm and she drank, and afterward he held his hand open against the floorboards and frost came up through the grain of the wood in a slow fan of grey petals, every one of them pointing not toward the harbor this time, but up the hill, toward the readings-room, toward the lead box and the leaf and the counting-annex behind it where the vault-arches stood open and he had glimpsed, on his way out, shelf after shelf of grey leaves in their thousands, and a brass counter ticking to itself in the dark like a patient pulse-reader.

She knows her sister's paperwork, he thought. Or her mother's. The frost petals held their pointing, and he watched them until they melted with the dawn, and made his counts, and did not sleep again.
