# Chapter 98 — Some Things Were Meant to Be Shared

The Door stood open for as long as a great account takes to be read into its columns, and the reading took a day and a night, and Ashkarad came down to witness it in relays because the chord was holding the Door the way a sustain holds a note — as long as someone kept giving the sound a floor to stand on.

Dar stayed through all of it. He could not have said why, at first, except that the chord wanted its center of pressure where it had always been, and then, as the green light rose and fell in the shaft like slow respiration, he understood that he was waiting for an audit. His own. The account of Darius Mehran, opened nineteen years ago with a failing heart and a forged census line, was about to be read out in the presence of everything, and he intended to stand for it.

The Gardener worked while the Door stood. That was the thing that undid him first — not a god at the hinge of the world, but a woman with her sleeves turned back, tending. She had brought her daughter's garden through the Door's margin somehow, or the garden had come because she was in it; either way the antechamber's cold stone grew a green margin at its edges, rows of it, and she moved down the rows the way the white-gloved figure had moved down rows in a burning garden that looped forever — but without gloves now, and without the loop. She touched each row once, a thumb to the soil's rootline, an auditor verifying inventory by feel. Where her thumb passed, the grey salt of a century retreated in a thin bright tide, the way a stain retreats when the cloth finally gets the right water. She did not hurry. She had rehearsed nothing else for a hundred years.

Dar watched her turn, at the end of the rows, and look at him straight on.

The face was not new to him. That was the bottom of it, the whole ruinous ledger of it: the face was the oldest thing in his memory and it had never once been his. The swept room in his mind — the room where his mother's face had hung before the Blacktide charged him for it — had been swept of the wrong exhibit. The woman in the memory-bleed, the white gloves at the salt garden, the humming over planted sleepers: he had filed that face under MOTHER because it had arrived in a mother's register, at a mother's hour, humming a mother's song, and a boy's ledger does not question the filing when the entry hurts correctly. It was the Gardener's face. It had been the Gardener's face all along, misfiled at the root of him, and every time he had spent a memory to keep the city standing he had been spending, in part, a copy of a stranger.

The Lily gave it back to him there, at the Door, in front of the original.

She did it the way she did everything — without speech, without ceremony, a press against his ribs like a page turned toward the light. She had been holding the misfiled face since the fusion — since the oldest of her dreams, the one where his memories flooded into her when they married their blood to each other in a gut-shot alley — and she had kept everything, even the errors, the way a bank keeps cancelled notes. She opened the room in him and furnished it: the salt garden, the tower of heartbeat windows, the woman bending over rows that would not take — and the woman turned around in the memory, as she had never turned around before, and Dar saw the face whole and free of the kitchen where he had wrongly kept it. The Gardener, in the antechamber, met his eyes across the rows, and inclined her head a fraction — an auditor acknowledging a correction to the file.

"I sang it to her," she said, unprompted, in the voice that was mild and flat and older than salt. "To all of them, after. You had it the way the rain has the sea."

The lullaby. Dar felt the last column of the oldest account foot itself. The song had come to him through her — memory-bleed, the Lily's leak of her own inheritance into the body that carried her — and he had called it his mother's for a year, and lost it the way you lose something by selling it, and grieved it as blood. It was the Gardener's cradle song, older than the burning, sung first to a daughter who became the First Vessel and then, after the daughter died, to everything the Gardener grew from her — nine seeds, nine little roots, nine daughters grown past death and sent out with a commandment that had never stopped being a benediction underneath: *Go. Find your hearts. And bloom.* The Church had known the song only as maintenance: forty years of harvested throats humming a stolen copy of it down through the Choratorium's foundations to keep the lid's tension in the collar, a cradle song degraded into a schedule, sung to a prisoner by her jailers' machines. Vey had hummed it over tea. The wain had carried the last complete copy of it up the Reclaim road, catalogued like cargo. It had never once, in a century, been sung where it was going now — home, to its grave, to its garden, to the daughter it was written for. The three-heartbeat face at the Lowmark registry had mouthed *little root* at him with the Gardener's mouth, because it had never been his mother's face and never his mother's word. It had been an endearment for planted things, and he had been one, and had not known.

And the true mother's face came anyway.

It came the way the chord had taught the city to move: given, not bought.

It arrived in the middle of the night watch, when the standing count was thin and the freed vessels were resting in rings around the antechamber. Milla brought the registry of the willing down on Milla's own shoulder, and Suri read from it in her flat unhurried voice, and what she read was not debts. It was testimony. Mrs. Gable's recorded stories — the clinic had them transcribed, because Mrs. Gable had talked her way through three purges and two funerals and a lifetime of Saltstairs winters, and the girl who ran the transcription had not known she was filing evidence — told of a young woman on the landing who sang to her window-boxes because the salt killed everything and she refused to let the salt have the last word. The neighbor in 4C remembered her hands, how she mended other people's linens when her own ledger was bleeding. The man who had once watered her steps remembered the tune she hummed — not the lullaby, something smaller, hers alone — and Vell chalked it, and Ilay heard it, and said it was true.

The chord took the testimonies the way the room took payments: held in common, then given back made whole. The swept room in Dar did not refill with his own past — the costs he had paid stayed paid, and no power in the stack of worlds un-spends a memory. What came instead was a new entry, credited from other people's remembering: his mother's face, assembled from the city's love for her the way a chorus is assembled from single throats — slightly foreign in the joins, true in the whole, smiling the way you smile when a neighbor's cat has had kittens and everyone has known for a week. It was not the face he had spent. It was better bookkeeping than the face he had spent. It was every witness he had ever had, agreeing. And Mrs. Gable was in it, which undid him worst of all — her line-item death in the vault-mouth's inventory had closed nothing, because it turned out the dead keep their accounts open in other people's mouths, and the city had been reading her into the chord for a day and a night without knowing that was what it was doing.

He sat down on the cold floor of the antechamber and did surgeon things with his hands until they were quiet. Suri, who had read his mother into existence off a page, sat down beside him without a word and counted his breaths until they evened, which was her entire theology, and it was sufficient.

Ilay heard the song whole that same night.

· · ·

She was standing at the rail with Roya when the Sister took the pedal-tone up out of the floor and let the true song turn over, every verse at last, unbroken — the child's thread, the mother's under-line, the verses in the dead languages that the cracked door had taught them one bar at a time, and then the ending, which Ilay had never heard, because the ending had never once been played.

The girl went absolutely still. Then the tears came, fast and silent, the way they come from children who have done their crying in private for years, and she gripped the rail and listened with her whole body, and Roya stood beside her with her brass mask catching the green light, one hand on the girl's shoulder, deaf to all of it and hearing it anyway through the ears she had learned to read like a country.

On the last page of the score she had titled *for my grandmother* there was a note in her careful child's hand: *the ending is a door. don't let them count it.* She had written it months ago, from under the world's floor, without knowing what she was transcribing. The ending arrived now, and it was a door — the green hinge-light at the bottom of everything, standing open on a tended grave — and no one counted it. Ilay looked at Dar across the antechamber and nodded once, a bookkeeper closing a column, and mouthed: *that's it. that's the ending.*

The freed vessels sang it once, all the way through, into the opened Door.

Elias began it, because he had been given the first line at the vault-mouth a year ago and had never surrendered it; the eleven came in behind him; then the fourteen, then the donors, then the count above thinning down the shaft to add its grain; and the song went through the Door and out into the first garden the way a name goes into a registry, and the Gardener, at her rows, set down her tools, and stood, and let her daughter be sung to.

She came up the green margin toward them as the last verse ended. She stopped level with the grave-side of the rail, and looked a long time at the boy from the Saltstairs with the misfiled face and the corrected heart, and at the copper-haired girl who had transcribed her song without ever being told whose it was, and at the eldest seed's dark threshold beyond them all.

"Some things were meant to be shared," she said.

It was not the apology she had rehearsed through a hundred years of burned cities. The outline of it was the same, and nothing else was: no excuse in it, no arithmetic, no *I had no other way*. It was said the way benedictions are said, which is to say onto people rather than at them, and then she turned back to her rows and her tools, and the Door held, and the chord held. Dar sat with the two faces in him — the stranger's, returned to its owner across the years like a cancelled note made good, and his mother's, built overnight from the city's love — and he took stock, because taking stock was the oldest discipline he had. The pool of held things was deep now: a refusal, a drowned coastline, a kitchen, a brother's name, thirty-one sold slips of music. He kept the fraction that had been handed to him outright, and let the rest lie where it belonged, in common, unownable, drawing no interest and needing none. It was, he thought, the first time in his life he had ever let a balance stand uncollected. In his chest the Lily rested against his ribs like a guest who had finally stopped checking the exits. In the deep the first seed kept the low note going for all of them, glad, and unhurried, and — for one more day — within reach.
