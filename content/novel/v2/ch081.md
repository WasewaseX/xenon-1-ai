# Chapter 81 — Read Aloud

Suri had watched the fleet's tally-bearers walk the hymn-morning with their books open, and she had understood the shape of the trap before the last rank had folded off the flats. You did not defeat an audit with defiance. Defiance was a fine feeling and it compounded like everything else. You defeated an audit with provenance — you attacked the paper the appraisal was made on.

The fleet had spent one morning appraising Ashkarad's singing blood. Appraisals rest on ledgers. The ledgers the fleet had inherited from the Church were the same ledgers Ashkarad had lived under for forty years, and Suri had spent her whole life inside those ledgers — sold, resold three times, retired, burned — and she knew them the way you know a house you grew up in. She knew which rooms were real. She knew which rooms were drawn on the plan and never built.

So she went to the council with a motion that cost nothing and could not be refused, which was her favorite kind, and she drafted it in the grammar of the thing they were fighting. The fleet's terms — the stock children, the ninth seed, tribute at the tail — were prices. Prices presumed valuations. Valuations presumed records. Therefore, the motion ran, before any valuation of Ashkarad's collateral could be entertained by any party, the underlying record would be read into the public account, in full, aloud, so that buyer and seller and asset alike stood on the same page — and if the record proved unsound, then every price built on it fell with it. An audit could refuse many things. It could not refuse an open book. That was the whole disease and the whole cure of bookkeeping, and she had learned it from the inside, in a numbering line at eight years old.

"For three days," she announced in the quarry, from the floor of Mother Gavel's pit, to the assembled crews and the gallery and the Index's observers and the fleet's own distant lenses, "the clinic will read the Chorus's books aloud. Every page. Every name. Every Voluntary consent. You cannot unring a ledger that has been read out loud, and no audit on that seabed can price a city on paper that dies in the open air."

The couriers posted the hours on every door in the city that same night, next to the fleet's posted terms, lower and to the left, the way a correction runs under an error.

Mother Gavel granted the floor with a nod that moved one scar no more than a coin moves. "Truth's free," she said, flat as ever. "I'm supplying the acoustics."

The first day was the rescued pages — the name-pages the couriers had dragged burning out of the census halls a year ago, Suri's salvage, kept in the sanctuary's sealed shelves — and the reading of them was Suri's own, because she had led the smoke teams and she owed the names that debt. She read debt after debt in her counted voice: sums, dates, resales, the interest columns that grew while the debtors slept. Once she reached a page with her own graft-number on it — the page that should have been ash in a clinic stove, retired, the column at zero — and she read it the same as all the others, flat and exact, and did not once look up, and the pit let her have her privacy in the only way a crowd can grant it, by being silent enough to count as no one watching. The crews who had spent decades collecting those debts stood and heard them itemized, and the itemization was worse than the collecting, because collecting never showed you the whole book.

The second day was Milla's.

She took the registrar's stand with the coil of her printout in its oilcloth, and read the first pull of the RHYTHM UNRESOLVED machine aloud — the two rhythms, the fault-strike, the destruction-order number — into a silence that leaned toward her like water toward a drain. She read the order that had commanded her to burn it. She read the date. Then she told them, in the voice of a clerk reading minutes, what she had done instead: copied, kept, carried the true original sewn into her coat lining through a year of audits, purges, and bounties, salt in her nails the whole while.

"An anomaly is only a fault," she said, "until someone keeps the receipt. Then it's a record. I am the record."

Then she did the thing Suri had spent two nights coaching her on, and did it with a clerk's merciless economy: she dated the forgeries. The Voluntary consents came off the shelf, hundreds of them, and Milla walked the pit through the ink, the stamps, the hands — one consent signed in a child's careful letters dated three years before the child's hand could have written it; the same clerk's hand on four hundred consents in a single week; Voluntary stamps filed in sequence, in batches, like masonry. The Chorus's consent machine came apart in the pit the way the activation verse had come apart in the streets: not violently. Component by component, unable to resolve.

On the gallery rail, in the third hour, a senior Index stamps-clerk named Ivet stood up, unhitched the great Warden's stamp from her belt — forty years of writs and seizures in one brass cylinder — and carried it down the stairs and laid it on the registry table in front of the registrar.

"The record should be kept," she said, "by people who read it."

Suri looked at the stamp, and then at the woman, and made herself ask the question that mattered: "Why now?"

Ivet considered her answer the way she had apparently begun considering everything, freshly, as if the old procedure had been recalled. "Last winter I ran a seizure on a weaver's family in the Weep steps. Two presses, one sick child. The consent was signed. It was in order." Her thumb moved along the brass barrel of the stamp, finding the wear. "This morning your clerk read four hundred consents signed in the same hand in one week, and I thought: whose hand signed mine. I have been asking since breakfast. There is no answer in the file. There is no file. There is a stamp, and I am putting it down."

She was not the last. By dusk on the second day there were nine stamps in a row on Suri's table, and then nineteen, and the Index's docket of conscription and seizure died in the open air of provenance, unmourned, unsigned. Seft's readers could print RHYTHM UNRESOLVED all they liked; there was no longer a stamp in the room that would file the city's children under anything.

The third day belonged to the Choirmaster.

He came up from below with the last exhibit in a strongbox on a handcart, and he came up at the hour when the pit was fullest, because a choir-master knows you do not begin the anthem before the house has filled. The box held the unburned harvest-registry copy — the nursery's own working roll, the book of the taken, pulled from the sealed clerk-nest under the harvest floor where he had shut it the night the Choratorium's other ledgers fed the fires, and kept, and kept, and kept. He had held it through the burning, through the silence, through the year of the door, and he set it on the registrar's table now and opened it, and his baritone, which had once graded children like fruit, filled the quarry the way it had once filled the amphitheater.

He read the roll.

Every child's name. Every entry — name, stock, grade, date taken, and the column that recorded what was harvested and when. He did not hurry, and he did not soften, and when he reached the eleven, the quarry's breathing changed, and when he reached the ones who had never come out of the amphitheater, whose lungs and voices were already glass by the time the raid broke the doors, his voice did not waver at all, because it had not wavered on the harvest floor either, and that was the crime, and he made them hear it. He read his own annotations in the margins, the ones no supervisor had ever signed — this one hummed while they took the blood; this one asked if the choir was cold — and the pit heard the machine from the inside, in the machine's own hand, which no amount of doctrine on the seabed was going to un-hear.

He read until the roll was done, every child's name counted out into the open air of Ashkarad at last, and then he gathered the registry into his arms and carried it to the pit's great brazier and fed it to the fire page by page. In the count-room above the pit, a pickman named Sedge, who had been counting backward from one hundred and nine since a flowering pick taught him the price of touching the deep, felt the number in his skull let go of one whole unit at once — the quarry was a threshold, and things had crossed it — and sat down on the stairs and laughed until he wept, and Gavel's crews made a place for him without being asked. Mother Gavel took off her hat. That was all. She took off her hat and held it to her chest with both hands, flat as a ledger page, and no one who worked for her ever once mentioned it, because some entries you keep off the books.

"No more vessels," the Choirmaster said, to the fire, to the crews, to the seabed, to the deep. The register of the taken went up in a column of black and gold, and no one in the quarry moved to stop it, because there are debts that are only ever paid in burning, and every soul in that pit recognized one when it was finally, publicly, discharged.

It was Ilay who felt the last thing, because Ilay's hands were on the stone, and the stone had been carrying a rhythm for a year that skipped, and faltered, and counted back in threes.

She took her hands off the floor and turned to the council bench with her face full of a wonder she had no slate word for yet, and Roya read her before she found one, because Roya had spent a year learning the girl's silences the way other people learn weather.

"What?" Suri asked. "What is it?"

"It settled," Ilay said. "Under the pit. The big one — the deep one. It was counting back in threes and being all — crowded." Her hands made a shape in the air, an unclenching. "It's slow now. Even. Like —" she hunted for it, ears tipped toward the floor, ten years old and the best instrument in the city — "like when the Choirmaster finishes the anthem and nobody leaves."

Isra's slate came up slowly from the front bench, paying its warmth letter by letter, and said what the Sister had said down there in the dark, under a cracked glass door, when the books of her captors were read aloud in the open air above her for the first time in a century:

THE DEEP HAS BEEN READ TO ALL ITS LIFE. NO ONE EVER READ IT THE TRUTH.

Suri looked at the slate for a while. Then she opened the census, ruled a fresh column beside the standing order for the second verse, and headed it READINGS — THE DEEP, DAILY, and entered the quarry's three days as the first item, disbursements none, consideration truth. "Then we keep reading to it," she said. "It's the only neighbor we have that's never once lied to us about a balance." Vahid, at the bench's end, was already revising a lecture he would never deliver about the therapeutics of testimony; Roya chalked the settlement on her map wall — the spiral's innermost ring gone slow, gone even, gone quiet as a sleeping account — and looked at it for a long time with the mask tilted, as if the map had finally said something kind.

The Root's rhythm ran slow and even beneath the city until dawn, and the clerks of the quarry wrote that down too, because it was true, and because — apparently, and terribly, and against every Doctrine on the seabed's shelves — the deep liked being read to.
