# Chapter 47 — Bloom-Tallow

The announcement came at first light, and Suri counted it the way she counted everything now, because the counting was hers and nobody on this road could repossess it: two criers on the harbor stair, one writ of the Bridewarden read aloud in the warm administrative voice the priests all seemed to grow into their robes with, and four hundred and some faces on the roof-rows going still in the particular way of people doing arithmetic they do not want done.

"By grace of the Bride and the necessity of the tide," the crier said, "a marriage-tithe is declared. The account of the city runs short. The Bride asks a bride."

"The Bride asks a bride," the second crier repeated, and unrolled the schedule, and Suri heard the name from three roofs away, in the flat silence that followed it, and was moving before the crier's echo finished.

The crop was a child.

Hessa — no surname; the pruning roads had taken it, or she had; refugees off the flats learned to travel light in every column — was eleven years old, small for it, quiet in the way of children who have audited their own situation and filed it under unsolvable. She had come into Lowmark a month back with a salvage caravan off the drowned coast settlements, walking the whole distance, Maren said, with her shoes tied around her neck so the salt would not eat them, and Maren's dive-house had taken her in the way the dive-house took in everything the tide gave back: without a ledger entry. For four weeks she had been nobody's account. Then the priests had come to the roof-rows with their needle-test, grading blood in the doorways the way the Church of Ashkarad had once graded vessels — and the girl's blood had answered them.

Stock. Suri had stood in the doorway of Maren's common room and watched the needle come away from the child's arm and watched the priest's face do its entries, and she had known the word before he priced it, because she had sat up nights in a Saltstairs clinic guarding another child with the same column in her file. Sibling stock. Nursery-grown, hymn-fed, the Church's long investment — except that this one's Church had drowned, and the road's Church had come to collect the returns.

"The stock is the Bride's by the law of the route," the priest at Maren's door had said, not unkindly, which was the worst of it. "She was sown to sing. Let her sing where singing is kept."

Now the criers had scheduled it: marriage at dusk, the petal-road, the full rite. Suri spent the morning doing what she did instead of despairing, which was bookkeeping. She sat on Maren's roof with her ledger open on her knee and the harbor laid out below her like a column waiting to be totaled, and she added up a city.

The Bride's yields, as doctrine told them: the weeping of the petals after every marriage, the bloom rendered in the copper pans behind the temple, the whole of it the god's own substance, tithed to the town's keeping. The casks out: four hundred and six since the strangers came, and Suri had numbers for the weeks before that too, because Tetch's older sister kept the dock tallies and Tetch's older sister could be traded with. The marriages in: eleven in the season. Eleven marriages' weeping, against four hundred and six casks. Suri ran the rate twice, because you always run a shortfall twice before you accuse anyone of it, and the shortfall did not shrink. It was not a shortfall. It was a skim, and it had a schedule, and the schedule ran weekly, up the inland road, toward the reef-city on the leviathan's carcass where the books said the rendering was done at scale.

"They're not tithing the bloom to the Bride," she told Dar, in the common room, while he restocked the bench from Maren's salvage. "They're tithing it to Gullet. The god weeps, the priests milk, the traders haul. The marriages are just the udder."

Dar tied off a bandage, said, "I've heard worse theology," and did not smile, and Suri filed the line under the column she kept for the things he said when he was already three moves into a plan she hadn't been shown yet.

The audit itself she did that afternoon, in person, because numbers you have not stood in front of are rumors. The granary stood at the head of the petal-stairs, a stone-buttressed barn of a building where the tallow casks were staged for the weekly caravan, and its tally-boards hung by the loading door in plain view, because the priests had never needed to hide their arithmetic — who was left in Lowmark to check it? She walked the loading yard with Maren's tally-stick in her sleeve and Tetch on look-out at the stair, and she read the boards, and copied nothing, because the counting was hers and it lived in her head now — Vell chalked the totals after her anyway, a second copy, a clerk's insurance — filed under the day's column: casks rendered, casks blessed, casks tithed to the temple floor — and then, under the temple's own mark, the second ledger, the real one: casks sold, casks priced, the factors' chits stacked on an iron spike like receipts for a god they had already sold. Each chit was stamped with the flower, and each flower had been countersigned at the bottom by a Gullet factor's mark, and the prices on them were the prices of industry, not faith.

She was back over the rope-walk before the bells, with the whole skim priced and filed, and dusk came down on Lowmark like a hand closing a book.

The petal-road at dusk was the most obscene beautiful thing Suri had ever been forced to look at — the great black petals slicked with their own weeping, the wax lamps of the procession bowing their cold light along the road, the hum of the drowned choir rising up through the soles of the walkways until her back teeth ached with it — and down the center of the road, small in the too-big white, walked the crop.

The Bridewarden himself officiated, a vast calm man with a voice like the tide coming in, and the rite began the way the rites began, with the hymn, forty bars, economized — and Suri stood at the rail of the harbor stair with her whole body counting, and watched Dar walk out onto the petal-road.

He did not run. That was the first thing the crowd noticed, and the thing they told each other after: the cutter from the dive-house walked out into the procession at the pace of a man crossing his own clinic, and he went to his knees before the bride as if the road itself had summoned him, and he said, in a voice pitched to carry to the last roof, "The town's surgeon examines the bride. Every diver here has paid me in work and I have never yet billed any of you. Nobody prices this child today but me."

The Bridewarden's calm did not flicker; he extended one hand in permission the way you permit weather. And Dar turned back the wax-cloth from the girl's hairline, and the crowd saw what Suri had already seen from the stair and could do nothing about except count: under the marriage-crown, the crown of wax and bloom the priests had set on the bride's head, the child's scalp was already sutured. Stitched. Seven knots of waxed thread, worked in the tide-temple's neat surgical hand, anchoring the crown into living skin — the marriage had been performed in advance, in a side chapel, without consent, without witnesses, and the ceremony on the road was only the delivery.

"Seven sutures," Dar said to the crowd, and his voice did not rise either; it dropped, into the register Suri had heard him use on mothers in the Saltstairs, the one that makes a room go quiet by refusing to be loud. "She is eleven. The crown is grown in. If she walks the road today, the flower does not take a bride — it takes a graft that has already been sewn, and everything the priests have told this city about the married wanting for nothing is a lie stitched into a child's scalp." And he took out his knife, and he cut.

He cut the way he cut — Blood Vein quiet under the skin, the girl's blood holding in her veins at his word so that there was no red on the white cloth at all, seven knots unpicked in the space of twelve of the Bridewarden's heartbeats, the crown coming free into his hands like something aborted — and he stood up on the petal-road holding the marriage in his hands, and the town of Lowmark, which could count, counted.

Suri took the pulpit stair. Not asked; she had stopped waiting to be asked sometime around the second city of her acquaintance. She stood where the criers had stood at first light and she read the numbers, flat, at carrying volume, the shortfall and then the skim and then the schedule, eleven marriages against four hundred and six casks, the god's weeping priced and sold up the road to Gullet's factors at the prices of industry, and she finished with the worst figure, the one she had saved: the Bride's own share, per the priests' own second ledger, since the season began.

"Zero," she said. "They're not feeding her. They're farming her."

The silence afterward had a texture. Then Maren's voice came off the roof-row, gravel and rope: "My son sings flat up there," she said, "and you billed me for his candle," and the dive-house crews came down their stairs with their hands empty and their faces full, and the Bridewarden stood on the petal-road among his wax lamps and did the arithmetic of a man watching his congregation convert in real time, and gave no order at all, because there was no longer an order that totaled.

They took the child home through the crowd with Dar's coat around her and Suri's hand on her shoulder, and on Maren's roof, in the common room, with fish stew going cold around her, Hessa began to hum. Low, automatic, terrified — the claiming-hymn rising out of her blood because her blood had been taught it before she could walk, the Church's long investment waking to collect. Dar crouched in front of her and did not tell her to stop. He put two fingers on her wrist, the counting position, and breathed with her, one, two, three, until the hymn lost its place and the girl's own breathing filed in under it, and Suri sat across the table and entered the child in her ledger under the only heading she had left: OURS. THE ROAD CAN'T HAVE HER. STAY COUNTED. YOU ARE OURS NOW.

The granary burned itself that night.

No torch, no spark, no sappers — the divers on the roofs swore it to a person, and Suri believed them, because she had seen the frost. Grey petals had come up over the granary doors an hour after midnight, fine as breath on glass, fanning across the stone the way they fanned across Dar's camp-canvas, and then the tallow inside had caught from within, all four hundred casks of the priests' skimmed and hoarded god lighting along the seam-lines at once with a low cold green flame that did not gutter and did not spread to the neighboring roofs, a fire with the good manners of an audit. It burned to the waterline of its own floor and stopped. The priests stood in the yard and watched their surplus become light and did not reach for one bucket, and out in the drained harbor the drowned choir shifted, and the hum came up richer by one long note, like a ledger, somewhere, being balanced.

In the morning, in the wet ash of the loading yard, Suri found the chit. It had been on the iron spike with the others; now it lay on the staging table with the table's legs burned out from under it, face up, unburned — stamped with the five-petaled flower in strokes as fluid as breathing, and countersigned beneath in the Gullet factor's mark, and under the countersignature, in a third hand, small and vertical and terribly patient, the instruction that had licensed the whole skim, the whole season, the whole route:

PER THE RECLAIM — SUSTAIN THE ROUTE.

Suri read it three times. Then she folded it into her ledger, into the new column, the one she had opened on the rope-walk two days ago with the knife-edge of a wax stick, and she wrote beneath it the only entry the morning allowed: EVIDENCE — ONE (1) — and, after a moment, looking up the inland road toward the reef-city and the monastery and whatever else the route kept ahead of them:

THE ROAD IS SUSTAINED. THE QUESTION IS BY WHOM.
