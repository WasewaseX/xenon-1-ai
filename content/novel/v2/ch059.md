# Chapter 59 — The Locking-Collar

The Choirmaster had spent his life learning what rooms wanted to sound like, and the reliquary corridor at dawn wanted to sound like nothing, so he walked it on the flats of his feet with the lamp hooded and let it.

He made his rounds the way he had made them for a year now, past the shelves where the dead kept their labels squared to the rail — he had re-lettered every one himself in the first weeks, because a man should know the names of what he keeps, and because scavengers respect a tidy ledger more than a lock — and down to the iron shelf where the lead tithe-box sat with the unburned registry inside it, the vessel list entire, the reckoning he had saved from other men's fires and now guarded like the last honest line in a corrupted book. He was dusting the box's hinges when the tithe-clerk's step came down the corridor, Milla Ferun, salt-stained to the knuckle, with a sealed folder held out in both hands the way you hand a debtor his own signature.

"From the clinic," she said. "Certified copy. I took the deposition myself."

He read it standing up, because there was nowhere in the corridor to sit that did not have a dead donor's name on it, and he read it twice, and his hands were steady and his voice was steady when he said, "Who else has seen this," and she said, "The clinic. The council by nightfall. The Professor wants you at the congress," and the Choirmaster heard his own heart do the thing hearts do in choirs when the key changes without warning — one beat of pure refusal, and then the modulation, and then the new key, whether you like it or not.

He did not go to the dining hall first. He went to the archive, and he took the folder with him, and he pulled the doctrine.

The Church of the Silent Chorus kept its service books in graded holdings, and the Choirmaster had run the harvest, which meant he had held the keys to the inner holdings, which meant he knew where the harvest-office rubrics lived: the Ordinal of the Undersong, the liturgy no pulpit ever saw, the book the Custodians swore their Custodian's oath on. He laid it on the reading desk beside Milla's certified copy of the Sister's testimony, and he set the lamp, and he did what he had spent forty years doing — he read for the music — and this time he heard what the music had been for.

The rubrics were instructions. He had always known them as mystery, sung them as mystery, taught them to novices as mystery; laid beside the deposition they read like a works order, and the horror of it was not that the words had changed. The words had never changed. Only their account had.

THAT THE CHOIR ASCEND NOT, BUT DESCEND — forty years he had heard that as doctrine about souls. It was a pipe spec. The Choratorium's foundations were not foundations. He went down into the undercroft with the lamp and found the architect's plates where he had walked over them ten thousand times: the footings of the bone-arched hall cut not to bear weight but to bear it at a set pressure, ringed around the great shaft's throat in a collar of keyed stone, arch seated against arch, the whole cathedral-clinic a circlips of bone clamped on the neck of the deep — a locking-collar, the deposition's word, and the Church's own plates agreed with the deep's word, stamped in the mason's hand: COLLAR — MAINTAIN TENSION — SING TO LOAD.

The harvest-machine had been the pump. The tithe-warehouses, the organ docks, the grading floors, the nursery and its hymns: intake. The choir organs — forty years of harvested, still-living voices in glass, the sound he had tuned every morning of his working life — had never once been performing. They had been dosing. One long lullaby, fed down the shaft for forty years, note after harvested note, the whole gorgeous apparatus of the Chorus a machine for singing one sleeper to sleep. THAT THE SLEEPER DREAM, AND BE NOT WAKENED. THAT THE TITHE BE RENDERED SWEET. He had thought the last of those was about mercy at the table. It was a recipe.

And the choir had died at the Blacktide — the First Singer's release-song had silenced every organ in the city in an hour, and the Choirmaster had stood in the fall of that silence like a man in the rubble of his own house — and the Sister's count, read out in a cellar by a girl with her ear to a wall, said the sleeper's heart had skipped its beat last week. The Professor's arithmetic had come with the folder, in his crabbed one-handed hand, and the Choirmaster copied it into his own memory because it was the kind of line you memorized against the day you had to testify: the sedation stopped when the organs stopped. The skip followed the silence. The books of the Church, read correctly, said the city had been holding a note for forty years, and that someone had finally let it end.

"What is the city for," he said aloud, in the empty undercroft, in the voice that had once filled the harvest amphitheater, and heard the answer in the collar's own terms, and it was worse than any answer the Custodian had ever offered him: the city was for keeping the lid down. The city was maintenance. Every tithe, every pulse-reader, every graded child had been a hand on the collar, and the question he had carried out of his amphitheater with a broken wrist came home to him with its full invoice due: not what is the city for — whose is the city.

They convened at the sixth bell, in the dining hall of the Choratorium he had run for the Church and Mother Gavel now ran for herself, and the Choirmaster walked into it through a door two grey coats held open and heard the whole impossible chord at once, because that was the gift the years had left him.

Mother Gavel at the head of the long table with the brass tube lying in front of her like a conductor's baton, her ledgermen ranked behind her, her flat ledger-page eyes going over every arrival and pricing them by the coat. The Index down the right side: Auditor Seft with the new reader in its case on the table in front of him like a guest at a communion, tar-rectangle armbands, four patrols' worth of grey. The Open Counts up from the ground at last — a dozen of them, chalk-dust in their hair, led by Clerk Elara with her counting-house manner and her eyes that had learned things in cellars. And at the far end, the clinic: the Professor with his spectacles, the counting woman with the ledger, the Cartographer with her mask and her charts, the clerk Vell with the minutes' slate, the tithe-clerk with the certified copy, the two-hearted man sitting quiet with ash still in the seams of his hands, and beside him, very still, the woman from the mud with the slate on her knees and the petal at her collarbone folded closed like a hand keeping its own counsel. On the sideboard, where nobody had cleared them since the feast, the two untouched cups still stood with Hesh's folded gloves between them, patient as a stain.

The Choirmaster gave them the finding first, because findings before motions was the only honest order, and he gave it standing, in his smooth baritone, and he heard the room go from congress to congregation under him as he read the rubrics out over the certified copy: the collar, the load, the descend-not-ascend, the forty-year lullaby, the harvest-machine as lid-maintenance, the skip.

Then the debate, which was the city deciding what it was for, and the Choirmaster timed it like music because it was the last thing he had to offer the room.

Mother Gavel spoke for servicing. Her position had the terrible cleanliness of all her positions: the deep had been fed for forty years and had not once eaten the city; the account was in good standing; a city that stopped servicing a debt this size did not get forgiven, it got collected; the chorus could be rebuilt, and organs, she said, looking down the table with her flat eyes, keep. She did not say where new organs would come from. She did not have to. The silence where she did not say it was the old harvest, standing in the room in its bone-plate, and the Choirmaster heard half the hall hear it and flinch and half the hall hear it and nod, and he filed both counts.

Auditor Seft spoke for sealing. The shaft, the vaults, the door: lamplight, he said, banked fires of it, every lamp and lens and storm-glass in the district's stock, because the machine's own findings said what fed in the dark weakened in the light, and a lid you could not see could at least be lit. The stock children would be registered for their own protection — the reader on the table was capable of the hymn and nothing else, he said, which was the most honest sentence the Index had produced all season and somehow the most alarming. And the record, he added, the printout, the anomalous slip — the Index would want that filed where it could be audited. The counting woman's face did not move at all.

Clerk Elara spoke for publishing, in a voice that shook once and then held: every doorway, every cook-fire, the whole account — collar, lullaby, feed, eight cities, the count — read aloud to the city that had already learned to chalk its own dead on a cistern wall, because a city that could count its dead could count its lid, and people who have counted a thing together cannot be ruled by it the same way.

And the two-hearted man spoke last, for the clinic, and he did not argue the motions; he entered one. The door below was open, he said, and she has not crossed. She has asked for nothing. A hundred years of witness and the eldest's first act on arriving was to lie down over the sleeper and hold still — the lid, doing its office, unpaid, unasked. The motion was small and had no column in anyone's book: that the city shelter the Sister; that the door be held in trust, neither sealed nor fed through; that the wain be sent nothing, and the city learn her terms before it priced her. Mend, he said, at the end of it, in the voice the Choirmaster had heard him use once in an amphitheater over a boy on a table — mend, not replace. The room, which had come to argue about a lid, heard a man offer to keep a door the way you keep a vigil.

Mother Gavel called the question on her own motion first — servicing, the hands, the count — and the Choirmaster watched the hall begin to divide itself into ayes and noes with the flat fatalism of a city that had voted before, and that was when the doors went open at the end of the hall and the runners came in.

Two of them: one of the tithe-clerk's boys and a count-runner of the Open Counts, both salt-white to the thigh, both still in the iron ring of the descent's torchlight, and the boy could not get his breath to the sentence and the count-runner did it for him, at the top of a voice that had been used at nothing but whispers for eleven days:

"The door. The salt-glass door. It's open — wide open, the whole way — and nothing's come through." Breath. The whole hall gone soundless around it, even Mother Gavel's ledgermen, even the brass tube. "Nothing. It's just standing open. It's been standing open. And the cold coming up the shaft is like — " and the runner, who had run seven hundred and eleven steps to deliver a measurement, gave up the metaphor and delivered the count instead, the only kind of sentence Ashkarad has ever trusted: "Nothing has come through."

The debate died mid-vote, hands still up on both sides, the way a chord dies when the organ stops — not resolved; suspended; every voice holding its note with nowhere to put it — and the Choirmaster stood in the middle of the silence with the certified copy in his hands and thought, in the habit of forty years, of rooms and what they want to sound like, and understood that the largest room in the city had just changed its mind about something, and had done it the way the deep did everything.

Patiently. In the dark. Without asking anyone's price.
