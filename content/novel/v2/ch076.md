# Chapter 76 — The Quarry Assembly

Mother Gavel prepared the pit the way she prepared a closing, because to her every assembly was a closing: the moment when everyone's numbers were finally on the table and someone had to initial.

Two days after the terms went up on every door in the city, her crews swept the fighting pit — the quarry, the old amphitheater her grandfather's grandfather had dug out of the salt rock when the Gavel was a crew and not an institution — and set it for the largest gathering Ashkarad had held since the Blacktide. Benches in tiers. Torch-baskets rigged with counterweights. Her own box at the floor's center, with the brass speaking-tube on its stand like a relic. She placed the factions by her own seating doctrine, which was credit exposure: the Index under Seft to her right, where she could watch his hands; the Open Counts under Clerk Elara to her left; the Hammers' remnant and the fog-sick in the cheap tier; the clinic's people where the light was worst, because Dar did his best work in the dark. She set no guard on the gates. Guards at the gates told the city the vote was rigged, and she needed the vote more than she needed her pride, and both of those facts went into her books as COSTS. The sea was still gone beyond the seawalls — dry mile, salt flats, the horizon standing at attention where water had argued for a thousand years — and the pit's own cisterns were under her seal, rationed by her crews, and every family in the benches had come up out of that arithmetic, and she knew to a bucket what fear of thirst does to a quorum.

She had read the fleet's terms with her thumb on the tribute line, and she had read the Gardener's three entries off the Choratorium wall with the whole city's breath held, and she had spent two nights reducing both documents to what she sold in her own pit: products. Three of them, cleanly ruled.

The chorus. Reopen the machine — rebuild the collar's song, harvest again, feed the lid its lullaby — and live as ordered livestock. She gave the product its honest prospectus: bread against work, organs against bread, forty years of borrowed throats, and the fleet takes its tribute and rolls on to Weep and Ossuar and the rest, and Ashkarad endures, curated, milked, and filed. Every family in the pit owned a piece of that product already. It had the market's one great advantage: it existed.

The bloom. Plant the ninth — force the Throne while the choosing was theirs — and be a Throne: fed, free, and eaten last. She gave that its prospectus too, because the Hammers' remnant was already selling it on the terraces in a cheaper dialect: no more ledgers, no more ration, the city becomes a body that feeds itself, and the harvest queue comes for everyone else first. She noted in the margin what the prospectus did not print: eaten last is still eaten, and a city that is eaten last has seven neighbors' screams to watch first, and interest on dread compounds like everything else.

And the third product. Dar's. The unnamed one. He had been trying to articulate it for two days in doorways and cellars and her own counting-room, and she had watched him fail with something adjacent to respect, because she had seen a thousand men fail to name a thing they were certain of, and the certain ones failed the hardest. The third product had no name, no prospectus, no yield schedule, and one feature the other two lacked: it was the only one on the menu the Gardener had not priced.

In the infirmary above the pit, Sedge was counting. Nineteen. He had come down through the thirties and the twenties as they carried him from threshold to threshold; thresholds moved the number, and the assembly was nothing but thresholds. She had him placed where she could see his lips. A mine is a hole with an entrance, she had told the vault, and she had postponed; and now the fleet had come, and postponement had acquired a counterparty, and the only gauge she trusted of what the deep thought about all of it was a pickman counting backward with his lips moving like a man praying to a debt.

She rapped the tube once. The pit went quiet the way her ledgers went quiet, all at once, on her authority.

"The city is called," she said, and the tube carried her voice flat and enormous to the top tier. "Three products on the floor. We count them tonight."

The chorus went first, and it went the way the dead go, politely, with attendance. Dar had spent two days articulating his product in doorways and cellars and her own counting-room, and the city had spent those same two days rehearsing its objections, and the pit was where the rehearsals came to open.

Seft argued it as written — order, bread, the fleet satisfied, the children excepted by amendment, the amendment excepted by sub-amendment — and the pit let him run four minutes and then started drumming their heels, one heel at a time, a sound like rain deciding. The Index's own clerks would not co-sign it. Ten years of sealed wards and stamped lists, and when the product was finally on the table with the blood priced in, the men who had built the filing system looked at the collection line and would not hold the stamp. Elara did not speak against it. She simply stood, and read the tribute line aloud one more time — the stock children, entire — and sat down, and the reading did the work the whole Index machine could not.

The bloom went harder, because hope always argues dirtier. The Hammers' remnant had the terrace, and the fog-sick had the grief, and both of them pointed at the same crack in the world: a city is a lid, the lid is held by songs, the songs are wearing out, and blooming at least makes us food that eats first. Against them stood the arithmetic nobody could unhear: the Sister's testimony, the granary chit, per the Reclaim — sustain the route. BLOOMED—TRUE THRONE, Isra's slate had translated once, means ripe. Eight cities had voted with their bloomers and been poured down the deep roads as feed. The bloom was not escape. The bloom was the harvest, self-served.

And the third product came to the floor, and Dar stood up in the bad light, and failed again.

He had the pieces. He had them all, and everyone in the pit could see him carrying them: the census of the willing, the medicine made from silence, the city that had counted its dead without the Choir's help and buried its old women by line-item and kept the names. Consent — he said that word, and the word went into the pit and fell like a coin down a dry well. The Hammers asked who ate under his system and he could not say, because the honest answer was he did not know what a city fed on consent ate, and no honest text existed. Seft asked who counted it, and he said everyone, which was either the first unpriced census in the world's history or a mob with a ledger, and the pit knew both readings were live. The Choirmaster — who had asked one question of this city across a harvest table, years and a broken wrist ago — stood up in the back with his hat in his hands and asked it again, quietly, without the tube: what is the city for? And the pit turned to Dar for the answer, because it was his product, and Dar looked at fourteen children who were not on the benches tonight, and at an empty storm-lantern in the clinic's window two districts away, and said: "For keeping." And it was true, and it was not a product, and the pit sat with it while the torches burned, and it would not close. Mother Gavel watched him fail to close his own sale and understood, in the ledger-way, what she was looking at: the first thing in her life's accounts that gained value every time it was refused, and she marked it unpaid and unsettleable, and hated it, and did not strike it.

They voted by chit and hand, both, because the Gavel trusted neither alone. Mother Gavel counted the hands herself, tube down, lips moving, Sedge's lips moving in the infirmary window above her in a slower count that no one was collecting. The chorus died in the low hundreds. The bloom took the terrace and half the fog-sick and died at the thought of seven harvests watched first. And the third product — refuse both, hold the city, name it later — climbed, and climbed, and stalled.

Nine short. Nine votes short of half the pit, with the abstentions burning unread in the Gavel's counting-box, and Mother Gavel looked up from her count and gave Dar the news the way she gave all news, flat, at cost: "Nine short. I don't round."

Deadlock. And deadlock, in her books and in the Gardener's, had a default clause.

In the infirmary window above her box, Sedge's lips stopped. Not the end of the count — the Gavel had audited that fear every night for a month — but a pause, mid-number, like a teller looking up from a ledger because the room's light had changed. She marked it. She had learned to mark everything the pickman did; he was the only instrument in Ashkarad that the deep read directly, and instruments, in her experience, were what you checked before you signed.

The pit began to understand what the default was. The terms were already on every door; unanswered terms collect at par; and the wain was on the seabed with its engine warm. Nine short. Dar stood in the bad light doing the arithmetic of a city that had come within nine souls of refusing the world, and somewhere below all their benches, under the salt, under the pit's old tally-scarred rock, the account they were all drawn on decided, for the second time in a hundred years, to turn a page.

The deep heartbeat skipped.

Every soul in the quarry heard it — not through the ears, or not only; through the benches, through the rock, through the fluid of their own bodies, one vast beat that failed to arrive and then arrived doubled, a stammer in the world's floor, and the Sister's counted total, two million six hundred and thirty-one thousand and some, gained its second missing entry in a century. The torches did not gutter. They bent. Every flame in the pit leaned seaward at the same angle, all together, a congregation of fire turning to face the water that wasn't there — toward the dry seabed, toward the grid of lanterns, toward the wain — as if the air itself had been drawn up into the world's lungs for the next word.

In the silence after the skip, in a quarry where nine votes had just failed to save anyone, a child's voice began to hum.

Elias. Perfect lungs. He sat in the third tier with the younger ones packed around him like a clutch, and he hummed the true song's first bar — Ilay's transcription, four notes, the opening of the thing the garden sang when there was a garden — and his voice did not go down through the rock or up through the collar; it went out, plain, unhurried, a boy's hum with nothing claimed in it.

No one stopped him.
