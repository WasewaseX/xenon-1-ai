# Chapter 25 — What the Fire Kept

The fires started at low tide, because even arson in Ashkarad kept business hours.

Suri counted them from the roof of the courier-house: seven glows at once, laid out across the Marrowworks belt like coins on a merchant's palm. The census halls, the tithe-warehouses, the records annexes — the places where the city had kept forty years of who owed whom. Two of the fires burned greedy and joyful, which meant debtors. Two burned tidy and ashamed, set from inside, which meant clerks destroying their own entries before anyone could read them. Three burned in formation, lit at even intervals with sentries on the approaches, and those were the grey coats, and those were the ones that frightened her, because organized fire was not rage. Organized fire was policy.

She went in at the second bell past midnight, with six couriers and a stack of boiled-wet blankets, and her reasoning was simple enough to be stated in a ledger: a name was the last receipt most people in this city still owned. The Choir was dead and its books were ash, and yes, good — but a debt destroyed and a credit destroyed look identical once they're smoke. Somewhere in those halls was the page that proved what people had paid. You did not leave receipts in a fire. Not if you had ever been the one holding the note.

"Courier rule," she told them at the canal stairs, wetting her own blanket with canal water that tasted of rust. "We take pages, not prizes. Anything stamped with a person's name on it comes out. Anything with a number where the name should be comes out too. Anything with a flower on it—" she paused, and found she had no column for the flower, "—comes to me first."

Nim, the youngest, asked the sensible question: why risk a wall of fire for paper nobody would ever honor again. Suri looked at the girl's unscaled arms and did not answer with the truth, which was that she had once watched a man pay for a tithe-clinic's services with the only arm he had, and had later watched the same man's sons inherit the balance. "Because it's all still evidence," she said instead. "The Chorus kept books for forty years. Somewhere in those books are the pages that say what was taken from your street, and when, and under which stamp. When the new counting starts — and it's starting, look at the sentries — the only leverage anyone downstairs will have is proof of what was already taken. Paper burns at the same temperature either way. The question is whose hands are warmest when it does."

The first hall was already lost, its roofbeams glowing like the ribs of a tithe-barge. The second was the records annex, three storeys of shelving around a yard, and the fire had started in the yard and was eating inward, patient as interest, and the smoke inside had that sweetness she had never been able to stop naming: preserved tissue, a smell like the Marrowworks remembered itself.

They went in through the loading dock, low, counting steps. Forty-two steps to the first gallery. Eighteen paces between shelf-rows. Suri's left forearm still wore its splint and her right still wore its ruined scales, and the scales hissed faintly whenever the heat crossed them, like a receipt being asked for twice.

The debtors' fire had already opened the cabinets. Papers lay everywhere in drifts, scorched at the corners, and the couriers moved down the rows with sacks, sorting by the only method that worked in a smoke-dark: feel. A page with a name-line at the top. A page with a column of marks. Suri took the gallery herself, because she had come for one page in particular, though she had told no one that, including herself.

The gallery gave up its dead by touch. A consent form with a Voluntary-Stamp so light it could barely be felt under the thumb — the machine's one piece of honesty, some dead clerk had once said, and Suri had laughed at the time and had not laughed now. A heart-tithe docket for a boy of nineteen, KAELEN, L., the page stamped through with a word that no longer needed the machine to be true: BLOOMED THROUGH. She put it in the sack that would go to the clinic wall, where Dar was starting to pin the names of the taken, because the dead of Ashkarad were counted by hand now and hand-counts needed pages. A nursery intake sheet with eleven names and eleven grades and a twelfth line crossed out — she read the twelfth line twice, ILAY, and put that page in the sack that came to her first.

And everywhere, on every second page, in margins and headers and stamp-corners, the five-petaled flower, drawn in grey ink by hands that had stamped twelve thousand hearts and never known they were also drawing something. She had thought the flower was Dar's. The fire was teaching her the flower had been everybody's.

She heard the grey coats before she saw them — boots in step, which no debtor fire had ever managed. Six men down the far gallery with wet rags tied over their faces, and they were not looting. They were feeding the shelves. Deliberate, orderly, shelf by shelf, a fire laid with the patience of an audit, and on their left arms, smeared in tar, a mark she hadn't seen before: a blank rectangle, a box with nothing in it. A placeholder. A space where a count would go.

"Index the whole hall," their leader said. "Nothing leaves that can't be verified. If it isn't ours, it's ash."

Suri put her couriers behind a fallen cabinet and did the arithmetic she hated: six professionals, two exits, one splinted arm, one arm that was worth less than it had been and more than anyone guessed. Direct was a deficit. She reached up instead and pulled the gallery's sprinkler-chain — old Marrowworks fitting, brine-tank overhead — and dropped forty years of rusted brine onto the aisle between them, and in the dark and the stink and the sudden screaming hiss of steam, six men with wet rags met a wetter fire and lost their formation to a floor of sludge. Nobody needed cutting. It was the cheapest work she had ever done, and she filed it under salvations, pending.

"What is that mark?" breathed Nim, the youngest courier, when they regrouped in the dock.

"Index," said a voice from the smoke — one of the grey coats, half-hidden, not advancing, talking the way clerks talk across a counter. "That's what's coming after the ash. Every soul counted once, under one seal, in one book. No resales. No interest. A census so complete it can't be argued with." A pause, and something almost like grief under the ash in his voice. "The old books can't be reconciled. The Choir was the reconciliation. So we burn what can't be audited and start clean."

"A census is a net," Suri said to the smoke. "You're naming the net."

"The net's already cast, courier. We're just agreeing to call it something."

They withdrew without a fight, which she distrusted more than a fight, and the couriers worked faster after that, because everyone had understood the same thing at the same time: the debtors' fires were ending and the organized fires were just beginning, and paper that survived the first would not survive the second.

On the way out, past the dock, Nim stopped at a low cabinet and pulled one last page from a drawer that had half fallen into the yard: a wages ledger, the Marrowworks payroll, clerks' names against years of service. She had gone grey under her smoke-grime. "My mother's in it," she said. "Fourteen years. She stamped hearts her whole life and the book says she was paid for it." She looked at Suri with the question that has no column. "Do I take it?"

"Take it," Suri said. "A wage is a debt going the right direction for once." And then, because the girl was still standing there with the page open to the fire's light: "It also proves your mother existed. In a week there may not be one other sheet in this city that does. We're not saving paper tonight. We're saving witnesses."

She found her own page at the fourth gallery, in a cabinet whose lock had been cut by someone in a hurry years ago and never mended, because nothing in the Marrowworks got mended, only harvested.

SURI KAVEH, said the header, in the Choir's grey ink. Graft registry number beneath it — the number the Wardens' readers used to note when they passed her on the street, the number she had carried on her skin for twelve years without once seeing it written down. And below that, in the column that had followed her since she was nineteen, the line she had paid for in skin and never been shown:

RESOLD — THREE TIMES.

Three holder-entries. Three parties who had owned a debt secured against her arm, listed with purchase dates and margins. Her mother's surgery, the whole chain of it, reduced to a transfer history. Somebody had owned her the way you own a bond, and sold her the way you sell one, and here was the paperwork, entire, still legible, edges scorched, the fire having circled it and gone on to something easier to eat.

The scales on her arm whispered against each other. Suri read the page twice, which was all she allowed, and then did the thing she had trained herself for in seven years of keeping ledgers: she recorded her own reaction instead of having it. Flat columns. Zero sentiment entered. The receipt had outlasted what it was a receipt for — that was the whole of it, that was the thing receipts did — and there was a freedom in the flatness, because a page that could be held could be filed, and a page that could be filed could someday, in front of witnesses, be burned.

She folded it into the spine of her own ledger, against the binding, where the truth of her kept company with the sums.

They came out at the fourth bell with eleven sacks of name-pages and no casualties, which Suri entered as the night's net profit, and stood in the alley behind the annex watching the third hall burn in formation. The smoke went up straight, windless, like ink drawn upward into water.

The salvage clerk found her there — a hall-clerk she half knew, one of the tidy ashamed kind, his eyebrows burned back to stubble, his hands shaking with the particular tremor of a man who has spent a night destroying his own work. He looked at the sacks. He looked at the fire. Then he leaned close, under cover of the noise, and spent his whisper like a man paying down a debt he feared would compound.

"They're posting bounties already," he said. "The grey coats. Not for people — people they just take. For paper." His eyes went sideways, down the alley, up. "There's a price out for a single sheet. It has a name on the posting. They're calling it the Ferun printout."

"Ferun," Suri said. The name didn't file itself anywhere. She kept her face the way she kept her columns. "What's on it?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows. That's what the bounty's for." He swallowed. "Printout of an old pulse-read, they're saying. Flagged. Anomaly class. Whatever's on it, the Index wants it the way the Church wanted vessels — you can hear it in the figure. People are being priced to find it. And courier—" he was already backing into the smoke, because informants, like debts, did not linger to be thanked, "—the figure's rising every day. It's the only line in this whole city still growing."

He went. The fire kept eating. Suri stood in the alley with eleven sacks of rescued names at her feet and opened her ledger one-handed against her knee, and on the current page, under the column where unknown liabilities went to wait, she wrote two words:

FERUN — ?

Then, because blankness was expensive and she had stopped permitting it years ago, she added a third word underneath, small, in the hand she used for things that didn't fit in columns:

(ask Dar)
