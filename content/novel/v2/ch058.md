# Chapter 58 — A Century of Witness

Isra had been a vessel of the deep for longer than she had been anything else, and the deep had never once asked her to take minutes.

They convened after nightfall in the cold cellar, with the chalk ladder on the wall and the banked lamps hooded to a coal-glow, because the session's gravity was downward and everything in the room arranged itself accordingly: Roya at the head of the salt shelf with her mask turned to the wall, Vahid with a fresh chalk-stick and the spectacles down on his nose, Milla Ferun at the little table with a ream of scrap paper and two sharpened pencils, like a woman drafted into a census against every instinct and unable to leave it, Vell at her feet with a slate of her own, chalk in her teeth. Suri kept the ledger open to a new page and ruled it with the wax-stick knife in long straight columns, and wrote at the head of the middle column, in her square clerk's hand, a title Isra watched her hesitate over and then commit: TESTIMONY — THE SISTER — TAKEN NIGHT ONE, UNDER SEAL.

The two-hearted man sat against the cellar wall, upright by will, ash-grey still in the seams of his hands. The copyist rhythm under his own had re-locked and held, and he had spent the day being argued over by the Professor and refusing to spend anything on the argument, and now he sat and listened the way a vessel listens, which is to say with all of himself.

Isra knelt in the middle of the floor and put her palms flat on the salt.

She had spent the day learning the city the way she had learned the flats, through the soles of things. The clinic above her was a weather of small counts: the stock children's rhythms going through their evening like a row of small bells, the counting woman's pencil, the Professor's chalk, the salt-stained clerk's page-turning, and under all of it, through forty feet of salt, the beat she had walked forty miles to lie down beside. The city still did not feel like a place to her. It felt like a ledge on a very tall dive, and the water under the ledge was asleep, and everyone on the ledge went about their counts as if the water were a floor. She was not afraid for herself. She had made her arrangement with the deep a long time ago, on the flats, in the dark, in the only currency she had left to offer. She was afraid the way the slate had said: for the one below. Because the mother was coming, and the mother had never once, in all the century of her accounting, sent anything ahead of her that was not a bill.

The floor was cold, and the cold was a door. She had known that since the flats; she had walked forty miles of it with her feet split and the world's whole underside talking to her through the soles. Here, in the Saltstairs, the salt under the cellar had answered the deep music at a half-step down since before the fall, and tonight the deep was rising to meet the session the way a tide rises to a channel, and the petal at her collarbone opened its edges and drank the dark, and her hand began to move.

She did not write the first line. It arrived in her hand, the way the water-arrivals had arrived on the flats: chalk pressed to slate, letters cut slow and deep, the root paying for the ink out of her warmth, a coin per stroke, her fingers going colder line by line, and at the little table Milla Ferun copied the words as they landed, in clerk's hand, square, dated, initialled, the first deposition the deep had ever given the surface.

Ilay stood at the wall with her ear against it and her eyes shut, and she was the second channel. What the stone said, Ilay said, in her spare carried voice, word for word, a beat behind the chalk; and when the chalk and the girl disagreed, they never disagreed — the two accounts reconciled to the digit, and Vahid, watching both, wrote one word on his own slate and underlined it twice: BALANCED.

THE ACCOUNT OF A CENTURY, the slate began, and under it, in the deep-cut letters that cost Isra warmth by the stroke:

THERE WAS A WORLD BEFORE THIS ONE.

Then the testimony came, and the room took it the way the city took weather, in silence, in counts.

The world before: wide, tilled to its own horizons, one garden's weather from edge to edge. Ilay gave its measure in the Sister's own units — so many rows, so many keepers, one sky — and Isra's hand gave what Ilay's could not: the smell of it, green and turned and expensive, a world that had cost something to plant. Then the Burning. Ilay said: it came up out of the ground. Isra's chalk wrote: WE PLANTED IT. WE DID NOT KNOW WHAT WE WERE PLANTING. It burned from the root up — the root, the first root, the thing the garden had been grown over like a bandage over a wound no one had ever looked at — and the sky took the fire and gave it back as ash, and the ash fell for a generation, and the keepers planted into the ash, and the rows would not take, and they planted again.

Isra had seen the rows. Everyone in the room who had touched the contact had seen the rows, and the figure in the white gloves at the burning's center, and nobody in the room said so aloud, and the not-saying had a price, and they paid it.

THE GARDENER HAD A DAUGHTER, the slate wrote then, and the cold under Isra's palms came up through her knees into her teeth.

THE DAUGHTER WAS SET ABOVE THE DEEP, AS A LIVING LID. THE DOOR CLOSED. IT DID NOT OPEN AGAIN.

And then the amendment — the Sister correcting her own deposition the way an auditor restates an entry, and Ilay's voice going very quiet to carry it: the flight was first. The flight preceded the lid. She had fled the Burning down the deep roads with the sky coming apart above her, seed and child together, down into the salt, down past the sleeping thing, up the other side of it, into the vault-dark under this city when this city was stone and salt and nothing — and there the flight had ended, or been ended, the grammar of it not hers to give: locked above the deep as a living lid, door closed, did not open again. Accident of history, Isra's chalk wrote, because the Sister's bitterness had a bookkeeping format: NO MALICE IN THE LOCKING. ONLY ARITHMETIC. THE LID WAS WHAT THE LEDGER REQUIRED. THE DAUGHTER WAS WHAT WAS ON HAND.

The lamplight stood still in the room. Down through the floor, immense, patient, one beat behind the two-hearted man's, the risen heartbeat held its place, and under it, older, the other beat went on, regular as interest, and Isra understood for the first time in her diving life what she had been listening to through the soles of her feet: the lid's floor. The sleeper under the lid. The Sister had been lying on the thing for a hundred years the way a lid lies on a pot, feeling it dream through her bones, and the room above her head — every room, all of them, the whole stacked canyon of Ashkarad — was the pot's other half.

A CENTURY AGO, the slate went on, NINE LIGHTS WENT OUT OVER THE SALT.

Nine little sisters, sown. Ilay gave the words that had come down through the stone a century ago, and Isra felt the room hear them land one at a time: "Go. Find your hearts. And bloom." Nine seeds in lead and glass, carried out over the flats to nine cities, and the Sister had lain in the dark and counted them go — nine small heartbeats crossing the world above her, faint, lantern-bright, one by one by one, until the ninth crossed last and the dark was the dark again, and her count had begun, because there was nothing else left to keep.

There had been tenderness in it once, the slate made Isra write, and the writing of that line cost more warmth than the number had: SHE CALLED US HER LITTLE ROOTS WHEN SHE PLANTED US. IT WAS NOT A LIE. IT WAS NOT UNKIND. IT WAS A GARDENER'S WORD FOR A THING SHE MEANT TO GROW AND GATHER. Isra's fingers ached around the chalk. A hundred years below, and the eldest's ledger still gave the mother her due: the tenderness and the harvest had been the same word, said in the same voice, at the same hearth, and only the schedule had been kept from the daughters.

RECLAIM, Vahid said then, into the pause, in the voice he saved for dissection, the word already in his mouth like a blade he wanted to be done holding. And Isra's hand wrote for a long time.

Not a word for a debt. A word for a mouth. The bloomed cities — stamped, all eight, BLOOMED—TRUE THRONE — were not colonies. They were ripe. TRUE THRONE, Ilay said, in the Sister's reading, meant the fruit had come in; the seed had bloomed through its city and the bloom could be cut. The round was a harvest schedule. The rendered bloom went down the deep roads, cask after cask after cask — Isra's chalk wrote the arithmetic and Milla's pencil copied it and Suri's flat voice said, to the table, without inflection, "Bloom-tallow," and then said nothing else, because there was nothing else — and the Root drank, and dreamed, and the dreaming was the point: THE ROOT MUST NOT WAKE. THE FEED KEEPS THE DREAM. THE SONG KEEPS THE DREAM. FEED THE SLEEPER. SING IT DOWN. HOLD THE LID.

City by city. The Sister had heard each pruning through the stone: the song of a city's bloom rising, the round arriving to cut, the seed coming out of the living Throne like a tooth, and everything that had ripened poured down the deep roads to the mouth below. She had counted those too. She gave Brack in one line — HEARD IT. FELT THE TOOTH. COUNTED THE SCREAM. — and Vell, at Isra's feet, put her chalk down flat on her slate and did not pick it up again for the rest of the session.

Suri, without being asked, opened her ledger to the column headed EVIDENCE and read the room the line she had carried out of a burning granary two cities and a lifetime ago, in the small vertical hand of a third, patient party: PER THE RECLAIM — SUSTAIN THE ROUTE. She read it twice, at working volume, and closed the book, and Isra's chalk wrote the confirmation under the harvest arithmetic in letters that came slow and deep: THE ROUTE IS SUSTAINED. THE ROUTE IS THE FEEDING. YOU HAVE BEEN CARRYING A SPOON AND CALLING IT A ROAD.

RECLAIM IS THE MOUTH'S WORD, the slate finished. IT IS NOT A COLONY'S WORD. IT IS NOT A LEDGER'S WORD. IT IS WHAT THE HUNGER IS CALLED IN THE BOOKS OF THE ONE WHO FEEDS IT.

And then, because the deposition was an account and every account closes: the count.

She has numbered the sleeper's heartbeats since the Burning. Every one, in the dark, through a hundred years of lid and the pouring of the ripened cities, exact, unbroken, the only column that had never once been wrong. Ilay spoke it with her ear to the wall and her hand pressed flat beside her head, digit by digit, the way she gave depths: two million, six hundred and thirty-one thousand, four hundred and eight. Isra wrote it in full across the slate's width, the chalk gone grey, her fingers beyond feeling, the root's warmth spent down to the wax of a candle, and Milla read it back for the record, and Vahid checked it against nothing, because there was nothing in the world to check it against but itself, and it balanced.

Then Isra's hand moved once more, on its own, the way water arrives, and wrote the line under the number, and the line was small, and the room has never, in Isra's hearing, been as loud as the room was when Milla read it out:

LAST WEEK. FOR THE FIRST TIME. IT SKIPPED ONE.

The lamps burned. The number sat on the slate with one beat missing out of a century of counting, and every person in the cellar who could count — and in Ashkarad, everyone could count — sat with the same arithmetic arriving in them: a missed payment in a ledger this old meant one of two things. Either the sleeper was failing.

Or it was done waiting.
