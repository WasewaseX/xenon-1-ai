# Chapter 13 — Nerve

The silence in the Saltstairs hideout was a physical thing, a thick wool blanket pressed over the mouth. Ilay sat in the corner, tracing a finger along the floor's cracked salt-glaze, her small face turned away from the group. Suri counted the seconds between her own breaths, her carbon-scaled arm wrapped around her knees. Vahid polished his spectacles with a stained handkerchief, though they hadn't been dirty. Nobody spoke. They had all learned the same lesson in the same city: silence was never free, and this one was already accruing.

Then it started.

A high, thin note from the Choratorium, miles away and yet inside Dar's skull. A low thrum from a harvest-floor beneath the Undergleam. A chorus of screams from the ledgers, compressed into a single, vibrating chord. They layered over each other, a cathedral of pain laid across Ashkarad like a second map, invisible but inescapable — every street a nerve, every nerve an invoice, the whole city one enormous body being read aloud. Dar's hands clenched on the dusty floorboards, his nails splintering against the salt-glaze.

"Dar?" Suri's voice was a knife through the noise. "You're white as a ledgerman's sheet."

He couldn't answer. The noise wasn't sound; it was pressure, a physical force pushing against his bones, an ocean that had decided his skeleton was seabed. Each choir organ was a different instrument, a different agony played in a different key. The Choratorium's was a pipe organ, solemn and cruel. The ledgers were a shrieking violin section. Somewhere, a debtor's heart was failing, a slow, mournful cello that vibrated in his teeth. He gagged, bile rising in his throat, and somewhere beneath the nausea the Lily lay very still, listening the way a collector listens.

Vahid was beside him, his hands on Dar's temples, steadying them the way you would steady a shaking lamp. "Focus, boy," he said, his voice a calm island in the storm. "Don't listen to the song. Listen to the silence between the notes. Build a door."

A door. In the noise. The idea was absurd, impossible. The cathedral filled every inch of Dar's awareness; there was no room in it for a door, no room in it, he realized with distant horror, for a Dar.

"Close your eyes," Vahid instructed. "Imagine a wall. Thick stone. No windows. No doors."

Dar tried. He pictured the salt walls of the hideout, but they dissolved into the screaming of the ledgers. He pictured Saint Ferrous Hospital — its wards, its smell of clean linen laid over old sins — and it became the rhythmic thump of a harvest-floor piston.

"No," Vahid said, his grip tightening. "Not a place. A concept. A barrier. Think of it as a surgeon's field. Small. Contained."

A surgeon's field. Draped cloth. One opening, and everything outside it surrendered. Dar focused on the feeling of his own skin. The texture of his shirt against his collarbone. The warmth of his own breath on his lips. He held onto that tiny sensation, a single thread in the hurricane. Slowly, painstakingly, he built a wall around it, a wall of pure will, stitch by stitch, the way he closed a wound he could not afford to reopen. The screaming of the city didn't fade, but it... receded. It was still there, pressing against his barrier like floodwater against a door, but it was no longer inside.

"Good," Vahid said, his voice strained. "Now, open a crack. Just enough to hear one street."

Dar did. He eased a corner of the wall, and a flood of information rushed in. Not pain, not yet. Just the mundane symphony of the Saltstairs: the cough of a dying woman in the apartment above, the frantic pulse of a rat in the wall, the slow, steady beat of a child sleeping in the next room. He focused on the child, the pure, untroubled rhythm, four beats to the bar of sleep. He made that his door.

The hideout rushed back into focus. Suri was staring at him, her eyes wide. Ilay had stopped tracing the floor and was watching him with unnerving stillness. Vahid let out a breath he'd been holding.

"The Nerve Vein," he said, wiping his brow. "It opens the city to you. Every living thing, singing its own song. Most drown in it. You... you built a raft."

Dar was sweating, his heart hammering against his ribs. The Lily inside him pulsed in time with it, a cool, steady counterpoint to his own panic. It wasn't afraid. It was... curious.

They spent the next days in a state of controlled chaos. With his doors cracked, Dar could triangulate the hidden harvest-floors the Chorus used when the official tithe-barrys were too slow. Suri would case the location, her carbon arm a silent weapon, and Vahid would find a way to cut the power or jam the doors. Dar would stand by, his senses narrowed to a single street, listening for the tell-tale thrum of the harvesting machinery. It was, he had to admit, the closest he had come since Saint Ferrous to feeling like a surgeon again — one patient at a time, one street at a time, the whole screaming city held at arm's length by a wall of will that he rebuilt each morning like a wound dressed fresh.

The first liberation was brutal. A converted warehouse in the Marrowworks, where three debtors were strung up like sides of beef, their bodies chalked with annotations — yield, grade, balance outstanding — in the same contented hand Dar remembered from the registry. Their screams were a physical assault even through his doors. Suri moved like smoke, disabling the guards with swift, economical strikes. Vahid worked at the restraints with a bone-saw he'd carried for decades, the old steel singing softly through the pins. None of the three thanked them. One wept. One laughed until the laughing turned into something else, and the third simply mouthed the terms of his new debt as if naming the price could make it his.

They didn't free them into the city. They couldn't. The Wardens would scoop them up for new debts before their feet remembered the streets. Instead, they guided them to a pre-arranged drop with a syndicate that owed Suri a favor. The price of their freedom was a new debt, a smaller one, to the Gavel. It was a choice between the Chorus's knife and the syndicate's ledger. They took the ledger. Dar understood the arithmetic even as it sickened him: they hadn't saved those three. They had transferred the account.

They did it again. And again. Each time, the city's song shifted. The screams of the harvest-floors were replaced by the frantic pulse of gang wars, the territorial drumming of syndicate enforcers. They were tipping the underworld, and the city was singing about it. Some nights the new song was almost worse. Greed had a brighter timbre than pain, and it carried farther.

But the cost was intimacy. Suri sat beside Dar one evening, cleaning her carbon scales with a rag, each stroke rasping like a file across tin. He flinched.

"What?" she asked, not looking up.

"Your pulse," he said. "It's like a hammer on a tin roof."

She stopped, her hand hovering over her arm. "It's always been like that."

"Not to me. Now I can't unhear it. I can't unfeel it. The man in the apartment above us has a heart murmur that sounds like a teakettle. The rat in the wall has a fever. Everything is a noise, and everything is a feeling."

Suri was quiet for a long time, and he could hear the quiet itself — her breath held, her heart not slowing at all, only being counted. "Count it," she finally said. "Count the beats. Make it a number instead of a sound."

He tried. It helped, a little. But the numbers had their own rhythm, a cold, mathematical pulse that was just as invasive as the biological one. He had escaped the choir only to discover the choir kept a ledger too.

Ilay was different. She had gone nearly wordless; when she spoke at all now, it was only to name what she had drawn. On scraps of paper, with charcoal she'd found, she made maps. Not of streets, but of what she called "the singing." She would draw a cluster of sharp, angry lines and say, "The Choratorium." A series of slow, heavy circles: "The ledgers." And between them, a space that was empty, a silence in the noise.

"The city's song has a shape," Dar told Vahid one night, showing him Ilay's drawing. "And the shape has a hole."

Vahid frowned, tracing the empty space with a grimy finger. "The hole is where the First Singer should be," he said, his voice low. "The Chorus's secret. The thing they're building beneath the Marrowworks."

The next day, they went deeper. Using Ilay's crude map as a guide, they tunneled through service corridors beneath the industrial belt, following the thrum of machinery that fed the city's vital-debt system. They were looking for the central harvest-floor, the one that powered the entire operation. The one that must be connected to the First Singer.

The air grew thick with the smell of ozone and something else. Something organic. Old. They followed the sound of it, a deep, resonant thrum that vibrated in Dar's bones, a bass note beneath the city's screaming treble, so low it seemed less like a sound than like a property of the earth itself.

They found a grate in the floor of an abandoned maintenance tunnel. Below it, darkness. But the sound was louder here, a physical presence in the air, a pressure on the eardrum like deep water standing on the chest.

"Can you hear it?" Vahid asked, his face pale in the dim light of their lamp.

Dar nodded, closing his eyes. He opened his doors wider, letting the cathedral of pain flood in, but focused on the sound below. It wasn't an organ. It wasn't a choir. It was something else. Something vast. Slow. Older than the Church. Older, he suspected, than the debts it inspired.

"It's not an organ," he whispered. "It's... alive."

They pried the grate open. Below was a shaft, dropping into absolute blackness. The thrumming was coming from down there, a sound that felt more like geological time than biology.

"I'm going down," Dar said, before either of them could protest.

The ladder was rusted, rungs slick with condensation. He descended into the darkness, the Lily in his chest growing warmer with each step, as if greeting a relative. He did not like knowing that the warmth was comfort, and he liked even less that it was. The thrumming grew louder, filling his entire world. It wasn't a sound he heard with his ears; it was a vibration he felt in his teeth, in his bones, in the very marrow of his spine.

He reached the bottom. The space was vast, bigger than the Choratorium. The air was cold and still, smelling of wet earth and something ancient and sweet. In the center of the chamber, pulsing with a slow, rhythmic light, was something that defied description. It was a mass of tissue, vast and slow, like a buried heart or a dreaming brain. It was the source of the thrumming, the furnace the Chorus fed.

And it was awake.

The thrumming stopped.

For a single, perfect second, there was only silence. The silence of the deep earth, the silence before a star is born. Then, the thing pulsed again. But differently. It was no longer a steady, furnace-like beat. It was a question. A slow, deliberate thump that seemed to echo in the chamber's stone.

Thump.

Dar's own heart answered.

Thump.

It was a rhythm he knew. A lullaby. The one his mother used to sing.

Thump.

The thing pulsed again, matching his heartbeat exactly. It was amused. Ancient. And it was listening.
