# Chapter 2 — The Courier

The door to Dar's tenement cell groaned on its rusted hinges, admitting not light but the scent of rain and carbon-scales. Suri Kaveh stood framed in the doorway, her right arm sheathed in those scales like armor, a ledger tucked under her other arm. Her eyes, sharp as broken glass, swept the room—once, twice, then fixed on Dar's chest where two heartbeats struggled beneath his skin. She had the look of a collector who has come for a payment and found the account doing something accounts should not do.

"You're breathing," she said, not a question but a statement that cost her three steps into the room. She counted them aloud. "Three steps. Fourteen tiles to the door. One dead man breathing. That's a ledger imbalance, Mehran."

Dar sat up, the movement sending a familiar ache through his ribs where the Lily had knitted him back together. "Death notices get filed wrong sometimes. Especially in the Saltstairs."

"Not this wrong." She tossed the ledger onto his cot. It landed with a wet thud, the pages swollen with humidity, ink feathering at the edges where the damp had gotten in—the city got into everything eventually, even the records of what people owed. "Your debt's called in. Thirty days. Saint Ferrous Hospital wants their payment or they send you to the ledgers." She tapped the ledger with a fingernail painted black. "I saw your death notice posted myself. Two days ago. Room 7, third floor. Same building."

Dar didn't touch the ledger. The Lily inside him pulsed, a cold knot against his ribs. It liked Suri's presence—her warmth, the steady rhythm of her blood, the way she filled the small space with her carbon-scented energy. He could feel it leaning toward her, a plant toward a window. He kept his hands flat on his knees and pretended he couldn't feel it leaning.

"Someone made a mistake," Dar said, though the lie tasted like ash in his mouth.

"No mistakes in ledgers, Mehran. Only entries." Suri circled the room, her boots whispering on the concrete. She stopped before the barred window, peering through the salt-fogged glass at the vertical city rising above them, tier upon tier of lit windows like a wall of open accounts. "I came to collect from a corpse. Instead I find a man with two heartbeats." She turned, her eyes narrowing. "Tell me how many heartbeats you have, Dar Mehran."

"One," Dar said automatically. The Lily contracted at the word, a sharp pang in his chest, as if the lie were a physical thing he'd swallowed sideways.

"Wrong answer." Suri stepped closer, close enough for Dar to see the tiny scars around her eyes—places where skin had been traded for grafts, margin notes written on her face in a ledger she never discussed. "I count everything. Steps. Debts. Heartbeats. Yours don't match the pulse-readers at the hospital. They show one. But I hear two."

Dar's throat went dry. The Lily pulsed again, this time with a different rhythm—curious, probing. It had been quiet since the heist, content in the darkness of his cell, feeding on the blood he'd lost during his recovery. But Suri's proximity had woken it, the way a struck tuning fork wakes its twin across a room.

"What do you want, Suri?" Dar asked, keeping his voice even.

"The truth," she said, "and a favor."

Dar waited.

"You're a dead man walking, Mehran. Either to the ledgers or to something worse. I can make your death notice stick. Paperwork's easy when you know the right people. In return, you run a package for me. High-grain insulin, unlicensed anesthetic, stolen sleep—through the Undergleam tonight."

"The Undergleam?" Dar frowned. "That's the Cartographer's territory. Too many eyes."

"Exactly why it's perfect." Suri smiled, a flash of white teeth in the dim light. "The Cartographer sees everything, but notices nothing if you know how to move. And you, Mehran, have hands that move quiet. I remember."

Dar did too. Six months ago, Suri had come to Saint Ferrous with her arm torn open—carbon scales ripped away, muscle and bone exposed. She'd paid in blood for a quick stitch, the minimum the tithe would buy, but Dar had worked longer than necessary, carefully layering sutures beneath the scales, reinforcing the graft, doing the kind of slow work the hospital never billed for because it never counted as payment. It had cost him three vials of his own blood to the hospital tithe, but he'd done it anyway. She had never thanked him. She had also never forgotten. With Suri, those were the same thing.

"Why me?" Dar asked, though he already suspected the answer.

"The Lily likes me," Suri said, her eyes dropping to his chest. "It's singing to me. Soft, but clear. A debt-singer's song."

Dar didn't deny it. Instead, he stood, the movement sending a wave of dizziness through him. The Lily stabilized him, a second heartbeat steadying his balance, correcting his blood pressure like a clerk quietly fixing a misfiled column. "One run. That's all."

"One run," Suri agreed, "and your death notice gets filed as 'natural causes.' No questions asked." She extended her hand. Not to shake, but for him to seal the bargain. In the palm of her hand, a small flower was drawn in chalk—the same flower Dar had seen chalked on his neighbor's doorframe that morning.

Dar hesitated, then pressed his palm against hers. The chalk smeared, leaving a gray smear on both their hands. The Lily pulsed once, hard, as if in approval. He tried not to think about what it meant, that the thing in his chest witnessed his contracts now.

Suri retrieved the parcel from a satchel under her ledger and pushed it against his chest until he took its weight. "Wire seal stays intact," she said. "If you open it, I open you. Thirty days of debt canceled, one night of work. Simple arithmetic."

The Undergleam smelled of damp stone, lamp oil, and something sweet—rotten fruit left too long in the dark. Suri moved through the narrow alleyways with the confidence of someone who knew every crack in the pavement, every shadow that shifted. Dar followed, his own steps quieter than he remembered, the Lily in his chest humming a counterpoint to Suri's counted footsteps. Between them he carried the package: a wrapped parcel of medical tins bound with wire, sealed at the seams with black wax, light as bread and worth more than his lungs.

"Sixty-three steps to the first drop," she whispered, not looking back. "Then a right through the spice market. The air gets thick there—cumin and regret. Don't breathe deep."

Dar nodded, keeping his eyes on the back of her carbon-scaled arm. The scales shifted in the dim light of the gas lamps, each one catching the glow like a tiny mirror. He wondered how much of her she'd traded away for those scales—skin, muscle, years of life. The Lily seemed curious about them too, its pulse quickening whenever she moved her arm, a dog straining at the end of a leash only Dar could feel.

They reached the spice market, a narrow canyon between crumbling buildings where air hung thick with the scent of a hundred different spices. Dar did as Suri warned, breathing shallowly, but the Lily inside him seemed to thrive on the dense air, pulling it in through his skin, feeding on the scent of cumin and turmeric and something that smelled like burnt sugar. He felt wider. That was the only word for it. As if the walls of his body had been moved outward a handspan and the world was being poured into the extra room.

"Left here," Suri said, ducking through an archway that led into darkness. "The Cartographer's territory."

The Undergleam opened before them—a warren of tunnels and stalls lit by sputtering lamps and the phosphorescent glow of certain fungi growing on the walls. Merchants sold information, stolen goods, and memories. One stall had jars of preserved fears, another of forgotten dreams, each jar labeled in a spidery hand and priced by weight. Suri moved through the crowd with practiced ease, her carbon scales parting the way like a ship's bow.

"Twenty-seven steps to the canal," she counted, not bothering to lower her voice. "Then we'll be in the clear. The Cartographer doesn't deal in contraband—only maps. We're safe."

Dar wasn't so sure. Every shadow seemed to hold eyes, every flicker of light seemed to reveal faces that turned to follow their progress. The Lily in his chest was singing now—a low, resonant hum that vibrated through his ribs and into his bones. It was the same song it had sung to Suri, but now Dar could hear it too, and it was calling him forward, toward something in the darkness ahead. He wanted to ask it to stop. He suspected it wouldn't. Debts, the Lily seemed to say, in a language older than ledgers. Everything here was payment for something.

They reached the drainage canal—a wide trench carved into the floor of the Undergleam, where runoff from the Saltstairs emptied into the sea. The air here was thick with the smell of salt and decay, and the water in the canal was black and still, a page no one had written on yet. A crowd had gathered at the railing—merchants, couriers, a few Syndic Wardens who'd clearly been paid to look the other way. All of them were staring into the water, their faces illuminated by the sputtering lamps, and not one of them was speaking. Silence, in the Undergleam, cost more than any of them could afford. They were paying it anyway.

"What is that?" Dar asked, though he already knew. The Lily was singing louder now, a chorus of one.

Suri stopped dead, her carbon scales going still. Her face, usually sharp and alert, had gone pale as salt. "No," she whispered. "Not her."

In the dark water of the canal, something was moving. Not a body, but something alive. Wet, black petals unfurled from the stone of the canal wall, pulsing with a soft, internal light, opening and closing like slow gills. Between them, something sang—a clear, harmonic voice that rose and fell with the rhythm of the tides. It was a courier's voice, familiar and terrible. A voice that used to count deliveries, swear at dockhands, laugh at bad jokes over cheap liquor. It was singing now the way glass sings when it is about to break.

Suri took a step back, her hand going to her throat. "That's Lena," she said, her voice cracking. "Lena Vance. Went into a tithe-clinic last spring. Said it was for a routine organ harvest. Never signed out."

The singing continued, beautiful and terrible, as the black petals in the canal wall pulsed in time with the melody. Dar watched, mesmerized, as the Lily in his chest began to answer the song—a counterpoint that resonated through his bones and into the stones beneath his feet. It was the same song, but different—older, deeper, full of questions it had been waiting to ask. The crowd at the railing did not move. Somewhere behind him, a jar of preserved fears cracked in its seller's hands.

Suri stared at the singing canal, her face ashen. "We have to go," she said, her voice barely a whisper. "Now."

But Dar couldn't move. His hand had drifted to his chest without his permission, pressing flat over the place where the Lily lived. The Lily was singing to the singing, and he was caught between them, a bridge of flesh and blood in a city that ran on debt and song. In the darkness of the Undergleam, surrounded by the scent of salt and decay, Dar Mehran finally understood the price of his survival. It wasn't just his own life anymore. It was the lives of others, drawn into the orbit of the symbiote in his chest, singing a song he had no choice but to follow.
