# Chapter 43 — The Drowned Choir

Lowmark announced itself by its rooftops. They rose out of the flats for an hour before anything else of the city did — chimneys first, then ridge-lines, then the grey backs of roofs ranked down the old hillside like scales on a submerged animal — and by the time Dar stood close enough to see the doors, he understood what the route stones had been politely not saying. The city was drowned to the second story. The sea had taken the ground floors a lifetime ago and never given them back, and Lowmark had adjusted its books rather than argue: ladders and gangplanks ran where streets had been, boats were moored to chimney-stacks, attic doors opened onto rope walkways slung from ridge to ridge, and the whole town went about its business at the height of a gull. Down in the flood-line, under green glass water and sheets of recent mud, the drowned first stories stood furnished and open, shopfronts dark, salt crust on every shelf — with the tide out this far, you could walk on the roofs of the drowned street and look down into the rooms of the drowned houses, and the people of Lowmark had built their walkways precisely so that they would not have to.

The harbor was at the bottom of everything, and the harbor was occupied.

The seed had bloomed in it like a black water-lily the size of a district. Dar saw it from the ridge of the salt-warehouse and stood still for a count of ten, because there was a limit to what a man should take in at one gulp. Petals the size of sails lay slicked back across the drained harbor bed, black at the heart, grey-edged with dry salt, ranked in whorls, and at the center of the last whorl stood the flower's true middle — a dark the eye kept sliding off, a closed bud the size of a chapel — and out of that middle came the hum. He felt it in his teeth before he heard it with his ears. The whole city sat inside the sound the way a thing sits inside its own name.

He did what Vahid had taught him and built the door: narrowed the Nerve Vein's hearing from the city's entire chorus down to one instrument, then one section, then one line. Under the hum there were voices. Dozens. Then, when he let the door stand wider, more than dozens — a congregation of them, braided into the harmonic the way interest is braided into a debt, each voice a person who had walked down the petal-road on a wedding day and been taken into the flower's keeping. The tide-priests told the town that the married still sang. The tide-priests, for once in the history of clergy, were telling the truth.

The priests were everywhere above the flood-line. They wore grey-blue robes hemmed in wax that had been sewn and resewn until the hems hung like candle-drip, and they walked the planks in processions of two and four, and from a pulpit of stacked hull-timbers at the head of the harbor stair, one of them preached the day's arithmetic to a roof-edge congregation. Dar and Suri stopped to hear it, because you always audit a man's doctrine before you audit his books.

"The sea withdraws because the Bride is hungry," the priest said. He had a good voice, kept like a ledger line, and he did not raise it. "The waters go to whoever feeds her, and we have fed her thin. Look at the harbor. Look at the mud. The sea is counting our devotion, neighbors, and finding it short."

The congregation looked at the mud. The mud was very lookable; it went to the horizon.

"Marriage," the priest said, "is the tithe that fills the account. The tide comes home to a faithful household. Ask of the Bride, and she will ask of you, and the ledger of the world will balance." He said it the way Vey used to say things, Dar thought — warmly, maternally, with the whole price list folded inside the tenderness — and then he rebuked himself for the comparison, because Vey was a residue in another city and this man had grown his own.

They lodged among salvage-divers, because the priests' hospice charged by the prayer and the divers charged by the week. Maren's dive-house was a second-story hall built over a drowned chandlery, floorboards strong, windows glazed with what had once been ship's glass, and a stair of rope and spar running down into the open air of the drained street. Maren herself was sixty if she was a day, hands like knotted mooring line, a voice pitched to carry over weather. She looked at Dar's surgeon's hands for exactly as long as it took to price them, looked at Suri's ruined arm for exactly as long as it took to believe it, looked at Vell for longer than either.

"Counting-house?" she said to Vell.

Vell wrote on her slate: BRACK.

Maren's face did something complicated and then settled into the expression of a woman updating an account. "Brack," she said. "We heard Brack's number. Zero." She jerked her chin at the harbor. "Ours is going to be worse. Sit. You'll pay in work. The little one dives, the big one — whatever she does — and the cutter sets hands."

"The cutter sets hands," Dar agreed.

So the week set itself. Dar worked the dive-house's common room each morning, and the divers came to him the way debtors used to come to Saint Ferrous — except that here nobody was billed. Crushed fingers from hauling on the drowned street's doors. A knee that had gone the way of a bad hinge. A child's ear, ruined by a dive into the flooded cistern under the old customhouse. He stitched, and splinted, and reduced, and the clinic of the Saltstairs reassembled itself in him piece by piece, the muscle memory of the unpriced transaction, and the divers began leaving things by his bench without being asked — a pot of rendered fish-oil, a spool of waxed thread, once, wet from the dive, a child's shoe.

He learned the economy in self-defense, because Suri had learned it first and would not stop narrating it.

"Four hundred and six," she said, on the third evening, lying on the bunk with her boots off and her ledger open on her chest. "That's the casks that went up the inland road since we came. The first caravan was thirty-one casks, two factors, one outrider. Today's was twenty-eight casks, same two factors."

"And the marriages?" Dar said, from the bench, winding thread.

"Two, since we came. The old diver's son, and a woman from the roof-row. Two marriages in, seventy-one casks out." Suri turned her head and looked at him with her flat ledger eyes. "The Bride weeps a lot, Mehran. More than the weddings take. Either she's grieving by the ton, or somebody's milking the flower and calling it doctrine."

The tallow was the why of everything here. He had seen it rendered — the tide-priests let the town see it, that was the display of it: great copper pans behind the harbor temple, the grey-gold grease skinned off the pans and poured into casks, each cask stamped before it went up the road. Bloom-tallow, the divers called it. It burned longer than any wax Maren had ever bought, burned with a low cold light that never guttered, and it glued, and it caulked, and it burned. The seed wept it after every marriage, from the broken edges of the petals, the way a wound weeps — and the priests collected the weeping, rendered it, and sold it inland by the caravan-load. Lowmark's god had been turned into an industry, and the industry's fuel was funerals conducted as weddings.

Tetch, the youngest of Maren's crew — a wiry boy with a diver's swollen knuckles and a voice like a gull — explained the doctrine's economics from the doorway, spitting carefully between every sentence, because the divers spat toward the harbor on principle.

"Marriage gets you heaven, they say. Your girl walks down the petal-road, the flower takes her, and now she sings in the harbor, and you can hear her if you're good." He spat. "My brother's up there. In the chord. He used to sing flat." A pause. "He still does."

The marriage they witnessed was on the fifth dusk, and Dar could not make himself look away from it, because he had spent his life learning to watch a procedure without flinching and he refused to stop being useful now. A bride of the roof-row walked down the petal-road in wax-sealed cloth, attended by priests swinging lamps, and the town watched from every ridge and chimney like a congregation watching a tithe-cart leave. She did not struggle. That was the worst of it — she walked like a woman who had reviewed the terms, and at the end of the road the petals curled up and over her with a slowness that was almost courteous, and the hum of the drowned choir gained one voice, and the harbor's note came up richer by exactly one singer, and the crowd made a sound that was grief and satisfaction filed under the same word.

Suri stood beside him on the ridge and counted the hymn's bars under her breath. "Forty bars," she said. "Shorter than Ashkarad's. They're economizing on the god."

Vell wrote, and turned the slate: THEY ARE PAID IN CANDLES.

It was after, as the crowd thinned, that the readings began. The priests set a canopy of waxed cloth at the foot of the harbor stair, and under the canopy they set a table, and on the table they set a box. The box was lead, small, worn dull at the corners by a century of hands, and the sight of it did something to the back of Dar's neck before his mind caught up and told him why: lead was for keeping things from the world, and things kept from the world were kept by people who feared them.

A queue formed. Pilgrims, roof-row widows, divers off the rope-walks, anyone with a coin or a memory to sell. For a price, the priest at the table opened the box — Dar saw the inside of the lid, lined in more lead, and a cloth of the greyest grey — and took out what it held, and pressed it to the pilgrim's cupped hands, and the pilgrim's face would go somewhere else entirely. Every one of them came back from that somewhere weeping, or smiling, or both, and the priest would fold the thing back into its cloth and say, "There. You have seen her morning. She wakes, she breaks her bread, she is content. The married want for nothing."

"What is it?" Suri asked, low, watching the box like a creditor watches a strongbox.

The priest at the table answered over the head of his next customer, in the warm administrative voice, as if reading from a notice: "The Bride's relic. Her mercy. A leaf of her own keepsake, preserved in the first salt of the first flood. Readings are two coppers, or one small memory of your own, freely given." He smiled at them both with the smooth confidence of a man whose till has never once come up short. "For the mourning, sisters and brothers. The dead have mornings still. The Bride keeps them for us."

They walked back along the ridge in the dark, above the hum, above the choir, Suri's boots quick and even on the planks. At the door of the dive-house she stopped, and looked back once at the little canopy and its queue, and said it flat, the way she filed things:

"That's not a relic, Mehran."

"No," Dar said. His chest ached where the Lily lay banked and listening; all night she had been leaning toward the harbor, down, toward the petal-road, the way she had leaned at the deep vaults, and he had been leaning with her. "That's a page."
