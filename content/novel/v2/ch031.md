# Chapter 31 — The Salt-Glass Door

Vahid put the prohibition in writing, which in a man who had spent his life being discredited in writing was either irony or surrender to form. He chalked it on the cellar wall beneath the Vein-ladder, underlined it twice with his one hand, and stood back from it the way a man stands back from a fence he knows will not hold.

"The Mind Vein is open in you the way a door is open in a house whose owner is out," he said. "You have walked it once, cleanly, I'll grant the cleanliness. But the deep, boy — the deep is where a thing older than the Church learned your mother's lullaby off your own pulse. It counted back at you from a hundred and nine heartbeats and now it has added to the tally without your hand. You do not visit a creditor who writes in your ledger while you sleep."

"It's not a creditor," Dar said. "It knocked. I intend to look through the keyhole before it comes up the stairs."

"It is a *summons*," Vahid said, and then his voice did the thing it did when the gin burned down to the exactness underneath, the lecture swerving from drunkenness to surgical clarity mid-sentence. "You have in your chest the ninth of nine seeds. Below this city lies the vault where the first of them has been waiting out a century in the dark. Whatever is leaning on you leaned on her first, and she has been down there alone since before your grandmother defaulted on her own spine. You walk into that vault and you are not a surgeon, Dar. You are collateral."

"Then I'd rather be collateral in the room where the terms are read out," Dar said. "Ilay's map says *soon*, Professor. I've learned to price other people's 'soon.' It's always dearer than it looks."

Vahid looked at him for a long moment over the round, salt-scoured spectacles, mended with wire, and Dar watched him weigh the argument and lose it to the worse arithmetic on the other side — the tally that grew without a hand, the thread of light under a door that had no business being furnished, a girl's lettering that said *soon* in a child's careful loan-cursive. The Professor took up the chalk, and instead of underlining the prohibition a third time, he wrote the descent rules beneath it. Dosage, a physician calls it.

"Suri counts every step aloud and you do not correct her. You carry the girl's map and you do not follow it past the last circle she drew. You carry a lamp and you keep it *low* — darkness feeds her, boy, and I would rather not learn what she does at a feast. And you take this." He worked a stoppered phial out of his sleeve, tincture dark as ink. "Tincture of hawthorn and salt-wort. If her hum goes past your teeth, you drink it and you climb, and you do not stop to be polite."

"And you leave the girl above," he added, quieter, and the quiet was worse than the shout would have been. "The nursery taught her tissue to be compliant before she could write her own name. The deep knew her before it knew you. Whatever calls from under the salt, Dar — do not let it learn her address."

Dar took the phial. It was not agreement; it was witnessed dissent, which in the clinic's currency was the same thing accepted at par.

They went down through the Saltstairs at the dead hour, by Milla's route — the tithe-clerk's route, the way paperwork traveled when it wanted to reach the harvest floors without standing in line. She had drawn it for them on the back of a medicine slip: culvert stairs behind the cistern, the pump gallery, the clerks' door with its lock long since stolen and its silence never replaced. Above, the Gavel's tallymen marked the alley mouths with their chalk strokes; one of them watched the three of them — Dar, Suri, and Roya carrying Ilay's map in a wax-cloth roll — pass into the Marrowworks underbelly, and made a mark, and said nothing. Let it be counted, Dar thought. Everything in this city was counted. It was what got *collected* that mattered now.

Ilay had not argued to come. That was the part he kept turning over in the culvert dark. Roya had told her, in the flat metallic calm the brass mask gave every sentence, that the deep was not for children, and the girl had looked at Roya for a while — at the mask, or through it — and said only, "It's not for anyone. Take the circles. The numbers are how many steps. The circles are how loud." And then she had gone back to her charcoal, drawing the innermost ring one more time, bigger than the others, as if size were emphasis, as if *soon* had not been emphatic enough.

The Marrowworks was a drowned animal above them and a picked bone around them. Tithe-warehouses stood empty with their brass signs pried off; the harvest floors' long tables held only salt-fur and the ghosts of clamps; a tithe-clerk's station gaped open, three stamps still racked in their rest — Heart, Debt, Voluntary — drying out like dead urchins. Suri's lamp swung past it and her count did not falter.

"Forty," she said. "Forty-one. The manhole's at forty-four, map says forty-five, so somebody's moved the grate, which somebody's going to answer for— there." Iron clanged under her boot. "Forty-five."

Roya stopped at the manhole rim and did not follow them down. She had said her piece on the walk over in four words — *someone holds the top* — and held out the wax-cloth roll to Dar now with both scarred hands, the way you hand over something you have been carrying for someone else. He unrolled it by lamplight: the girl's spiral, ring inside ring, in copied chalk on good paper, the numbers running down the margin in her careful hand. The circles were loudness. The numbers were steps. Below the last circle, in a nine-year-old's lettering, the word *soon*, written twice.

"Severed or not," Roya said, metallic and quiet, from inside the mask, "nothing below learns your signal through me. That's worth one lantern at the rim. If you're not back by the time the oil burns to the wick, I will not come down after you. I will go and get the Professor, and the Professor will come down after you, and you will deserve him." She folded the empty wax-cloth and sat down on the rim with her back to the shaft, minding the door.

Below the clerks' door the stair became the shaft — the great bored throat, older than the Church above it, ringed with hand-holds worn to silver. Chalk marks climbed the crust of the first landings, scavengers' tallies and worse — a crude sun, a price list for glass, one line of a child's hand copying its own name over and over until the name ran out of wall — donors who had woken whole in a dead machine and walked up into a city that had no column for them. Most of the marks climbed. None descended. The old chart's margin was scratched into the salt crust at the first landing too, ledger-script in the Gardener's cramped certainty, and Dar read it by Suri's lamp with the words arriving one at a time like figures on a debt-slip: DO NOT COUNT THE HEARTBEATS.

"Two hundred and ten," Suri said, counting.

The margin was a century old and had never once been obeyed, which was itself evidence that anything down here kept books.

The First Vessel's chamber opened at the shaft's knee the way a cathedral opens — suddenly, sideways, space arriving before light. They stood on a gallery of salt-cut rock, lamps low, and looked across at it: the cold cathedral of flesh, forty years of harvested organs grafted and grafted again into one vast dreaming bulk, now a cathedral after the congregation has gone home and the god has been repossessed. The air in the chamber had the smell of a shop after closing — old biogel, cold glass, wax that had been warm too long — and the glass reliquaries studded into its flanks were dead, row on row of them, dull as the windows of a house whose debts died with it. The choir organs had gone silent in one song and stayed silent, and what silence they had left behind was not absence but pressure, the kind that makes the inner ear file a complaint. Its face was turned away from them. Too many eyes, all closed. Vey had promised the city a heart and been handed instead the bill for one, and the bill had come due on the night the altar broke.

Frost furred the nearest flank in slow grey petals. Even dead, things grew toward it. Nothing grew away.

"Six hundred and forty," Suri said softly. Her count had not wavered once since the clerks' door, and Dar understood now that the count was not bravery. It was ballast. A counter knows that whatever else is true, the numbers are hers, and no one — not gods, not ledgers, not things that count back — can repossess the numbers. "The map's last circle is at six-eighty. Beyond that she didn't draw. She said it was too loud to see."

They passed the dead First Vessel the way you pass a collection plate no one will ever pass again, and the shaft went on down.

· · ·

At six hundred and eighty steps the walls changed.

Salt had been under everything all along — grey crust, white fur, the weeping of a city built on a drowned sea — but here it became the whole substance, vault after vault of it, translucent as thick glass, and when Suri's boot scuffed a ripple of floor-salt the note it gave hung in the air for ten full seconds, a struck-glass chime that harmonized, when the great drum beneath them swelled, a quarter-tone flat. The vaults rang with their own footsteps. Every step was an entry; every entry was answered. They stopped speaking. Suri counted with her fingers against her thigh, and Dar watched her hand move and kept the map's last circle in his mind — too loud to see — and measured his progress toward it by the rising tide in his sternum.

The second heartbeat was no longer something he heard. It was something the vault did to him. It came up through the salt and the floor and the bones the Marrow Vein had made dense as coral, and it filled the dark the way a slow tide fills a cave — not arriving, *present*, patient and immense, one beat behind his own, one line down, entering after him.

"It's still one behind," he said. There was no reason to whisper. The vault was louder than both of them.

Suri considered that with the blank economy she gave all figures that refused to reconcile. "Then it owes you a beat," she said. "Everything in this city runs behind. Chapel bells. Wages. The tide. First thing down here that ever paid late instead of taking early." Her jaw tightened. "Don't start liking it."

The lamps burned low and leaned. Not out — *toward*, the way a plant leans, both flames bowing gently down the corridor into the deep dark as if the darkness there were a table set for them. Suri looked at the flames and then at him, and Dar tipped his lamp lower and walked on, because the tincture was in his coat and the rules were in his head and the appointment was in the girl's handwriting, and you do not break an appointment you were too late to decline.

At seven hundred and eleven steps the corridor ended, and the map was proved, and Ilay was right: it was too loud to see.

The door stood at the vault's end, and it was made of salt-glass clear as deep water, and behind it there was no wall. Behind it was darkness — not a shade but a presence, a darkness with mass and tide — and the second heartbeat filled that darkness like the sea fills a basin, slow, vast, unhurried, patient. The door had no handle. The door had no hinge he could find. It had only height and width and the cold shining of it, and the dark behind it breathed against the far side the way a sleeper breathes against a window in winter.

Suri's hand stopped counting. Her fingers curled closed.

"Seven hundred and eleven," she said, in a voice with no numbers left in it.

And pressed against the far side of the glass, level with Dar's chest — level, exactly, with the place where the Lily rooted in him and two hearts kept their separate books — was a single black petal, wide as his palm, veined in lace, waiting.
