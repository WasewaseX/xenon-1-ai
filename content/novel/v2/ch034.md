# Chapter 34 — The Surveyor's Kindness

The grace storm spent itself against the storm-walls for two days, and when it blew out, the wind that followed it brought the caravans.

They came up the shore road in the storm's wake the way they always had — League wagons riding three and four abreast, hulls of curved wicker under salt-stiffened canvas, their storm-sails furled along the spines like the fins of sleeping fish, draft-beasts steaming in the washed light. Ashkarad had traded with the Caravan League for longer than the Chorus had kept its ledgers; the League moved freight, grain, glass, and rumor over the hills and around the coast while the sea lanes sat empty, and a city that had just watched a page of pollen point seaward could be forgiven for watching the shore road instead.

The man who came to the clinic on the second morning after the storm arrived without escort, without letters of credit, and without — and this was the part that put every sense Dar owned on watch — without a price.

He was fifty-odd, weathered to the color of old rope, with a surveyor's compact build and the soft, precise hands of a man who had spent thirty years measuring things and never once harvesting them. Grey at the temples, grey-blue League coat brushed clean, a plumb-line coiled at his belt and a slate case under one arm. He stood in the clinic doorway among the smell of antiseptic and salt-damp stone, ducked his head to the lintel like a man entering a church of a different denomination, and introduced himself.

"Coram Hesh. Surveyor, out of the League, overland and coast. We've a custom after hard storms — a kindness, some call it, though I'd never presume — structural surveys, no charge, no obligation. Your district took the wind hard. The Saltstairs always does." He smiled, and the smile had patience in it the way some ledgers have patience, line after line, no hurry. "I'm told this house stitched up half the ward for free through the storm nights. It seemed the right place to start."

He is everything Vey was not, Dar thought, and the thought was the trap, and he walked into it with his eyes open. Vey had offered tea, terms, and endearments with the warmth of a stoved room in winter; Vey had priced the air between two cups. This man offered nothing but competence and asked nothing but the lint between the floorboards, and in Ashkarad that was so far outside the known market that it rang against the teeth like a coin too pure to be true.

"We'll take the survey," Dar said. "And you'll take payment."

"I won't."

"Then you'll take it from someone else. Nothing in this city is free, Surveyor. It's only billed late. You'll let me know whose account you're drawing on, and we'll get along."

Hesh looked at him for a moment — looked, Dar noted, not at the silvery veins at his collar but at his hands, a tradesman's glance, tool to tool — and inclined his head as if Dar had made a fair opening bid. "The League writes its own costs," he said mildly. "Call the payment an old debt of mine to this coast. I was here once, before your storm-walls went up. The city did right by me. Surveys are how I file the interest." He set his slate case on the door ledge, opened it, and took out chalk, wax-paper packets, a folding brass rule, and a small knife for taking samples, all of it worn to a shine. "Where hurts most?"

He worked for six days, and he was very good.

Dar watched him the way he watched a new drug in a ward: dosage, effect, side-effects. Hesh walked the Saltstairs from the cistern stairs to the topmost catwalks, and where he walked he mended by advice — a props-and-braces schedule for a tenement bowing over the fourth stair, a drain-cut for the cistern that had been flooding the undercroft since before the war of ledgers, load-lines chalked neat as type on beam-ends and door-frames, each mark a small clean glyph of circles and stems that meant, apparently, something exact to the League. He propped a wall with two apprentices and a jack-screw while its family stood in the alley holding their furniture. He set a child's broken kite-spar without being asked and told the child it had been a surveying instrument, which was a lie, and a kind one, and the child kept it.

The district took to him the way a ward takes to a nurse who remembers names. By the third day, cook-fires saved him bowls. By the fourth, the Gavel tallymen had stopped marking the alley when he passed, which any auditor will tell you is either a pardon or an oversight. Milla Ferun, who had not trusted an official face since the morning Borin told her to destroy a printout and smiled while saying it, brought the surveyor tea on the fourth afternoon, unprompted, with her own hands — and Dar watched her do it from the ward window and felt the ground shift somewhere under his certainties, because the kindness looked real from every angle he could afford, and kindness that looks real from every angle is either the rarest entry in the city's books or the most expensive.

Vahid watched him from the cellar doorway with the flat hostility of a man watching a rival thesis defend well. "No one is unpriced," he muttered, on the fourth night, glasses off, polishing them on his sleeve with his one hand. "Show me a free kindness in this city and I will show you the invoice underneath, written in something less visible than ink. Vey used tea. This one uses *ladders.*" He set the spectacles back on his nose. "Watch the hands, boy. I have watched hands all my life. Hands tell you what the ledger won't."

Roya said nothing, which from Roya was surveillance. Suri said nothing, which from Suri was an investigation.

On the fifth evening, Suri stopped saying nothing.

· · ·

She found him in the clinic's back ward at the changing of the lamp-oil, and she found him the way she found everything, by counting: three wax-paper packets laid on the instrument tray, folded with clinical neatness, and the surveyor's small knife, and the little brass scoop, and Hesh crouched at the waste-bin with a curl of bloodied gauze lifted between two fingers as delicately as a clerk lifts a voucher.

"Forty-one steps of your marks on the beams," Suri said. "Nine packets of wall-scrapings in your case, by weight. And now my ward's dressings." She did not move from the doorway. The carbon scales of her ruined graft caught the lamplight dully, night sky with a hole kicked in it. "Rate it for me, Surveyor. What's the going price of my bin."

Hesh straightened. He did not startle — that was the first entry in the account Suri was keeping; working men startle — and he did not apologize overmuch, which was the second. He wrapped the gauze curl into a fresh packet, folded it with the others, and met her flat gaze with his mild one.

"Subsidence records," he said. "The Saltstairs sits on salt, and salt eats, and what the ground takes, the League logs. These houses are built of the city and on the city, and the city is —" he considered the word with a craftsman's honesty — "organic. Walls here carry more tissue than timber, and tissue settles at rates we can read. Your ward's dressings tell me what your walls are doing to your patients. It's waste," he added, with the first flicker of something that was almost humility, "and it's logged, and it's burned at season's end. I'd show you the page, but it's in League cipher and would only insult you."

Suri looked at him for a slow count of five.

"Everything's waste," she said, "until it's filed." She took one packet from the tray — the oldest, the wall-scrapings from the cellar, from the chamber where the Vein-ladder and a girl's spiral map hung on the walls — and weighed it in her palm, and Hesh watched her do it with a face like a tide-table. "This one you'll leave," she said. "It's mine. The rest you can bill to the ground." And she put the packet in her apron, and went out, and did not tell Dar until the lamps were banked.

"She took a sample of my samples," Hesh told Dar the next morning, rueful, presenting a fresh-cut prop-beam like a man presenting references. "Your courier has a court's nose. Requisition the packet back if you like; it's only chalk-dust and dead wall."

"I will," Dar said. "I audit everything in my house. It's a habit of the formerly harvested."

"Then we'll get along," said Hesh, and hummed at his work.

· · ·

It happened while they braced the undercroft beam, Dar holding the jack, Hesh setting the chocks, the two of them working in the easy silence of tools that fit. The Nerve Vein was always open a crack now — the city's pain played under his hearing like water under ice, and he kept the door of it narrowed, a surgeon's-field wall at a walk — but the undercroft was quiet, and the humming was *close*, and it came through the narrowed door before Dar had decided to listen to it.

The lullaby.

Three notes up, one hanging pause, two notes down — the opening bars, complete, correct, rendered in a soft closed-mouth hum with a working man's breath behind it, and Dar's hands went still on the jack-screw so smoothly that even Hesh read it as settling load. The song that the woman in white gloves had hummed over the planted sleepers. The song between Vey's two cups. The song under the salt-glass door. The song whose owner he had paid away and could not name. Here it was, note for note, in the mouth of a free surveyor from a caravan out of the empty sea lanes — note for note, except for one.

Where the pause hung — the hanging pause, the held breath in the middle of the phrase that every true singer of it kept open like a debt left on the books — Hesh sang a note. Small. Flat. A quarter-tone held where the original left blank, fitted in like a substituted figure in a column, entered so smoothly that only an auditor who had audited his own grief would ever catch it. Forgeries fill blanks. Originals keep them. Dar held the jack and kept his face at settling-load and let the man finish the wall, and filed the note where he now filed such things, banked, not spent — the Mind Vein's one free trade, an observation carried into the swept halls and shut in a room with no door.

That night, the first hard frost of the season came down the Saltstairs, and Dar found it when he made his rounds with the lamp.

The clinic's windows had bloomed.

Every pane — ward, stair, cellar-light, attic — wore the same grey lace, frost furred into five-petaled rosettes by the morning, and every rosette on every pane pointed one way, bloom-tips aligned compass-true as if each window had been surveyed by the same patient hand. Dar stood in the dark ward with the lamp down and followed their bearing out through the glass, across the frost-furred roofs, down the tide-gate hill to the League's lodging-house by the shore road, where a surveyor out of the caravans slept with his chalk and his wax-paper packets and his one wrong note.

The frost pointed at Hesh's lodgings, and it did not waver, and the city that had stopped trusting its bells told him, in the only handwriting it had left, exactly where its attention had fixed: the same direction, that same night, as the pollen on the page.
