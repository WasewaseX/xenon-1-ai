# Chapter 83 — The Bloom-Fog

The fleet stopped negotiating on the third night after the readings, though nobody in Ashkarad used the word. They used the sound instead: the sound of the seabed changing, out beyond the storm-walls, in the dark before first light — a long, structural groaning of hulls that had no water under them being made to move anyway.

From the wall, Suri watched the barges come up the flats into position with the courier's glass and did not like a single figure in the picture. They came in a line, towed by the wain's engines on great drumbed cables, and they sat low even empty, riding on runners that had been built for mud, and they were the color of fat. Rendered, whale-pale, staved like the hulls of ships that had been bled: the carcass-barges of Lowmark, riding up the dry seabed of a dead sea two cities from home, each one packed with the bloom-tallow of a city that had bloomed and been pruned and gone into the books as PAID. The tally-book in the Choirmaster's keeping had carried the stamp for a year — BRACK — PRUNED, PAID — and Lowmark's page had acquired its stamp sometime in the last three weeks, quietly, somewhere out along the round, while the fleet negotiated. The audit, Suri understood now, had been the courtesy. This was the paperwork.

They anchored the line upwind of the city — squarely, precisely upwind, the way you place a lamp — and they opened the barges, and they set the tallow alight.

Bloom-tallow burned the way nothing in Ashkarad burned. It burned without crackle, a slow white yielding, and it did not make smoke so much as make fog: the fog that had been gone from the coast since the sea itself went — the fog-sea that had defined the horizon all Suri's life — came back overnight, one night, from the landward side, thick and low and smelling faintly of flowers, and it climbed the storm-walls like a tide that had read the blueprints, and it came into the city through every gap the seal-crews had not yet plugged, and it was hungry.

You felt it in the breath before anywhere else. One breath of the bloom-fog and something in the base of the lungs sat up and paid attention — a settling, a yearning, a low animal certainty that there was somewhere better to be, somewhere down, somewhere warm and dark and enormous, and that every hour spent above the salt was an hour spent away from home. The clinics filled with people who had stopped mid-step on vault-side streets, facing the wrong way, licking salt off their own wrists. A ceiling-joiner was found in a cistern-vent at noon, elbow-deep, weeping, trying to enlarge it. People counted in their sleep. Not upward.

Roya mapped the fog the only way it could be mapped, which was blind: she chalked the city hour by hour off Ilay's hearing, marking the streets that went quiet as the fog's fingers found them — the fog did not roar, it emptied, it turned whole lanes into places where no one was breathing out loud — and by dusk the map wall in the clinic bore a picture no one who saw it ever described as weather. The fog had hands. The hands were patient. And every hand pointed, compass-true, down.

"They are not besieging us," Vahid said in the war-room, his spectacles flashing, one hand flat on the day's first casualty log. "Siege wants you starved or frightened. This wants you homesick. It is the most obscene thing I have ever watched a chemical do, and I have watched the Church teach children to hum."

Isra's slate came up slowly from her corner, where the lamps burned close and the sealed vents were stuffed with censer-ash, and said what her root already knew, letter by letter, the warmth draining out of her fingers as she paid:

THE SMOKE IS MY SISTER. THEY ARE BURNING MY SISTER.

Below the city, all that day, the deep did not stir. The Root's rhythm stayed slow and even, the way it had settled since the readings — and somehow that was the worst figure on the board. The fog was made of the harvested, and the deep beneath the lid slept through the smell of its own children's burning, and Suri entered that in her book under the only heading that did not lie: THINGS WE DO NOT UNDERSTAND YET.

Ashkarad sealed, and Suri rationed the air.

It was the quietest mobilization the city had ever run. The gates went shut at dusk, all of them, and the storm-wall flues were packed with clay and censer-ash, and the city drew inward around its cisterns and its stored breath like a body closing a wound. What could not be sealed was fed. The chorus-free smoke — the ash-recipe, the Vigil's rest, Vahid's whole stock and the censer-works' whole output — went to the censer-lines at the cistern-works and the clinic and the deep of the Saltstairs, and the censer-lines ran on Mother Gavel's water, because the deal she had struck was already paying: her pumps drove the smoke-lines, her crews ladled the censers, her pipes carried the city's clean breath the way they had once carried its debts, and she stood at the cistern-works the first evening with her arms folded, watching her arithmetic serve Suri's, wearing the expression of a woman who has discovered that two ledgers can hold the same page without either one catching fire. At each line Suri posted her breath-books, and the breath-books were the meanest and the cleanest arithmetic she had ever ruled.

Four counted breaths at the smoke, twice a day, per soul, entered by hand. No debts. No deferrals. No flesh. You could not owe a breath and you could not lend one, and she had the lines marshaled so that a mother's four and a magistrate's four came out of the same ladled smoke, and she stood at the cistern-line the first evening and watched the city of Ashkarad breathe on the count, in queues, in the fog-sick streets, thousands of chests rising and falling to a clerk's cadence, and she thought: this is what an honest ledger feels like from the inside. It feels like everyone breathing.

The fog pressed on the walls all night and all day, patient as appetite. And on the second night it sent her brother.

· · ·

She heard him before she saw anything, because the fog ate light and left sound.

She was walking the seawall's inner gallery because the fog-sick drifted to the walls and someone had to count them back, and out through the stone, muffled and doubled, against the outer face of the seawall, a man was coughing. Fleet livery, by the shape of him through the merlons — a clerk's coat, a clerk's stoop, one hand flat on the salt-black stone. He coughed, and the cough was not what stopped her. What stopped her was the count under it. He was coughing in cadence. Four beats, a held rest, four beats again, the cough fitted to the count like a word to a tune, and the cadence went through Suri like a needle finding an old scar, because she had known it since before she knew anything else — since grace storms in a one-room flat above the drying-racks, when their mother had sat between her children and counted their coughs aloud to keep the fear from compounding: four in, four held, four out, again, again, a rhythm to put your breath inside of, the way other mothers said hush.

Dathan.

Her brother had gone out of the record when she was nineteen — gone to find coin for a skin he was too late to keep from being sold, and had found it, somehow, in the only market that dealt in what he had: himself, signed, a counting-house boy, ledgered. She had never once had to wonder whose books held his page. She had known the way you know an unpaid balance. And now here was the balance, walking itself up the outside of the seawall in the enemy's fog, coughing his cough in their mother's count, and Suri Kaveh, who had retired her own page with fire and ink, stood on the inner gallery with her hand pressed flat to the stone exactly opposite his hand, and could not for the life of her make the two columns meet.

He coughed. She counted the beats. Her lips moved with them; she could not stop them.

And the coughing stopped, and there was a silence with two people breathing in it on opposite sides of the wall, and then his voice came through the stone, small with distance and fog, in the flat tone of a man reporting his own figures: "You still count, Su."

"You still cough in four," she said.

A sound that was half a laugh and mostly coughing. "It's the fog. It doesn't like it when I count. Nothing over here likes it when I count." The hand on the stone shifted, gripped. "I'm not coming to the gate, Su. I'm just — walking the wall. You can hear a city through its wall. I wanted to hear if anyone in there was still counting."

"Everyone is counting," Suri said. "Come to the gate and I'll enter you myself."

"No." Flat. Final. A clerk declining a posting. "I've got a column to keep."

The fog-whistles sounded somewhere down the flats — the fleet calling its people home out of the smoke — and he pushed himself off the wall, and she heard him straighten his coat the way their mother had taught them both, and he went, unhurried, back down into the fog toward the barge-lights, coughing in fours all the way. Suri stood a long time with her hand on the stone. Then she took out her book, and ruled a line where a column should go, and did not write anything above it. Nineteen years she had kept the account open in her head; it could stay in her head a while longer. You did not enter a brother in a ledger until you knew which side of the page he was on — and if she wrote him down as the enemy's, she thought, climbing back along the dark gallery with the fog pressing gentle and hungry at the stone, then it would be a fact, and facts in her book were the kind that got collected. She left the line ruled and the column empty, and shut the book on it, and kept the count.

The tide came back on the third night, and it came back wrong.

There was no sea behind it. There was nothing behind it at all — no wind driving it, no moon pulling it, no water anywhere on the charts or in the cisterns or in the memory of the port — and yet the dry seabed below the walls began, at slack midnight, to shine: a black gloss crawling in from the edges of the world, climbing the flats against their own lie of the land, uphill in places, curling around the barge-line like a hand closing, filling the tow-channels the fleet's own engines had cut. The sentries rang the wrong bell because there was no right bell for it. Water that had forgotten to come back for a year was remembering, all at once, from underneath, as if the earth itself had opened a vein.

And the tide bore cargo.

It lifted the wain — the wain, the Gardener's own reliquary on wheels, dark and vast at the center of the fleet's lamplight, the thing that had never once been late — and it carried it, without one engine turning, smooth as a pall at a funeral, directly to the tide-gates, and set it there against the sealed doors of the city with a courtesy that was worse than ramming. The fleet's ranks parted to let it pass and did not follow. Ashkarad stood on its walls in the bloom-fog and watched the black water lap the gates like a tide that had made a delivery, and on the wain's flank the lamp caught the white of a single glove, untied, hanging from the rail, patient as a filing date.

Suri stood at the wall with her book against her ribs and her brother's column still open, and watched the coffin come up to the door of the city. Dar reached the gallery at a run and stopped beside her, and she saw him put his hand flat over his own sternum the way you quiet something pressing at a door — and then she felt it too, through the stone, through her boots, through the marrow of everyone standing watch: his second heartbeat, the one that was not only his, humming, very low. Not the lullaby. Not any tune the city had a name for. A note like an acknowledgment of receipt.
