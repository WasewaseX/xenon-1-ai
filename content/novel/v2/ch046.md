# Chapter 46 — Three Heartbeats of Her Face

Dar came back to the counting-annex at the turn of the tide-priests' lazy morning, when the lamps in the arch hall burned their lowest and the vault shelves stood quiet, and he laid Vey's folded page on the counter beside the Ashkarad box, because today he was going to stop being a customer and start being an account.

"The face," he said. "Not a playing. A look at the entry itself."

Orlo did not ask which entry. The registrar turned the index leaf over with one finger, and the groove his serenity had worn the day before was still there, and he read the tariff line the way a man reads a weather he has no intention of arguing with.

"A decade of a life," he said. "That is the price of the face. Not eleven heartbeats — a look is not a loan; a look is the door opened onto the deposit, and the deposit is the whole of her, pressed at the moment of filing. The house prices a door like that in living. Ten years, standard rate, pressed and filed to the house before the door opens." He folded his hands on the counter. "No one pays it twice. Very few pay it once. I have been registrar of this house for twenty-two years and I have seen the decade paid three times, and two of the three were seed."

"We don't have a decade," Suri said, from the end of the counter. "We have a road, a crate of cylinders, and whatever the tide-priests haven't confiscated."

"Then you have the house's paper," Orlo said, "or you have nothing," and that was when Vell stepped forward and put her slate on the counter, and wrote on it, pressing hard enough that the chalk squealed, in her vertical counting-house hand:

COLLATERAL: ME.
BRACK CUSTOM. THE BODY SIGNS.

Dar read it twice, because the first reading refused to be true. Vell stood beside the counter with her chin up and her burned-salt hands flat on the wood, a small woman who had survived the drowning of one city and the drowning of its books, offering herself into the ledger the way her people had offered themselves for a hundred years — and Brack's custom was the oldest entry in Ashkarad's oldest nightmares: the body in the book. Not a promise. Not a signature. A lien, held against flesh, exercisable at default, and every counting-house in Brack had exercised, in the end, because that was what the harvest had been — the whole city foreclosed.

"No," Dar said.

Vell wiped the slate with the heel of her hand and wrote again, fast, the chalk snapping once against the wood: I AM ALREADY COLLATERAL. BRACK COUNTED ME WORTH EATING. I WOULD RATHER THE BOOK BE ONE I CHOSE.

"You're not paper," Dar said. His voice came out in the surgeon's register, the one that does the counting during the bleeding. "You're a witness. You're the only person on this route who has seen the harvest close up and kept her numbers — and a witness who signs herself into a ledger stops being a witness the moment the holder decides to collect. That's not a rate, Vell. That's the whole account, given away for a look through a door."

Vell looked at him. Then she wrote, slower, and the slowness was the argument: THE OTHERS SIGNED. EVERY ONE OF THEM. THEY WERE NOT PAPER EITHER.

"And that sentence is what the burning was for," Dar said. "No."

He put his hand flat over the slate, over the words, the way you close a ledger you refuse to balance, and Vell looked at his hand for a long moment, and then did the thing he would remember about her for the rest of a life that had been getting shorter in entries all season: she nodded, once, like a clerk accepting a ruling she intends to appeal forever, and stepped back from the counter.

It was Suri who closed the gap, because Suri understood instruments the way Orlo understood them — as load-bearing things.

"The paper," she said. "The house's paper. Read me the terms."

Orlo produced the promissory from a drawer lined in lead, and it was a single leaf of the grey velum, blank, humming faintly the way the vault shelves hummed, and he laid it flat and read the terms in the voice of a man who has read them so many times they have worn smooth. The debt would be denominated in the house's currency: a decade of a life, pressed at the standard rate, from any account the signatory designated or, failing designation, from any account the house designated. Interest would accrue at the road's discretion. The holder of the paper would be the road itself — not the house, not the Bride, not the Registry's far vaults, but the route, the round, the collection entire — and the road's collections were not performed by clerks.

"Who collects for the road?" Suri asked, though Dar could see by her face that she had already read the answer in the room, in the wax hems, in the flower stamped through everything in this city like a watermark through money.

"The heralds," Orlo said. "The road's paper is herald paper. It is honored on presentation."

Suri read the leaf twice, clause by clause, and she counted the clauses aloud as she read, because the counting was hers and no one could repossess it, and Dar watched her do the thing she had done in Mother Gavel's fighting pit and at the seawalls and in every transaction of her life: price herself into the arrangement before anyone else could. Eleven clauses. She made Orlo repeat the seventh, the one about designated accounts, and watched his eyes when he repeated it, and then she took the registrar's pen of grey bone and signed her name in a hand as flat and vertical as a column total, and the leaf drank the name out of the air the instant it was written, the way salt drinks water.

"On account," Orlo said, "the house shows three heartbeats of the deposit. The door opens that far. It opens no farther until the decade is paid." He drew a shallow reading-tray of black bone across the counter and set the MOTHER — FACE leaf in it, face up, and the leaf was smaller than the others and heavier than it should have been, the way a thing is heavier when more than one person's grief has been pressed into it.

Suri's fingers found Dar's wrist. The counting position. Her eyes went to his pulse, not his face.

He touched the leaf, and the face arrived.

It came up out of the grey the way the bride's morning had come up, whole and warm and immediate, but there was no morning around it — no room, no city, no light — only the face itself, held in the dark of the deposit like a lamp lit in a swept room, and for the space of one heartbeat it was the most complete thing he had ever seen. The jaw was right. He knew the jaw was right the way his hands knew sutures, below thought, below memory, in the place where the body keeps what the mind has spent. The mouth was right, and the way her head sat on her neck, tilted two degrees, listening for something in another room — the kettle going, his own small crying, the bell of the seawall works tolling the tide — all of it right, all of it hers, and Dar stood in the second heartbeat and understood that he was looking at his mother.

In the third heartbeat, it went wrong.

Not by much. That was the obscenity of it. The jaw stayed right, the mouth stayed right, the tilt of the head stayed right — and the eyes changed. They were older than any mother's eyes. Not tired-old; not grief-old; old in the way the route stones were old, in the way the five-petaled flower carved a hundred years ago under the salt bloom was old — a time-depth that no human face could carry without the face collapsing under it, and the face did not collapse, which was how Dar knew, in the last instant he had the face at all, that whatever he was looking at, it had never once been his mother.

The Lily went still.

Not banked. Not dim. Still — the stillness of a guest who has recognized a stranger in a family photograph, the stillness of a thing that held, folded away in whatever ledger it kept inside him, the original of this picture, pressed whole the day the two of them fused, and knew precisely, to the note, how the copy in front of him differed from it. She held her stillness out to him like a verdict, and Dar read it the way he read a pulse: not her. Not her.

And the face, fading — the grey rising through it like frost rising through a floor, the way every filed thing in this house eventually frosted over — shaped its mouth around three syllables of an endearment he had never heard in his life.

There was no sound. The deposit kept no sound, or the sound was pressed somewhere else, filed in some other box, sold in some other city. But the shape of the syllables carried their meaning the way the lullaby carried its own — a dead language that understood itself without hearing — and the meaning arrived in Dar whole, priced and labeled, from a mouth he did not know by a mother he could not remember, and the meaning was:

Little root.

The grey took the rest. The tray was empty. The lamps stood up straight again. Suri's fingers were still on his wrist, and she said, "Three," and let go, and the counting was done.

They stood on the rope-walk outside the customhouse for a long time, the three of them, above the drowned street with its drowned shopfronts and its heat-berry glow coming up green along the flood-line, and the harbor hummed its district-sized chord below, and nobody filed anything for a while.

"It was wrong," Dar said at last. "The face. Everything was right except it was wrong — and she knew." His hand lay flat on his sternum, over the banked heat of the second heart. "She has the original. When we — " he did not finish that. When we fused, you drank my memories, and you have been keeping hers ever since, in the dark, in the ledger you keep inside me. He did not say it. He said instead: "The Registry doesn't hold my mother's face. It holds whatever the house decided my mother's face was. A composite. A copy of a copy. And copies of copies, in my trade, are how wrong limbs get attached."

Vell wrote on her slate, and held it up: THEN WHO FILED THE COPY?

That was the question, and it had teeth in it, because a deposit did not press itself. Someone had taken a face that was not quite his mother's and entered it, under his name, in the World Registry's books — signed it, in three strokes, blooming from a crack — and Dar could think of exactly one hand that signed that way, over and over, like a stamp, and he could not yet think of one honest reason why that hand would bother to forge his mother.

Suri did not say any of it. Suri sat down on the rope coil by the walkway rail, opened her ledger on her knees, ruled a new column with the edge of her wax knife — four strokes, unhurried, a clerk making the books fit the world instead of the world fit the books — and wrote the column's title at the top in her flat bookkeeper's hand, each letter priced and filed:

WHAT WE OWE THE ROAD.

Below it, as the first entry, she wrote: ONE (1) LOOK — THREE HEARTBEATS — PAID: PROMISSORY, UNSIGNED BY ME IN SPIRIT, SIGNED BY ME IN INK.

Then she closed the ledger, and looked out at the harbor, at the black flower the size of a district lying in its drained bed, and said, to the city as much as to him:

"Interest at the road's discretion, Mehran. I've been charged by worse."
