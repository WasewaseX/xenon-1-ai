# Chapter 92 — What the Daughter Was

The Lily began counting to nine on the night after the books were served, and Roya caught it first, because a rhythm on paper is still a map.

Dar felt it before he understood it — a change in the pedal under his own two heartbeats, a new figure worked into the hum the way a clerk works a reconciliation into an open column. Nine pulses. A rest. Nine pulses. The rest between was not silence; it was held breath, the pause a household makes when it is waiting on a name. He lay on the clinic roof in the dark with his coat as a pillow and counted her counting, and the count did not vary, and that was how he knew it was language: nature never counts that evenly twice.

By the second night Roya had chalked it. Ilay heard it too — of course she did; she had been transcribing the garden's true song since before anyone knew there was one — and the two of them, the severed surveyor and the nine-year-old instrument, worked the figure out at the attic window while Dar stood behind them holding a lamp he was not allowed to light. Nine pulses, rest, nine pulses. "She is not keeping time," Ilay said, in the flat voice she kept for findings. "She is keeping a family."

Roya chalked the figure out as a diagram by the third night, because that was what it was: nine circles in a ring, one shaded, and above the nine a single larger circle struck through with a black bar — the Burning, entered as a date. Dar stood looking at the shape on the attic wall, a genealogy drawn by a woman who could not hear and a girl who heard everything, transcribed off a rhythm kept by a seed, and the shaded circle was the ninth, and Roya shaded it the way she shaded living things.

The pulses assembled what the verdict at the shaft mouth had only implied. Nine of a kind, the rhythm said, rest, grown from one. Nine issued against a single account. And when the figure reached the spent ones, Dar felt each disbursal land — eight small closed cadences in sequence, like pages being turned in a ledger someone else is holding: a seed in Brack, cut and spent; a seed in Lowmark, drowned and rendered; seeds sown in Sarn, Weep, Ossuar, Hallowfen, in the Vigil's lead-glass dark, in Gullet's engine; every one an account now closed, harvested down the deep roads to feed the thing their mother slept above. The ninth still open. The rest cut, and spent, and fed.

Vahid confirmed it against the Church's own papers in the cellar, one-handed, turning the nursery registry pages with the flat of his palm: the sibling-stock ledgers, generations of children grown from tissue taken from the First Vessel, graded, reserved, sung to. "The Church knew a hundredth of it and banked it as breeding stock," he said, and his voice had the wobble in it that meant the gin was not working fast enough. "The Gardener knew all of it and banked it as daughters. Same tissue, boy. Same source. Every seed in the World Registry and every child in the Church's registry, drawn on the same account." He pulled the old photograph out of the drawer then — he kept it the way misers keep a coin — the first volunteer, his and Shirin's first, blooming into a garden at the edge of the frame, and laid it on the nursery ledger so the two documents could audit each other. "We thought we were growing a heart that couldn't break," he said. "We were growing somebody's daughter, and we did not know whose. Shirin knew before I did. Shirin always knew before I did." He looked up, and the wire-mended spectacles caught the lamp. "Drawn," he said again, quietly, testing the word and finding it exact. "Every one of them drawn on the daughter."

And the daughter was the thing that lay dead in the cold chamber down among the deep salt vaults — the First Vessel, the Gardener's daughter, the lid — and the account had been opened at the First Burning, and nine cuttings had been issued against it, and the ninth was asleep in his chest, counting to nine in the dark, waiting for someone to read her arithmetic right.

Isra felt the testimony arrive the way she felt everything from below now: as a change of temperature in the root at her collarbone, a slow warming, like a hand closing around hers from underneath.

The deep wanted to depose. She knew the wanting well — a century of it, banked. She took her slate and went down the seven hundred and eleven steps with Dar behind her and Suri behind him, because the Sister had asked — the slate said SHE WILL SAY IT ONLY ONCE, in Isra's own careful letters — and what the eldest of the nine seeds had to say, she would say at the door, to her family, with the city's witnesses on the stair.

Her root paid the toll from the first step and paid it rising. The deep did not speak to her in words; it spoke in warmth, in currents under her skin, in the old arrangement she had made with it on the flats, and tonight the current ran heavy, the way a pipe runs heavy before it floods. By the four hundredth step her breath showed white. By the six hundredth her fingers were stiff and she was warming them under her arms between phrases, and she thought, with the cold clarity the deep lent her: a century of testimony, and she has been waiting for the room to be full enough to hold it.

The antechamber was cold and the salt-glass door stood open as it had stood open since the night the city learned what a lid was, and beyond it the vaults held their one heart in three chambers, counting back in threes. Isra set her bare palm flat on the glass, and the petal at her collarbone opened all the way for the first time since the flats, and the testimony moved through her like water moving through a diver, weightless and enormous, and she wrote as fast as her hand allowed and warmer letters cost her, and did not stop.

The Gardener's daughter was dying at the Burning. The garden's heart was already buried, already hungry, already waking to its feeding, and the daughter — burned, bled, past saving — had been carried into the deep and locked above it as a living lid, because a dying daughter's song was the only song that could keep the Root dreaming, and the world had needed the Root dreaming.

And the Gardener, who did not accept accounts she could not pay, had gone into the dying and begun to cut.

Nine cuttings from the daughter as she died. Nine seeds, each one a daughter grown past death, each one sown with the same instruction the white hands had given across a century — Go. Find your hearts. And bloom. — and the first cutting was the eldest, taken while the daughter's heart still beat, taken from the living dying of her; and the eldest fled the Burning in a lead-and-glass reliquary with eight smaller sisters coffined around her, and watched through glass for a hundred years while the world above buried the garden and the harvest grew and the pour-routes opened and her mother's books came for city after city; and Isra wrote and wrote and her letters went crooked with cold and cost, and the last line of the deposition she wrote slowly, pressing hard, so it would not be mistaken for anything but what it was:

I AM THE FIRST. SHE IS THE LAST. WE ARE ALL OF US THE DAUGHTER.

The antechamber was silent. Suri had her ledger open and was not writing in it. Dar stood with one hand flat over the place where his second heartbeat lived, and the Lily pressed back against his palm, once, the way a child presses back.

Then every bloom in the vault spoke.

It was not loud. Frost-flowers on the vault walls, the grey lace on the fractured door, the black petals folded at Isra's collarbone, the faint blooming in the salt itself where the old harvest had seeded the stone — every flowering thing below the city opened at once and gave up one sentence, in a voice like a woman reading her own apology off a page she has rehearsed so long the ink has worn through:

"Some things were meant to be shared. I shared her the only way I had left."

The words came and settled like ash. Isra found she had written them on the slate without being asked — the blooms spoke through her root the way the deep spoke through Ilay, and her hand had taken dictation from her enemy's mother without consulting her — and she looked at the sentence standing there in her own careful letters, an apology rehearsed for a hundred years, and could not decide which was worse: that it was monstrous, or that it was true, or that it was both and would therefore never balance.

The Sister said nothing. The one heart in the vaults behind the open door went on counting in threes, and the silence it kept was not assent and was not accusation; it was the silence of someone hearing, at last, the reason for the worst thing that ever happened to her, and finding that the reason was smaller than the wound.

Then, below the glass, the pedal resumed — but changed, the way a held note changes when the player decides to go on. Isra felt the deep take the deposition the way a ledger takes an entry: filed, cross-referenced, not closed. The vault air warmed by one degree, no more; a body's worth of warmth, spent from somewhere. Against the far side of the salt-glass, level with Isra's hand, frost gathered and bloomed and un-bloomed, a slow clench and release, clench and release, the deep's whole grammar reduced to a fist learning not to be one. She had carried a century of this thing's words and she had never once felt it refuse to strike until tonight.

It was Ilay who spoke, from the stair, where she had come down uninvited again with her chalk already out — nine years old, copper-haired, the garden's daughter, the girl the Church had graded PERFECT and the city had failed to price. She had listened to the whole deposition with her head tilted, doing the arithmetic children do, the honest kind, the kind adults file under sentiment and lose.

"Then the eleven are my cousins," she said.

Nobody corrected her. Vell's chalk hung over the slate. Suri's pen hung over the ledger. Dar looked from the girl to the door to the vault where the cold chamber lay with its grave-goods — his own original heart buried there like a wreath — and could not find one column in which the correction would balance. The eleven freed vessels and the nine sown seeds were flowers off the same stem, the Church's breeding ledgers and the Gardener's World Registry two hands writing in the same book, and the smallest witness in Ashkarad had closed the account in four words while the adults were still ruling columns. Elias, half a flight above her on the stair, heard it and grinned, and she did not grin back, because it had not been a joke. It had been a filing.

Ilay turned her palm over and looked at the chalk dust on it, and added, in the same flat finding-voice, the part she had actually come down to say:

"And the music says the eldest has been counting us all since the Burning. She wants to know if the cousins can visit."
