# Chapter 93 — The Honest Text

The fourteenth cylinder had been mocking him for a year, and Professor Vahid Anousheh had been letting it, because mockery was the only form of conversation left to them.

Nine days on the city's clock. Nine days, served like a summons, chalked on the clinic wall under the provision's terms in Suri's straightedge hand, and the whole of Ashkarad walking around the number the way you walk around a hole in a stair. Vahid did not walk around it. He sat in the cellar under the clinic, under the census and the wards and the shaft-house, with his one hand and his wire-mended spectacles and the fourteen wax cylinders of Dr. Shirin Bahar, and he worked the problem that had ruined his life twice, once with gin and once without.

He had transcribed her words months ago. That was the bitter joke of it: her voice, dropped and recovered from the wax, all of it, every lecture and margin-note and grumble about the bench hygiene of juniors, and the words ended where his papers ended — at Bone. Everything past the Sixth Vein, in her recordings, was silence, or near enough. The fourteenth cylinder ended mid-sentence, mid-word: and then you will hear the sound— and the stylus lifted and the groove ran empty and he had sat in this cellar a year ago with his hand over his mouth while the machine hissed.

Tonight he stopped listening to her words.

He had been listening to her words because he was an anatomist and words were dissections, and because listening to her voice was a debt he paid in installments and did not intend to retire. But the city needed the third way to have an instrument, and Dar had come down the cellar stairs the night before with the look of a man carrying a diagnosis he could not yet file — Both, or neither, the sentence the whole city was rationing around — and had said, in his flat surgeon's way, what Suri's ledger had said: we have a song the deep cannot count, or we have nothing. And Vahid had lain awake with the gin untouched, which was itself a symptom, and heard, in memory, not her words.

Her hums.

Between the sentences of every lecture, in the gaps where she thought, she hummed. He had filed it for forty years as a habit, the way you file a tic. He had recorded it for a year as texture. It had never once occurred to him — and the not-occurrence would keep him up the rest of his life — that Shirin Bahar did not do anything without filing a reason.

He took the third cylinder, her lecture on graft-bed preparation, and set the needle down, and did not transcribe the words. He wrote the pitches. Note by note, hum by hum, in the rest-gaps between her sentences, he took down the intervals she was carrying the way other people carry a tune, and the intervals did not wander, did not decorate, did not once resolve the way a satisfied hum resolves. They were exact. They were staved. They were — and his hand began to shake at the eighth bar, and he pinned it flat with his forearm and kept writing — ordered.

"Marginalia," he said to the empty cellar, in the voice that had once held four hundred students. "She called them marginalia. The word was not decoration, it was a filing instruction."

He worked until the lamp oil was a coin's depth in the glass. Cylinder by cylinder, hum by hum, the gaps between her words gave up a continuous text — pitches and rests, bar after bar after bar, a long file of them, and when he laid the pages out on the slate shelf and looked down the whole run of it at once, he saw what it was, and he sat down on the stool very slowly, like a man sitting down in front of a mirror that has decided to be honest.

It was not the lullaby.

It was the true song. Ilay's transcription — the garden's song, every verse, the mother's line included, the score the child had titled for my grandmother — was copied fair on the wall above the chalk ladder, and he took it down and put his pages beside it, and the two texts agreed. Note for note. Rest for rest. Two witnesses, a century apart, deposing to the same song: a nine-year-old girl hearing it through the city's bones, and a dead woman hiding it in the gaps of her own voice forty years ago.

"Test-seed," he said. His hands were cold. It was not the cellar. "There was a test-seed. Before the Church took the work. Before Vey's predecessors came to our bench with their polite letters and their ledger smiles. We grew it from the ancestor strain and it sang — once, at night, in the dark of the workshop, when the lamps were out and the blood-feeds were late. I was drunk. I remember the drone of it and not the notes." He stopped. Corrected himself, the way he had corrected students, gently, with the blade: "I remember that I did not hear it. She heard it. And she understood, before I did, what a plain notation of that song would be worth, and to whom, and she spent the rest of her recorded life filing it where no auditor reads — inside the wrapper of her own voice, between the words, in the rests, in the margins, humming her honesty into the places of the record where meaning is not looked for."

The honest text of the upper Veins existed. It had existed for decades. His partner had written it, and hidden it, and died for the archive that held it, and the Church had kept her voice six years in glass and never once thought to listen to the silences.

Vahid Anousheh took off his spectacles, and wiped them with the heel of his one hand, and put them back on, and read the text again from the top. This time he read it as what it was: not a song deposed but a doctrine scored. The notation did not climb. That was the first thing. Every chart of the Nine Veins he had ever chalked — his own included, his own especially — climbed: Flesh at the base, rung above rung, Throne at the top like a salary. Her notation did not climb. It layered. Voice upon voice, line upon line, each part complete and each part incomplete alone, the whole of it demanding to be struck together.

He chalked it in the morning, on the cellar wall under the old ladder, for the council to find: the seven opened rungs drawn as he had always drawn them — and then, where the eighth and ninth had always stood as rungs, he drew them as she had scored them. Not steps. Lines in a chord. Soul, the door shared between two — he had paid one lifetime learning that. And the ninth, the Throne, drawn not as a rank above the others but as the chord entire: Flesh through Throne, every carrier's line held at once, one harmony, none of it sounding if any of it is struck alone.

"The ninth is not a rung," he said to Dar, who had come down the stairs with two cups of the clinic's acorn coffee and stood holding both, because there was only the one hand to receive with and the boy had not yet learned to hand things to him left-first. "I have been teaching your generation a ladder, boy, because a ladder is what a man can climb alone, and I am a man who was left alone. My partner taught the truth: the ninth is a harmony of carriers. Ilay drew it in chalk a month before I proved it — nine petals, every petal connected to the others. I have never in my life been so furious or so proud, and both were at a child."

Dar set the cups down on the slate shelf and looked at the wall for a long time. "The Gardener climbs," he said at last. "She offered to climb it with whoever said yes. One climber, her holding the far side."

"One climber, and the Gardener's hand on the far side, and the ledger unrewritten, and the deep still fed blind." Vahid tapped the chord with his knuckle, line by line, Flesh to Throne. "Her text shows what the Door checks. Not fortune. Company. It was never a treasure at the top of a climb, boy. It was an ensemble at the bottom of one, and the audition is the score." He looked at the wall, and then at the boy, and permitted himself the one thing he had sworn off in the Blacktide's ashes, which was hope with the interest left running. "And count your ensemble, since you are a man who counts. The census of the willing, held in you. A Sister's pedal-tone under the city, kept true for a century. A child's score. A count that sings instead of being counted. The ensemble is not coming, boy. The ensemble is here. It has been tuning for a year." He put his one hand flat on the chalk. "The Gardener offered you her hand on the far side of the door. My partner's notation says we will not need it." He heard himself, and winced, and corrected the claim to the size of his proof. "Will not need it, if the share holds. If." He took his spectacles off, and wiped them, and put them back. "I am an old man advising you to bet a city on a dead woman's hums. I am aware of how it sounds. I was there when she hummed them, and I have been wrong about her exactly once, and it was the not-listening, and I am done being wrong on that line item."

He finished the protocol that afternoon, because grief is fuel if you are careless with it and he had been careless with it for forty years: the chorus-free distillation, the numbered vials, Shirin's counter-singing that sings to nothing — complete at last, every dose a sentence of hers, the whole of it written fair and signed, and the last line he ruled across the page with a straightedge like a man closing an estate.

THE LINE CLOSES HERE. THE LILY IS THE LAST SEED OF THE STRAIN.

He signed it with his one hand, and dated it, and did not explain it to the council beyond what needed explaining, because some rulings are not arguments: the honest text made new seeds unnecessary and made new seeds obscene — the world did not need a tenth daughter grown past death; it needed the nine voices it had, struck together. Whatever the Gardener's books said a strain was for, the strain ended where the harmony began. He had spent his life on the ladder. He was ending it on the chord, and the chord was her notation, and the Lily would be the last of the line because the line had never needed more than one good listener and one good singer, and he had married the listener and carried the singer, and both of those were past him now, and the music was not.

The lamp was nearly out. The fourteenth cylinder sat on the spindle where it had sat for a year, mid-word, mid-accusation: and then you will hear the sound—

He knew now what he had not known: that the stylus had not lifted because the sentence failed. It lifted because the recording jumped bands — because the last stretch of her message was not cut in the voice-groove at all. There was a second groove. Finer, shallower, cut with the workshop's light sapphire needle that he had mourned as lost with everything else of hers: a hairline track spiraling inside the voice-track, readable only at half the weight, and he had played this cylinder a hundred times at full weight like a surgeon operating through a drape.

He changed the needle. He halved the weight. He set the arm, and let the outer band run to the cut — and then you will hear the sound— — and the stylus dropped into the hairline and the cellar filled with her voice again, younger, from a night with the lamps out, and she finished the sentence she had begun forty years and one lifetime ago:

"—is the share."
