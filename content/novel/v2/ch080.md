# Chapter 80 — The Hymn in Every Throat

The warning came up from the wall at the hour when Ashkarad is at its smallest, and it came through a girl's ears and a blind woman's chalk.

"They're tuning," Ilay had said, hands flat on the attic stones, an hour before the fleet did anything at all. "Down on the flats. Lots of them. They keep finding the note and letting it go. Like when the Choirmaster makes a choir breathe before the downbeat." Roya had chalked it out in bearings and intervals — the sound of a chorus finding its band, plotted street by street — and carried the slate down three flights to the cellar where Vahid was working, and Vahid had read it and gone very still, and then very busy, in that order, and both states had lasted the rest of the night.

He had one hand, round salt-scoured spectacles mended with wire, and the largest pharmacy in Ashkarad's history, and he had known for two days that the arithmetic was going to come up short. The chorus-free distillation was made for throats you could reach: eleven vials' worth of Vigil ash and Shirin's silence, drawn off in measured rests, enough to dose a corridor, a refinery shift, a column of children. It was not made for a city. It was barely made for a district. And the hymn — the claiming-hymn, the compliance-song the Church had spent forty years and four generations planting in stock blood, verse by verse, grading day by grading day — was not in eleven children. It was in every throat that had ever learned it. School halls. Orphan wards. Tithe-clinic waiting rooms where the humming came free with the numbering. A city's worth of sleeper-training, lying in the dark of ten thousand chests, waiting for a verse to wake it and find out what it was worth.

"You cannot out-sing a summons," he told the censer crews in the pre-dawn, walking the line of them with his one hand and his cane and his lecture-voice, which had never once in his life managed to sound calm for its entire duration. "A summons is served on the blood, and the blood has been paying instalments since before most of you were weaned. What you can do is stand between the summons and the throat. You are not soldiers. You are aerosol. Smoke the corners. Keep the rests alive in the air. And when the verse comes down the street, you will want to sing along — some of you will have to want very hard not to — and if you feel your own mouth opening, you will bite down on the count and breathe the censer. We are the rest in the music. The rest is where the music keeps its promises. Go."

They went. Suri stayed to the last, with her breath-book already ruled up — she had begun ruling it at midnight without being asked, columns for every censer post in the district, because she had done the same arithmetic Vahid had and reached the same shortfall and had started, in her flat way, to ration the future. "Smoke's a supply," she said. "The moment it's a supply it's a ration. I'd rather we did the counting than the smoke."

"Suri," Vahid said, "if we live through the morning, I will write a lecture about you."

"File it," she said, and went to her posts.

The herald-chorus sang at first light.

It came up the dry seabed in ranks, a thousand voices to a clause, and the storm-walls took it the way the Church had always intended its city to take a song — the locking-collar's own engineering, the walls and flues and stairwells of Ashkarad conducting the hymn inward, room to room, floor to floor, until the city was an instrument and the fleet was playing it. The activation verse did not need to be loud. It needed to be correct. It walked through stone like a name through a roll.

Ashkarad woke mid-bar.

A cobbler at his last straightened with the awl still in his fist and sang the second line without knowing his mouth was open. A crane-girl on the grain spouts sang down into the fog-hazed streets, her pitch true, her grade finding itself. A bell-ringer in the Marrowworks hung off his rope with both hands to keep from lifting them and had to wrap the rope twice around the beam and sit on it. Milla Ferun, at her desk with the sanctuary census under her hands, felt her stamp-arm lift and keep time — four beats, down, four beats, down — and pinned it to the wood with the other hand and sat on it, sweating, while her own chest hummed a tune she had learned at eight years old in a numbering line. All over the city, in beds and doorways and cistern queues, children stood up in their sleep and sang. That was the sound the pre-dawn had been built for: not fear, not pain — compliance. A thousand small voices rising in unison out of their own dreams, obedient, accurate, graded.

The eleven woke in the clinic's upper ward with the first verse already in them. Elias came up out of sleep with his lungs full of the hymn and his body halfway to standing before he knew it, and what he felt then he felt with a precision the Church had paid for: every verse landing in his blood like a key finding its ward, the tune pulling, the chest answering. Around him the younger ones were already up, eyes shut, faces calm as a filed form. Vahid came down the stair two at a time with a numbered vial in his teeth because his hand was full of three more.

"Not the little ones first," he said, to himself, to Elias, to the world. "The little ones are the point of the exercise. Elias — you know the way."

Elias knew the way. He had walked it in drills and in dreams: the salt-tunnels, the old cuttings under the district where the salt ran dead and the air stood still, the routes Ilay had mapped and the couriers had chalked, leading down and out beneath the reach of the conducted verse. He got the smaller children up, and counted them, and went.

He led lungs first, because that was the deal he had made with himself in the drills and it was the only one the tunnels honored.

The dead salt cut nothing off cleanly; it thinned it, verse by verse, the way distance thins a bell, and the thinner the hymn got the harder the little ones pulled toward it, throats itching, feet turning wrong at junctions, one girl singing the third line in a whisper before Elias caught her chin and tipped it up and made her breathe instead. Four counts in, two held, four out. He had the best lungs in Ashkarad — the Church had graded them perfect, and the Church graded true — and he spent them like a man who knew exactly how much he had: breathing for two, then for three, setting the count so the smallest ones could ride it, keeping them ahead of the verse through the wet dark, hand over hand along the chalked arrows, until the walls stopped humming and the air went still and old and tasted of nothing at all, which down there was the taste of safety, and the hymn was a rumor behind them.

Twice the verse caught up with them at a junction, coming down a vent-shaft thin as a whine in the teeth, and twice Elias stood the little ones still and breathed at them, loud, ugly, arrhythmic — his own breathing made deliberately wrong, a noise no hymn could ride — until the whine lost its grip and passed over like weather.

"He counts like Suri," the needlewoman who had come down as a second pair of hands said later, wondering. "Out loud. All the way."

"He counts like somebody who was graded," Elias said, and counted.

They came up out of range at the heat-berry shelves in the old cistern-farm, thirty-one children and two babies, blue-lit and breathing, and Elias got them sat against the warm stone in a circle and taught them the only lesson that had ever been free in Ashkarad: you are not your throat. Above them the city rang with the fleet's choir, and not one voice down there in the blue dark joined it.

In the streets, Vahid fought the hymn verse by verse, and lost, and lost, and won.

The second verse came down and the censer crews answered with smoke, the ash-recipe's vast neutral rest rolling through the tenements, and whole streets went toneless in the middle of the hymn like pages torn out of a choir-book. The third verse came down and Lena Vance answered, her freed voice climbing the drainage canals the way it had screamed warnings once, and behind her the canal-singers — the once-harvested, the freed donors whose throats the Church had borrowed and the First Singer's song had given back — rose out of the walls in counter-harmony, under the streets, through every grate and culvert, singing the claiming-hymn wrong on purpose, bar by bar, flat by flat, until the activation verses came apart into their components and could not resolve. It was the Choirmaster's arrangement, though his name appeared nowhere on it: the baritone in the reliquary gallery giving the downbeat along the culvert-lines his corridor had re-tuned all year, the corridor's wrongness propagating outward through the waterways like a scandal. The Church had built the canals to carry hymns inward. It had never considered what one of its own choir-masters could do with the same plumbing in the other direction. The fourth verse came down and Vahid himself stood in the clinic doorway with his one hand on the frame and his spectacles flashing, dosing the nearest sleepwalked singers with the needle-silence, one throat at a time, methodically, the way he had once graded anatomy, refusing the hymn with his whole furious body and never once singing.

The final verse needed the full chord. That was its flaw and its honesty: the claiming-hymn had always been written for a complete roll, every graded voice in the register, and this city's register had been spent — the nursery emptied years ago, the eleven underground, the smallest children out of range in the cistern dark, the streets smoked toneless, the canals singing back. The chord reached for its missing notes and found the rests where they should have been, held a moment at the top of its reach like a hand closing on nothing, and broke.

It broke the way debts stop. No crash. Just an absence where a sound had been, citywide, total.

The herald-chorus withdrew in step, unhurried, ranks folding off the flats the way a clerk folds a completed docket. That was what wrong-footed everyone — no retreat, no rout, no fury. The tally-bearers had never stopped writing. They had walked the whole engagement with their books open, and they withdrew with them full.

"They were never trying to take the children," Roya said. Her voice through the mask was tin and level, and everyone at the shaft head heard the arithmetic under it. "They were appraising them."

The chorus-free crews stood down. The city counted its children, and its throats, and the census came home by districts: every throat in Ashkarad that had woken and sung, entered somewhere — the fleet's tally-books now held the pitch, the grade, and the address of every sleeper the Church had ever trained, a complete audit of who in the city could sing, taken in one morning, paid for in one morning, and Ashkarad had spent that morning saving its children from the harvest without once noticing that the harvest had come and gone already, done, invoiced, complete.
