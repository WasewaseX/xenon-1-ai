# Chapter 24 — The Silence Audit

Three weeks after the Blacktide, the dead of Ashkarad were counted by hand.

For forty years the Choir had kept the count — filed in triplicate, stamped, interest-bearing, complete — so that no one in the city had ever learned how to do it themselves. Now the counting was a neighbor's chore, done in chalk. On the cistern wall below the Saltstairs, where the water-line used to be marked, someone had started a column of names, and the column had grown until it wrapped the wall and turned the corner and became, in the way of Ashkarad arithmetic, a ledger: four hundred and twelve names in three weeks, some written by people who could not write and had borrowed letters the way the poor borrow everything, at a cost. Nobody collected on this ledger. That was what made it new.

Dar's free clinic collected the survivors instead. Thirty-one patients under one roof that had been designed for six; pallets in the corridors, a saline rig rigged from a tithe-barge fitting, and a queue each morning that Suri counted through the door — arrivals, departures, and one column she kept for people who came in intending to pay and had to be talked out of it, which took longer than the stitching.

The clinic had one ledger of its own. It recorded names, wounds, doses, and dates, and nothing else. No debt columns. No interest. No next-of-kin liability. It was, as far as Dar knew, the only book in the city that listed bodies without pricing them, and he kept it out of the same instinct that made a surgeon count sponges: because medicine without records is just butchery with better manners.

Elias moved through the ward with his perfect lungs, carrying basins. The Church had graded those lungs — *perfect, reserved* — and now they spent their perfection hauling wash-water for debtors, which Dar considered the finest audit the boy's registry entry had ever received. In the corner, Ilay sat cross-legged with her charcoal, drawing spirals that began wide and tightened inward, and did not explain them. Suri stood at the door with her ruined scale-arm held against her chest, counting the queue.

The audit arrived at the tenth bell, which Dar noted because it was the hour the Wardens' fathers had always chosen for collections, when the queue was longest and the desperate were most willing to be reasonable.

There were three of them. Two grey coats by the door, and one man in a better coat, grey velvet gone salt-stiff at the shoulders, carrying a machine.

Dar knew pulse-readers the way a hangman knows rope, but he had never seen one like this. It was new — genuinely new, the lacquer still sharp, the brass un-greened — a hand-cranked housing with none of the old Choir fittings, no tissue-ports, no resonance chambers, nothing left of the instruments that had listened for a debtor's signature in his blood. Whatever the Church's machines had been, this one was only a machine. Somebody had built it in the weeks since the silence, and somebody had built it fast.

"Warden-Auditor Seft," the man said, producing a card stamped with the storm-wall seal. "Second Reconciliation. I'm instructed to collect the clinic's patient ledger. For reconciliation."

Dar wiped his hands and did not offer them. "Reconcile it against what?"

"The census. The city is to be re-counted, cutter. Every district, every household, every bed." Seft's voice was mild, the voice of a man reading terms aloud in a language he did not expect to be argued with. "Your establishment is unregistered. Unregistered practice is a sanction. Registration requires a ledger. The ledger is the registration."

"The ledger is the patients," Dar said. "Thirty-one of them. You're not asking for paper. You're asking for the queue."

"I'm asking for names."

Outside the door, Suri had stopped counting. Dar heard the absence of her count the way a surgeon hears a changed rhythm under his fingers.

"Then deliver the instruction to them," he said. "One at a time. Some of them will say yes. People always say yes eventually — your office taught us that better than anyone." He let that stand a moment. "I won't hand you the list."

Seft's eyes went around the ward — the pallets, the basins, the boy with the basins, the little girl drawing spirals in the corner — with the flat appraisal of a man pricing fixtures at a bankruptcy sale. "You were at the Choratorium," he said. "They say you broke it. That makes you popular. Popularity is not a license. Forty years of records burned in three weeks, Mehran. Debt or no debt, a city that can't be counted can't be fed, can't be rationed, can't be governed. The fires didn't free anyone. They just made the counting harder, and the hard counting will be done by men like me, and men like me bill by the difficulty." He set the new pulse-reader on the front desk with the care of a clergyman laying down a relic. "Terms are posted at the hall. Registration, ledger, census marks on the doorframe. You have six days."

"The Truce of Salt says no tithe-taking from the Saltstairs," Suri said from the doorway, flat as a filed report.

"The Truce of Salt bound the syndicates and the Church," Seft said. "The Church is a silence now, and the syndicates are a rumor. Read the terms, courier. Everything is being reconciled."

They left. Dar stood a moment with his hands still damp, watching the door.

He wrote out the audit's own terms on the clinic door that afternoon — verbatim, in his small precise hand, the whole column of them: *registration of practice; surrender of patient ledger; census marks on the doorframe; six days.* He added nothing of his own except a line at the bottom, unsigned: *Posted per the Syndic Wardens' Second Reconciliation. Free of charge. We do not charge for the terms, and we do not pay them.*

The unpriced word traveled faster than any stamped notice had ever moved in the city's history. By evening the doorway had a crowd in front of it four deep, and Mrs. Gable from the next tenement read it aloud twice for the benefit of those who couldn't, and a man who had lost his brother's name in one of the burning halls copied the terms onto his own door across the alley, and then someone else copied the copy, and Dar watched his defiance become a census of its own — a count of doorways that refused — and thought that this, at least, was a ledger the Wardens had not asked to collect.

Suri found him on the stairs at dusk, carrying two cups of the clinic's barley tea, which was the only currency the building honored after dark. "They'll come back with more men."

"They'll come back with better machines," Dar said. "That one counts heartbeats. That's all it does now. It's a pulley where a nerve used to be."

"Everything they rebuild will be a pulley," Suri said. "The Choir's gone. Nobody downstairs knows how to listen like the Choir listened."

"Somebody's building," Dar said. "New brass in three weeks. That's not salvage money. That's funding."

She turned that over the way she turned all figures, silently, twice. "Six days," she said. "I'll start copying the ledger tonight. By hand. If they take the book, there'll be a second book they don't find."

"Copy the wounds," Dar said. "Leave the names out of the copy."

"Then the copy's worth nothing."

"The copy's worth exactly one thing," Dar said. "It proves we treated them. It doesn't prove where they sleep."

Suri looked at him for a moment, and something in the ledger behind her eyes made an entry. "That's the first time anyone's ever asked me to keep half a record," she said. "It feels like counterfeit."

"It feels like mercy," Dar said. "They're adjacent."

The Wardens came back on the fourth day, a day early, with a portable desk and a queue-form, and Seft announced a sample reconciliation — one room, one hour, nothing forcible, purely to calibrate. Dar could have refused the room and lost the hour; instead he leaned on the desk and watched, because a surgeon watches any instrument enter a body he is responsible for.

Seft worked the waiting room down the line. The new machine was beautiful the way new machines are beautiful — silent, effortless, a little smug. Crank, press, print; crank, press, print. Each slip fluttered into the tray with the soft obedience of paper. Most read clean: a rhythm, a count, a number, filed.

At the sixth chair the slip came up wrong.

RHYTHM UNRESOLVED.

Seft frowned, cranked harder, ran the boy in the sixth chair again. The slip came up wrong again, and this time Dar saw the boy's face — Elias, patient as an altar, holding his bandaged forearm still — and understood one beat before the machine's tray confirmed it. At the eighth chair, RHYTHM UNRESOLVED. A girl of six, one of the eleven, asleep against her sister's shoulder. At the eleventh, the same line again, a stock-thin boy with a cough.

Three slips. Three children the nursery had graded, drilled, and hymned until their tissue forgot how to be only theirs.

"Machine fault," Seft said, to the room, to himself. He shook the housing. The crank turned loose, eager, printing nothing.

"The machine's fine," Dar said, taking the three slips from the tray before the Warden's hand arrived. "The machine is the only honest auditor in this room. It read what's there. Those children were built to carry more than one rhythm. Your new brass just found out what the old organs used to know." He folded the slips together. "They're minors. Their rhythms are their property. Sample concluded."

Seft looked at the folded paper, and then at Dar, and for the first time since the tenth bell of the first day his mildness acquired a price. "The anomaly file burned with everything else," he said quietly. "You'd be amazed what three slips are worth at the hall."

"Then the hall can wonder," Dar said, "what they're worth here."

Seft packed his desk. At the door he paused, and made the mistake of a thorough man: he ran one last crank-cycle on his own machine, an empty cycle, housekeeping, the tray cleared.

The machine printed anyway.

One slip, no patient in the clamp. Seft tore it off, read it upside down for half a second, and his face did something that no ledger in Ashkarad had ever recorded him doing. He put the slip in his coat, said nothing, and left — and Suri, watching the door, counted his footsteps down the stairs and said, "He walked faster than he came."

Dar had seen the slip for less than a second. He had needed less. Five words in fresh ink, machine-straight, printed by a device whose every function he had just watched and none of which was that:

REQ: VESSEL REGISTRY — PAGE NINE.

That night he sat in the attic with Vey's registry page unfolded under the lamp — nine cities, eight stamped, one circled red — and laid the three confiscated rhythms beside it, and did not total anything. In his chest the Lily's rhythm turned over once, slow, like a page being marked for later.

Page nine, the machine had asked. Not *entry*. Not *city*. Page.

Somewhere in the burned halls and the rebuilt brass, somebody had started keeping a book that counted the vessels by page — and the book knew it was missing one.
