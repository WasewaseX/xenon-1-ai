# Chapter 16 — The Offering

The Choratorium's doors opened like the petals of a dying flower, swallowing Dar whole. He walked under a truce-flag of white cloth, his empty hands held where the Syndic Wardens could see them, his footsteps echoing in the vaulted entryway. The air smelled of lavender and something else—old blood, perhaps, or the salt that had seeped into the stones over a century. A bone-masked usher led him past walls carved with anatomical diagrams of impossible bodies, figures with multiple hearts and spines that branched like trees. The floor beneath his boots was polished bone, cool and unnaturally smooth.

"You'll dine with Custodian Vey," the usher said, his voice muffled behind the mask. "She's been most eager for your company."

Dar nodded. He felt the Lily in his chest, a quiet hum beneath his ribs. It was restless tonight, feeding on his unease. He'd eaten nothing since morning, knowing the Lily preferred empty vessels when facing the enemy. The corridors were lined with locked doors, behind which he could hear the soft weeping of children. The nursery vessels, he presumed. Vahid was somewhere here too, though whether alive or already harvested remained to be seen.

The usher stopped before a set of double doors carved from what looked like fused ribs. He knocked three times in a pattern that matched a heartbeat too slow to be human. The doors swung inward.

The dining hall was a cathedral to gluttony. A long table stretched before Dar, laden with foods he hadn't seen in years—real meat, not the nutrient paste most Ashkarad citizens subsisted on. Fresh bread, butter that wasn't rendered fat, fruits with intact skins. At the head of the table sat Custodian Anra Vey, her white gloves folded neatly beside her plate, her bone-plate mask replaced by a face of serene middle-aged beauty, her hair the color of salt and ash.

"Darius Mehran," she said, her voice like warm honey poured over cold steel. "You honor us with your presence."

Dar bowed slightly. "Custodian Vey."

"Please," she gestured to the seat opposite hers. "Sit. You must be hungry."

Dar sat. The chair was carved from a single piece of bone, polished to a high sheen. It felt alive beneath him, like it was breathing.

"I am not," he said.

Vey smiled, a careful expression that didn't reach her eyes. "The Lily is particular about such things, I understand. But surely you can indulge in a cup of tea? It's from the eastern slopes, before the blight turned them grey."

A servant in a white apron placed a cup before Dar. The tea was amber in the candlelight, steam curling toward the vaulted ceiling. Dar took a sip. It tasted of mint and something else—metallic, like blood. The Lily in his chest pulsed once, a warning.

"You've been busy," Vey said, gesturing with her fork to a plate of roasted fowl. "Broker peace where others would see only war. A rare talent."

"I'm a surgeon," Dar said. "I mend what's broken."

"Some things," Vey replied, "were never meant to be mended. They were meant to be replaced."

She took a bite of her food, chewed slowly, swallowed. "The Blacktide approaches. Do you know what it is, Darius?"

"The salt moon eclipse," Dar said. "The city will be dark."

"Darkness is not merely the absence of light," Vey said. "It is a state of being. A place where things that cannot exist in the light are free to grow."

She stood, her movements fluid, graceful. "Come. I want to show you something."

They left the dining hall, the scent of food giving way to something older, more organic. The air grew warmer, thicker. Vey led him down a spiral staircase carved from living tissue—veins pulsed faintly in the walls, arteries branched like handrails. The further they descended, the louder the sound became—a low, rhythmic thumping, like a giant heart.

"This way," Vey said, opening a heavy door.

Beyond was the graft-theater. It was vast, circular, with tiers of observation seats carved from bone and flesh. In the center lay the First Vessel.

It was a person, or had been once. Now it was a cathedral of flesh grown too large for its space, its limbs elongated into pillars, its torso swollen into a dome, its head a grotesque mockery of a face with too many eyes, all closed. It was tethered to the floor by thick cables of living tissue that pulsed with a sickly blue light. Forty years of grafts—hearts lashed to its ribs, lungs breathing in tandem, kidneys straining to filter its impossible blood—had turned it into something both beautiful and horrifying.

"Our masterpiece," Vey said, her voice filled with pride. "And our greatest failure. It will not wake. It dreams, but it cannot dream itself whole."

She walked toward the Vessel, her footsteps silent on the organic floor. "Ashkarad is a body that refuses to be whole," she said, turning to face him. "The ledgers are its tumors, the salt storms are its fevers, the debt is its poisoned blood. It is a body at war with itself. And I will be its heart. I will end its pain."

"How?" Dar asked.

"By becoming the door," Vey said. "The First Vessel is locked from the outside. It needs a living Throne to open the door from inside. Someone who has walked the Nine Veins, who understands the body's transcendence. Someone like you, Darius."

She gestured to the side of the theater. There, on a raised platform shaped like an altar, lay Professor Vahid Anousheh, stripped naked, his arms and legs spread wide. Tubes and wires were being grafted into his flesh, connecting him to the First Vessor. His eyes were open, unfocused, his mouth moving in silent prayer.

"His theory made literal," Vey said. "The keystone that will complete the arch. His mind will guide the transformation, his body will provide the spark. Together, they will make the First Vessel whole."

Dar felt the Lily in his chest stir, a cold dread spreading through him. "You're going to kill him."

"We are giving him purpose," Vey said. "His work will not be in vain. His partner's voice sings in our choir; his mind will become the city's song."

She turned back to Dar, her expression softening. "There is another way. You could stand aside. We have your original heart, regrown in a vat. It beats in glass between the candles in the dining hall. It is yours, if you let us have the Lily."

Dar looked at Vahid, at the First Vessel, at the cables pulsing with stolen life. "You're offering me my old life back," he said. "At the cost of everyone else's."

"The cost of progress is always high," Vey said. "But think of the alternative. The Blacktide is coming. The city will be blind, vulnerable. Without a Throne, Ashkarad will tear itself apart from within. With one, it will be made whole. You can be part of that, Darius. You can be the cure."

Dar looked at his hands, the hands that had saved lives and taken them, the hands that now carried a symbiote in his chest. He thought of Suri, waiting in the Saltstairs, of the children in the nursery, of the city that would become a garden if Vey succeeded.

"I can't," he said.

Vey sighed, a sound like wind through dry reeds. "Stubborn child. The Lily will bloom through you eventually. Why not let it be on your terms?"

"Because it's not mine to give," Dar said. "It's a guest. And guests don't get to choose the house for their host."

Vey studied him, her eyes unreadable. "You love this city, don't you? Despite everything."

"I know its pain," Dar said. "And I know the cure isn't to replace what's broken with something else. It's to mend it."

"Mending is such a small word," Vey said. "For such a large problem."

She turned away from him, looking up at the ceiling where the first signs of the Blacktide were beginning to show—the lights in the upper levels flickering, then going dark, one by one. The darkness fell like a wave, swallowing the Choratorium from the top down.

When the last light died, only the bioluminescent veins in the walls remained, casting a sickly blue glow over the graft-theater. The First Vessel pulsed in time with the darkness, its multiple hearts beating a rhythm that made Dar's teeth ache.

Vey turned back to him, her face a pale mask in the gloom. She picked up her teacup from where it sat on a nearby table, the steam rising like a ghost. She held it for a moment, then set it down with a soft click.

"Shall we begin?"
