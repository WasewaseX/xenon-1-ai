# Chapter 7 — Memory Bleed

The tithe-barge groaned through the canal, its hull scraping against the salt-crusted stone as it passed beneath the Choratorium's shadow. Forty debtors stood in harvest-racks, their wrists bound to the rusted iron railing, paperwork nailed to the mast like a death warrant. "Voluntary" it read, in the fine print that always omitted the part about how voluntary stopped when the ledgermen came calling. Suri crouched in the fog, counting heartbeats with her pulse-reader. "Sixteen crew, eight Wardens. Tide's turning—we've got three minutes before the current pins us against the seawall."

Dar adjusted the carbon-fiber blade strapped to his forearm, its weight familiar as debt. The Lily in his chest pulsed in time with the barge's engine, a rhythm that wasn't his own. "They're taking them to the upper sanctums," he said, watching the Choratorium's bone-white spires pierce the salt-fog. "That's not standard tithe procedure."

"Nothing standard about this job," Suri muttered, her carbon-scaled fingers tightening around a length of monofilament. "Cartographer wants the census ledgers from the Warden's cabin. Says they list names not in the public registry."

The first grappling hook sailed over the railing, biting into the deck. Dar moved with the current, his body remembering the weight of the gravel-heart he'd lost, the Lily's strange lightness in its place. A Warden spotted them, his grey coat flapping as he raised his hand-cranked pulse-reader. "Defectors! Audit them!"

Chaos erupted. Dar ducked under a swinging baton, the blade on his arm extending with a whisper. He caught a Warden across the throat, the blade sinking deep, hot blood spattering across his face. The taste of it—metallic, coppery—triggered something deep within him. His blood answered.

It wasn't pain that hit him first, but pressure. A thousand tiny hammers behind his eyes, each heartbeat in earshot amplified into a drum. He saw Suri's pulse racing like a trapped bird, the debtors' terrified rhythms syncopated into a chaotic symphony. His own blood surged, moving beneath his skin like a dark tide, responding to his will even as his vision swam.

He fought blind, the blade in his hand an extension of his panic. A Warden charged, and Dar's blood lashed out, a tendril of crimson that wrapped around the man's throat, lifting him off his feet. The sound of the man's strangled gasp was drowned out by the drumbeat in Dar's skull. He saw Suri's carbon scales flash as she disarmed another crewman, her movements economical, precise. She was counting something—steps, blows, debts to be paid.

The deck slick with blood, the Lily in his chest pounding like a second heart. He felt it then, the Vein opening, not as pain but as expansion. His blood was no longer just his—it was a force, a weather system he could command. He directed it, a dark storm that swept across the deck, knocking men off their feet, tangling limbs in crimson strands. The debtors in their racks screamed, a chorus of relief and terror.

And then the memories came.

Not his own. A woman in white gloves tending a salt-garden. Rows of sleeping people planted in the earth like seeds, their heads barely visible above the crystalline soil. She hummed a lullaby in a dead language, her movements tender, pruning the sleepers with the same care a gardener might give to roses. Her face was serene, certain, but loneliness clung to her like the salt on the garden's surface. She knelt beside one sleeper, her gloved hand resting on the person's forehead, whispering words that weren't meant for waking.

Dar gasped, stumbling backward, the blood-storm receding as his vision swam. The drumbeat in his ears softened, replaced by the woman's humming. He was standing on the deck of the barge, Suri shouting his name, the last Warden falling to his knees. Victory. But the memory lingered, salt on his tongue, the lullaby echoing in his bones.

"Wake up, Mehran!" Suri's voice cut through the fog. She grabbed his arm, her carbon scales cold against his skin. "We've got the ledgers. Let's move before the backup arrives."

The debtors were free, stumbling off the barge into the canal's shallows, their chains discarded. Dar followed, his legs unsteady, the woman's face superimposed over the salt-fogged sky. He didn't know how long he'd stood there, lost in her garden. Seconds? Minutes? The time had bled away, borrowed from someone else.

They met the Cartographer in the Undergleam, the maps exchanged for the dream-mapping. The brass-masked figure listened as Dar described the salt-garden, the sleeping rows, the humming woman. "Custodian Anra Vey," the Cartographer said, the name like a stone dropped in still water. "Her private sanctum. Beneath the Choratorium. Registered to her alone."

Dar stared at the map the Cartographer handed him, the salt-garden marked in precise, careful strokes. The woman in the memory wasn't just any gardener. She was the Church's leader. The Lily inside him hadn't just replaced his heart—it had been inside Vey's world before.

Suri cleaned her blade in the canal's brackish water while Dar sat on the damp stone, the Lily in his chest quiet but not still. "You saw something back there," she said, not asking. "Something that wasn't the fight."

"Memories," Dar said, rubbing his temples. "Not mine. The woman in the garden—she was humming a lullaby. I've never heard it before, but I know every note."

"The Lily bleeds memories," Suri said, counting the steps back to their bolt-hole. "Professor Vahid said it. At the higher Veins, you carry more than just blood."

The Cartographer's map lay on Dar's lap, the salt-garden marked with a single, perfect circle. "It's Vey's private sanctum," he said, tracing the lines with his finger. "The sleeping rows—she's been growing something down there. Something that needs to be tended."

Suri's pulse-reader beeped softly. "The Blacktide is three days off. The eclipse. That's when she plans to ascend. To the Ninth Vein."

Dar looked at the map, then at his own hands. The blood that had answered his will was still beneath his skin, a dark weather he couldn't control. "The Lily wants to bloom," he said, the words tasting like salt. "But the memories it gave me—they weren't random. They were a warning."

The Lily pulsed once, a rhythm that felt like agreement. The woman's lullaby echoed in Dar's mind, a song of certainty and loneliness. He had seen inside the Church's leader, and what he'd seen was a garden of sleeping people, tended by a hand that believed it was doing God's work.

The Blacktide was coming. And the Lily in his chest had been to the garden before.
