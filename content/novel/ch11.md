# Chapter 11 — The Girl Who Heard the Singing

The bell tolled again, deeper this time, a vibration that traveled through the canal-works' iron supports and into Dar's bones. Suri's hand tightened around his wrist, her carbon scales pressing into his skin like cool coins. "They know," she whispered, her breath fogging in the damp air. "They know we have the registry."

Dar didn't answer. His focus was elsewhere—on the small bundle of rags they'd pulled from the pump vanes twenty minutes ago. A child, no older than nine, with eyes the color of river stones and hair the texture of spun copper. She hadn't spoken since they'd found her, hadn't even flinched when Suri had lifted her from the canal's oily embrace. Now she sat huddled in the corner of their hideout, a converted cistern beneath the Saltstairs, tracing patterns in the condensation on the wall with a grimy fingernail.

"Her name's Ilay," Dar said, finally looking at Suri. "She was in the registry. Perfect vessel. Reserved for the Throne."

Suri's gaze shifted to the child. "The Chorus lost their prize. That's more dangerous than us having a page." She knelt, keeping a careful distance. "How long have you been hiding here, little thistle?"

Ilay looked up, not at Suri, but at the ceiling where a single naked bulb flickered weakly. "Since the singing stopped," she said, her voice thin as ice. "In the walls. They called me the garden's daughter."

Dar felt a cold dread seep into his chest, a familiar ache that wasn't his gravel-heart but the Lily responding to something. The garden's daughter. That's what the choir organs had called him in his dreams—before he'd known about Ilay. Before he'd known about siblings.

· · ·

The hideout smelled of damp stone, old oil, and something vaguely sweet that Suri called "heat-berries"—small, bioluminescent fungi that grew in the city's warmer pipes. Dar had rigged a small burner to boil water, and now he watched the steam curl toward the ceiling, feeling detached from his own body. The Breath Vein was still active, a constant hum beneath his skin, but he'd learned to control it, to separate himself from the need for air. It was like being underwater without the water, a quiet space inside himself where he could think.

Suri was counting again, her fingers tapping against her thigh. "Three days before the salt-moon eclipse. Fourteen days since the Lily took root. Sixty-seven heartbeats since the bell tolled first." She looked at Dar. "The Chorus won't wait. They'll tear the Saltstairs apart brick by brick to find her."

Ilay sat on a crate, watching them with unnerving stillness. "They'll find me anyway," she said. "The singing knows where I am."

Dar knelt beside her, keeping his movements slow, non-threatening. "What singing, Ilay?"

"The singing in the walls," she repeated. "Before. When they took me away from the quiet room. It said I was the garden's daughter. That I had to go back to the garden."

"The garden," Dar murmured, thinking of the Umbra Lily, of its roots and petals and the black lace that had bloomed across his skin. "You mean the Choratorium?"

Ilay shook her head, her copper hair catching the dim light. "Deeper. Where the singing is loudest. Where the First Singer sleeps."

Dar's blood ran cold. The First Singer. Anra Vey's buried host. The thing beneath the Marrowworks that was being fed forty years of organs. He'd read about it in Vahid's notes, seen the architectural schematics of the buried shaft that led down to it. But Ilay had never seen those schematics. She'd drawn it herself.

· · ·

Two days later, Vahid arrived smelling of gin and old paper, his coat stained with what looked like ink and something darker. He'd been drinking, as usual, but his eyes sharpened when he saw Ilay.

"She's the one," he said, his voice rough. "The registry said 'perfect vessel.' I didn't expect..."

Ilay held up a piece of folded paper covered in crayon drawings. Vahid took it, unfolding it carefully. His breath hitched.

The drawing showed a cross-section of the city, but not as any map Dar had ever seen. It showed layers—streets, pipes, foundations—and at the bottom, a vast, bulbous shape surrounded by roots that reached up through the earth like grasping fingers. Beside it, Ilay had drawn a series of concentric circles, each labeled with a number.

"The singing told me," Ilay said quietly. "The numbers are how many steps down. The circles are how loud it gets."

Vahid stared at the drawing, then at Ilay, then back at the drawing. "She's mapped the shaft," he said to Dar, awe in his voice. "By ear. The girl who hears the singing has mapped the path to the First Singer."

Dar looked at Ilay, at the calm certainty in her eyes. This changes everything, he thought. Not just their escape, but everything. The Chorus wasn't just after him anymore. They were after her. And if they got her back...

· · ·

That evening, Dar sat with Ilay in the corner of the hideout, sharing a piece of bread dipped in heat-berry jam. Suri was on watch, her silhouette sharp against the doorway. Dar had his shirt unbuttoned, the Lily a faint pattern beneath his skin—black veins like roots spreading across his chest.

"Why do they call you the garden's daughter?" Dar asked softly.

Ilay took a small bite of bread. "Because I don't mind the dark. Because I like the singing. Because when they put me in the quiet room, I could still hear it through the walls. The garden wants to sing, but it's too deep. Too big."

Dar thought of the Lily in his chest, of its patience, its curiosity. It wasn't evil. It just wanted to bloom. And Ilay was part of that. A sibling vessel. A perfect one.

He looked at his hands—precise hands that had saved lives and now carried this thing inside him. Survival, then protection, then refusal. He'd been surviving since the Lily took root. He'd started protecting Suri. Now, with Ilay, it was different. This was hope. Fragile, dangerous hope, but hope nonetheless.

· · ·

The knock came at dawn, sharp and deliberate. Three raps, then a pause, then three more. Suri was on her feet in an instant, carbon scales gleaming in the dim light. "Chorus," she spat, reaching for the knife at her belt. "Too soon. Too clean."

Dar put a hand on her arm. "Wait."

The knock came again—two raps, this time. Ilay stirred where she slept, her eyes fluttering open. "It's her," she whispered, sitting up. "The one who hums."

Dar exchanged a look with Suri. The one who hums. Anra Vey.

The door creaked open, not kicked or forced. Standing in the doorway was Custodian Anra Vey, dressed in simple grey robes, her face unmasked, her hair pulled back in a neat silver bun. She held a lantern in one hand and a wicker basket in the other. No soldiers. No bone-plate champions. Just her, and a calm that seemed to absorb the room's tension.

"Good morning," she said, her voice warm as tea. "I do hope I'm not intruding. The bell must have woken you." She stepped inside, her movements unhurried, and set the basket on the floor. From it, she produced a thermos and three ceramic cups. "I brought tea. Chamomile. Soothing for the nerves."

Suri's hand was still on her knife, but she didn't move. Dar stood between her and Anra Vey, his body a shield. "What do you want, Custodian."

Anra Vey poured the tea, the steam curling around her like a living thing. "To talk," she said, offering Dar a cup. "And to offer a solution. A way out of this for all of you."

Dar didn't take the cup. "There's no way out. Not with her." He nodded at Ilay.

Anra Vey's gaze shifted to the child, then back to Dar. "The girl is safe with me. Safer than she is here, running from the very thing that will complete her." She took a sip of tea, her eyes never leaving his. "I can unmake the Lily, Dar Mehran. I can remove it from your chest and give you back your mortal heart. The gravel-heart that was failing you before. You'll be free of it. Free of the debt. Free of the bloom."

Dar felt the Lily stir in his chest, a pulse of fear. It knew. It always knew.

"All you have to do," Anra Vey continued, her voice soft as the steam rising from her cup, "is give me the perfect bloom. The girl. Ilay. She is the key. The garden's daughter. With her, the Ninth Vein is possible. Without her... well. We both know what happens then."

She set her cup down and looked at Dar, her expression one of gentle understanding. "The tea is poured, Dar. It's still warm. You just have to decide which cup to drink from."
