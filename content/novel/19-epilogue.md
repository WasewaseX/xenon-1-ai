# Epilogue — A Door in the Dark

The silence in Ashkarad is louder than the song ever was. For weeks, the city held its breath, listening for the return of the choir organs, the hum of preserved voices that had threaded through the walls for forty years. Nothing came. Only the ordinary noise: hammers on the Saltstairs tenements, arguments over ration portions, the cry of gulls wheeling above the seawalls. The silence is a space now, filled with the messy, unharmonized sounds of people who forgot how to breathe without being told.

Dar's fingers moved with the precision of a man who had cut too many flesh scars to count. The clinic's operating theater smelled of antiseptic, old blood, and salt-damp stone. A boy with a crushed hand lay on the table, his breath coming in shallow gasps. Dar's incision was clean, a silver line against the swollen tissue. He didn't need to look at his hands to know where they were; they were part of him, extensions of the will that now flowed through his veins. The Flesh Vein had knitted his wounds shut in minutes. The Marrow Vein had turned his bones dense as coral, letting him withstand blows that would have shattered a lesser man. The Blood Vein made the blood in this boy's veins answer his touch, slowing the flow as he worked. The Breath Veil meant the clinic's stale air was enough, even in the dim light filtering through the high, barred window. He was climbing the Nine Veins, one painful step at a time, and the city was climbing with him.

"Pressure's stabilizing," Vahid announced, his voice a gravelly rumble. He sat in the corner, his one remaining hand holding a retractor while his stump tapped a restless rhythm against his thigh. "The grafts took. Good work, Dar." His eyes, magnified behind thick spectacles, never left the operating field. Since losing his arm to a Chorus enforcer's bone blade, he'd thrown himself into teaching, his lectures growing more fervent with each passing day. "You see? The body is a ledger, and every entry has a cost. But sometimes, the balance comes due in ways you never expect."

Suri leaned against the doorframe, her right arm sheathed in its carbon-scale graft. The splint on her left forearm was new, a crude bandage of boiled linen and twine. She counted everything—the boy's breaths, the drops of antiseptic, the cracks in the ceiling. "Three hundred and twelve," she said, not taking her eyes off Dar. "That's how many times you've used the word 'good' today, Professor. You're running a deficit of variety."

Vahid grunted. "Semantics. The point stands. This boy will walk. That's a good."

In the corner, Roya sat with Ilay, the little girl drawing on a scrap of parchment with a piece of charcoal. Roya wore her brass mask always now, a shield against a world that had tried to harvest her song. Ilay was quiet, her eyes distant, as if listening to a music only she could still hear. She drew not streets or buildings, but swirls of what she called "where the singing used to be." Roya's hand, resting on the girl's shoulder, bore the permanent scar of a Chorus blade, a white line against her brown skin. "It's different now," Roya said, her voice soft. "The silence is loud."

The ledgers were burning. Not quickly, but burning. The Syndic Wardens still patrolled, but without the choir organs to track debtors by their unique biological signatures, their pulse-readers were just machines counting heartbeats. People were defaulting, disappearing into the city's underbelly, and the Wardens couldn't find them as easily. The system was fraying, and in the fraying, there was space to breathe. Space to be.

Dar finished the last stitch, his needle deft. He wiped the boy's brow with a clean cloth. "There. Rest now. The bone will set straight." He stepped back from the table, his own chest a familiar landscape of sensation. The Umbra Lily beat within him, a constant, living rhythm. It was no longer just a thing he carried; it was becoming a part of him, and he a part of it. He called it "her" now, though he never said the word aloud. It felt more respectful.

He walked to his small room in the clinic's attic, a space that smelled of dust and old paper. On his desk, folded inside a sterile wrap, was a single page of vellum. Vey's registry page. It listed eight cities, all marked with the same symbol: a stylized flower blooming from a crack in the earth. One city was circled in red ink: Ashkarad. Below the list, a single word: RECLAIM. And a title: GARDENER.

He didn't know what it meant. The Lily had shown him things in dreams—cities of black lace, gardens that sang, a door in the dark. He didn't know if they were her memories, or his own, or something else entirely. He didn't know which memories were his anymore. His mother's face was a blank space in his mind, a hole he couldn't fill. But the lullaby remained. The same one Vey had hummed. He no longer knew which of them it belonged to first, and had decided not to mind. Some things were meant to be shared.

The afternoon bled into evening. The sky turned the color of a deep bruise. From the clinic's roof, Dar could see the whole of the Saltstairs spread out below him, a tangled mess of stacked tenements and sagging catwalks. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys, thin grey threads against the darkening sky. The city was healing, in its own messy, painful way.

The first salt storm since the Blacktide rolled in on a wind that tasted of iron and salt. The "grace" they called it, though there was nothing graceful about it. The wind howled, and the sky turned white. The streets vanished. From the roof, Dar watched the fog swallow the city whole.

He stood on the edge of the roof, letting the storm hit him. The salt stung his eyes, the wind whipped at his coat, but he didn't flinch. The Lily within him drank the dark, drank the storm. It was feeding time. He closed his eyes, let the sensory input of the city wash over him—the creak of the building, the distant scream of a gull, the thrum of a thousand heartbeats in the tenements below. He was at Vein Seven now, the Mind Vein. Memory was architecture, and he was learning to navigate the ruins.

He felt the Lily stir, a soft pulse against his ribs. It was content here in the storm, in the dark, in the silence. It was blooming, slowly, and the bloom was not the nightmare he had feared. It was something else. Something patient. Something ancient.

And then he heard it.

A second heartbeat.

Not his own. Not the Lily's. It was patient and immense, a slow, deep drum in the earth beneath him. Or beyond him. He couldn't tell. It was like hearing the ocean from the bottom of the sea. It was there, and it was real. It wasn't the dead First Vessel. It wasn't Vey, dissolved into petals. It was something else. Something that had been waiting to be noticed.

The Lily stirred again, soft as a page turning. And for the first time since it had rooted in his chest, it spoke. The voice was not in his ears, but in his mind, a whisper that was also a song. A voice he half-knew, from a dream he couldn't quite remember.

"Sister."
